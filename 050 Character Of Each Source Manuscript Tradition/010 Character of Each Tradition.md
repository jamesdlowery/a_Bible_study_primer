# Manuscript Traditions: Character, Relationships, and Weighing

## Old Testament / Hebrew Bible and related witnesses

**Masoretic Text (MT)**
The standardized medieval Hebrew text of the Jewish Bible, represented chiefly by the Leningrad Codex (1008/1009 AD) and the Aleppo Codex (c. 930 AD). The Masoretes (primarily Tiberian, 7th–10th centuries) added vowel points, accentuation, and marginal notes (Masorah) to a consonantal text that had already been carefully transmitted. It is marked by remarkable internal consistency and a conservative scribal ethos.
**Relationships**: The dominant form among the later medieval Hebrew manuscripts. Dead Sea Scrolls (DSS) show that a proto-MT form already existed in the Second Temple period and was one of several circulating textual traditions. The Septuagint (LXX), Samaritan Pentateuch (SP), and some DSS often diverge from it, sometimes preserving older or alternative readings. Most modern Protestant and Jewish translations take the MT as their base text, corrected only where strong evidence from other witnesses exists.

**Septuagint (LXX)**
The ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, begun in Alexandria in the 3rd century BC (Pentateuch) and completed over the following centuries. It is not a single uniform translation; quality and style vary by book. It frequently reflects a Hebrew *Vorlage* (source text) different from the later MT—sometimes shorter, sometimes longer, sometimes simply divergent.
**Relationships**: Frequently agrees with certain DSS against the MT, supporting the existence of non-MT Hebrew texts in antiquity. It was the Bible of Greek-speaking Jews and of the early Church; the New Testament writers quote it extensively. Later Christian revisions (e.g., Hexaplaric, Lucianic) and the Latin, Syriac, and Coptic versions often depend on it or interact with it. In books such as Jeremiah, Job, and Samuel–Kings the differences from MT are substantial.

**Dead Sea Scrolls (DSS / Qumran biblical manuscripts)**
Hebrew (and some Aramaic and Greek) manuscripts dated roughly 250 BC–70 AD. They include complete or fragmentary copies of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther (plus many non-canonical works). They display textual plurality: proto-MT, proto-LXX, proto-Samaritan, and independent forms appear side-by-side.
**Relationships**: They demonstrate that the MT is not the sole ancient Hebrew tradition. Where a DSS manuscript aligns with the LXX or SP against the MT, it often supplies independent confirmation that the non-MT reading is ancient. They are primary evidence for the fluidity of the text before the standardization that produced the proto-MT.

**Samaritan Pentateuch (SP)**
The Samaritan community’s Hebrew text of the Torah only. It contains ideologically motivated changes (most famously the elevation of Mount Gerizim), plus numerous harmonizations, expansions, and linguistic modernizations.
**Relationships**: Shares a significant number of readings with the LXX and with certain DSS (the so-called pre-Samaritan texts) against the MT. It is therefore valuable as a witness to an ancient Palestinian textual form of the Pentateuch, even though its sectarian alterations must be discounted.

**Aramaic Targums**
Jewish Aramaic translations/paraphrases that began as oral renderings in the synagogue and were later committed to writing (Onqelos, Jonathan, Pseudo-Jonathan, Neofiti, Fragment Targums, etc.). They range from relatively literal (Onqelos) to highly expansive and midrashic.
**Relationships**: Secondary witnesses. They usually presuppose a text close to the MT but occasionally preserve or reflect non-MT readings. Their chief value is interpretive and linguistic rather than strictly textual; they show how the Hebrew was understood in late antiquity.

**Syriac Peshitta (OT)**
The standard Syriac translation of the Old Testament, produced for the Syriac-speaking churches (roughly 1st–3rd centuries AD for most books). The Pentateuch and some other books are translated from a Hebrew text close to the MT; other books show greater LXX influence.
**Relationships**: Generally supports the MT but can occasionally agree with the LXX or DSS. As an early version it is useful for confirming the antiquity of particular readings, though its translation technique must be taken into account.

**Latin Vulgate**
Jerome’s late-4th-century Latin translation. For the Old Testament he worked primarily from the Hebrew (*Hebraica veritas*), though he also knew the Greek tradition and earlier Latin (Vetus Latina) renderings.
**Relationships**: Largely a witness to a Hebrew text close to the MT, but it retains some LXX-influenced readings and earlier Latin traditions. It became the authoritative Bible of the Western Church and therefore shaped medieval and early modern Latin textual discussion.

## Apocrypha / Deuterocanon and its witnesses

The seven deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch with the Letter of Jeremiah, and 1 and 2 Maccabees), together with the additions to Esther and Daniel, have a manuscript history unlike either testament's: for most of them the Greek text is the earliest complete witness, the Hebrew or Aramaic original (where one existed) survives only in fragments or not at all, and the Latin tradition preserves forms of the text that differ from the Greek by whole sentences and paragraphs. The four tracked translations that include these books (RSV2CE, NABRE, Douay-Rheims, CPDV) split along exactly that line -- two from the Greek, two from the Latin -- which is why the Apocrypha chapters of Manuscript and Translation Differences read differently from the rest.

**Septuagint (LXX) manuscripts**
The great uncial codices -- Vaticanus (4th century), Sinaiticus (4th century), and Alexandrinus (5th century) -- are the principal witnesses to the Greek text of every deuterocanonical book, and for Wisdom, 2 Maccabees, and (largely) 1 Maccabees the Greek is the original language, not a translation. Tobit survives in two substantially different Greek recensions: the shorter (represented by Vaticanus and Alexandrinus) and the longer (represented by Sinaiticus), differing by about a fifth in length. Sirach's Greek is a translation made by the author's grandson around 132 BC, and its Greek manuscripts carry a well-known displacement of two whole sections (chapters 30-36) that modern editions must correct. The critical edition is the Göttingen Septuagint; Rahlfs-Hanhart is the standard hand edition.
**Relationships**: For these books the LXX is not a witness *to* an original but, in most cases, the closest thing to the original that survives. Modern translations made "from the Greek" (RSV2CE, NABRE, and the NRSV family) therefore rest on the uncials and on the recension a given editor prefers -- the RSV followed the shorter Tobit; the NRSV and NABRE follow the longer.

**Hebrew and Aramaic fragments (Qumran, Masada, the Cairo Genizah)**
Sirach was composed in Hebrew, and roughly two-thirds of its Hebrew text is now known: six medieval manuscripts from the Cairo Genizah (identified from 1896 onward), a first-century scroll from Masada, and small Qumran fragments. Tobit was composed in Aramaic or Hebrew; Qumran Cave 4 yielded four Aramaic and one Hebrew manuscript (4Q196-200), which support the longer Greek recension. Fragments of the Letter of Jeremiah in Greek (7Q2) and of Psalm 151 also come from Qumran. No Hebrew or Aramaic text of Judith, Baruch, or 1 Maccabees survives, though all three are generally agreed to have been composed in a Semitic language.
**Relationships**: Where they exist, these are the earliest witnesses to the wording, and they have changed editorial judgments: the Qumran Tobit fragments are the main reason recent translations prefer Sinaiticus's longer text, and the Genizah and Masada Sirach let editors correct the Greek in many places. They are, however, fragmentary, and no complete deuterocanonical book survives in its original Semitic form.

**Old Latin (Vetus Latina) and the Latin Vulgate**
Jerome regarded the deuterocanonical books as outside the Hebrew canon and translated only Tobit and Judith himself (from Aramaic texts, hastily, by his own account); for the rest, the Vulgate simply took over the earlier Old Latin translations, which had been made from the Greek in the 2nd and 3rd centuries. The Old Latin of these books is therefore a witness to a Greek text older than the surviving uncials, and in Sirach it preserves a longer form of the text (with many additional verses) that appears in the Douay-Rheims and the CPDV but in no translation made from the Greek. The Vulgate Tobit and Judith are Jerome's own, and differ from the Greek in wording, order, and length throughout.
**Relationships**: The Latin tradition is the reason the two Catholic translations made from the Vulgate diverge so far from RSV2CE and NABRE in these books -- not a matter of translation philosophy but of a genuinely different underlying text. The Stuttgart Vulgate and the Vetus Latina Institute's edition are the critical texts.

**Syriac Peshitta and other versions**
The Peshitta includes the deuterocanonical books (Sirach translated directly from Hebrew, the rest from Greek), and there are Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, and Arabic versions of most of them. These are secondary witnesses, chiefly useful for confirming the antiquity of a Greek reading or, for Sirach, for reconstructing the Hebrew.
**Relationships**: Rarely decisive on their own, but the Peshitta Sirach is one of the four main sources (with the Genizah, Masada, and Qumran Hebrew) that editors triangulate to recover the original Hebrew.

**How to weigh them**
The principles below apply, with one adjustment: for a book whose Greek is a translation of a lost Semitic original, the "original reading" an editor is reconstructing may lie behind the Greek rather than in it, and a Latin or Syriac reading that reflects a better Greek exemplar can outweigh the uncials. For a reader comparing the four tracked translations, the practical rule is simpler: a difference between the Greek-based pair and the Latin-based pair in these books is almost always a difference of source text, and the Manuscript and Translation Differences chapters for Tobit, Judith, and Sirach document the largest of them.

## New Testament text-types and editions

**Alexandrian text-type**
Represented by early papyri (e.g., 𝔓⁶⁶, 𝔓⁷⁵) and the great uncials Codex Vaticanus (B) and Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ), mainly from Egypt. Characterized by a generally shorter, less polished, and often “harder” text.
**Relationships**: Frequently judged the earliest recoverable form in many passages. It stands in contrast to the fuller Byzantine text; modern critical editions lean heavily on it.

**Byzantine text-type (Majority Text)**
The form found in the large majority of surviving Greek minuscule manuscripts (mostly 9th century onward) and in the later uncials. It tends to be smoother, more complete, and conflated (combining earlier readings). It was the ecclesiastical text of the Byzantine Empire.
**Relationships**: The dominant text in the Greek-speaking East for a millennium. The Textus Receptus is essentially a late printed form of it. Majority-Text advocates argue that its numerical preponderance and continuous ecclesiastical use favor it; most critical scholars regard it as a secondary, developed text.

**Textus Receptus (TR / Received Text)**
The series of printed Greek New Testaments of the 16th–17th centuries (Erasmus, Stephanus, Beza, Elzevir). Based on a small number of late Byzantine manuscripts available to Erasmus, with occasional conjectural emendations and influence from the Latin Vulgate.
**Relationships**: A particular late form of the Byzantine text. It underlies the King James Version and other Reformation-era translations. It differs from both the broader Majority Text and the modern critical text in a number of places (e.g., the Comma Johanneum, certain longer endings).

**Critical / Eclectic Text**
The text constructed by modern editors (Nestle-Aland, UBS, Editio Critica Maior) who weigh evidence from all manuscript types, early versions, and patristic citations rather than following any single text-type. The result is a reconstructed text that often (but not always) follows Alexandrian witnesses.
**Relationships**: Intentionally independent of any one tradition. It is the base text of nearly all contemporary scholarly translations.

## Principles of textual criticism that govern how scholars weigh them

Scholars apply a combination of **external** and **internal** criteria; no single rule is absolute.

External evidence

- Age and quality of the witness (earlier manuscripts generally preferred, though a late manuscript can preserve an early reading).
- Geographical distribution (a reading found in widely separated areas is less likely to be a local corruption).
- Textual character / genealogical relationship (membership in a known text-type or family; the goal is to get behind secondary developments).
- For the OT: the MT is given great weight because of its careful transmission, but DSS and LXX readings are preferred when they are demonstrably older or when the MT appears secondary (e.g., harmonizing or theologically smoothed).
- For the NT: early Alexandrian witnesses and papyri carry heavy weight in the majority critical method; Byzantine manuscripts are not ignored but are usually judged later developments.

Internal evidence

- Transcriptional probability: What is a scribe more likely to have done? (Scribes tend to smooth difficulties, add clarifying words, harmonize parallel passages, and prefer longer readings—*lectio brevior potior* and *lectio difficilior potior* are common guidelines, applied cautiously.)
- Intrinsic probability: What is the author more likely to have written, given vocabulary, style, theology, and context?
- For versions (LXX, Peshitta, Vulgate, Targums): the reading is evaluated only after accounting for translation technique; a version can confirm a Hebrew or Greek reading but rarely establishes it alone.

Additional OT-specific considerations

- Textual plurality was real in the Second Temple period. The goal is often to recover the earliest attainable form(s) of a given book rather than a single “original” in every case.
- Secondary witnesses (versions) are used to corroborate, not to override, Hebrew evidence unless the Hebrew witnesses themselves are divided or clearly secondary.

Additional NT-specific considerations

- Reasoned eclecticism (the dominant modern approach) evaluates each variant unit on its own merits rather than automatically following one text-type.
- The *Editio Critica Maior* and related projects aim at a more rigorous local-genealogical method that reconstructs the initial text and the history of transmission.
- Minority approaches (e.g., Majority-Text or Byzantine-priority theories) invert the usual preference and argue that the reading found in the largest number of manuscripts, especially when supported by continuous ecclesiastical use, should be preferred unless clear evidence of corruption exists.

In short: the MT and the Alexandrian witnesses are often starting points because of their relative antiquity and quality, but every significant difference is weighed by the combined external and internal criteria above. The DSS in particular revolutionized OT textual criticism by proving that the traditional MT, while excellent, is only one ancient stream among several.

### A worked example: weighing the evidence for Mark's ending (Mark 16:9-20)

The principles above are abstract until applied to an actual case. Mark 16:9-20 -- the "Longer Ending," addressed at length in Manuscript and Translation Differences -- is a useful example because both external and internal evidence come into play, and they point in the same direction.

**External evidence against the Longer Ending's originality:**
- Age and quality of witness: the two oldest complete Greek manuscripts of Mark, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus (both 4th century), end at 16:8.
- Geographical and patristic distribution: early church historians Eusebius and Jerome (4th-5th century) both stated that the Longer Ending was absent from "almost all" the Greek manuscripts known to them -- independent testimony spanning different regions, not a single localized gap.
- A specific manuscript feature: Codex Vaticanus leaves a blank column immediately after 16:8, unusual for that manuscript, which some read as evidence its scribe knew of a longer ending but lacked it in his own source.

**External evidence for the Longer Ending's originality:**
- Sheer manuscript count: the Longer Ending appears in the vast majority (an estimated 99%) of the roughly 1,700 surviving Greek manuscripts of Mark.
- An early citation: the church father Irenaeus quotes Mark 16:19 by name around 180 AD, earlier than either Vaticanus or Sinaiticus were copied.

**Internal evidence:** the Longer Ending's Greek shows a noticeable shift in vocabulary and style from the rest of Mark -- the kind of stylistic discontinuity transcriptional and intrinsic probability both treat as a signal of a different hand, though stylistic arguments are inherently softer than manuscript evidence.

**How these weigh against each other:** applying the principles above -- age and quality of witness favor omission (the earliest, most carefully copied manuscripts lack it); geographical/patristic distribution favors omission (the gap is attested across regions and centuries, not local); sheer manuscript count favors inclusion (the numerical majority of later copies have it) but carries less weight under reasoned eclecticism, which does not simply count manuscripts; and internal stylistic evidence leans toward omission. This is why the majority of contemporary textual critics conclude the Longer Ending was not part of Mark's original composition, while a substantial minority of scholars and textual critics continue to defend its authenticity on the strength of its early attestation (Irenaeus) and its overwhelming numerical support in the later manuscript tradition. Manuscript and Translation Differences presents this specific case in full, including how each of the 27 tracked translations flags it.
