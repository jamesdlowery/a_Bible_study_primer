# What Is Meant by an "Inerrant" Word of God?

If the previous entry establishes that Scripture is understood as God's own communication, this one addresses a further, more specific claim built on that foundation: that this communication, in its original form, contains no errors. "Inerrant" is a more precise, more technical, and more contested term than "the Word of God" — worth defining carefully before the rest of this book puts it to use.

## A Claim About the Originals, Not Every Copy

The most influential modern formulation of this doctrine, the 1978 Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, states plainly that inerrancy applies to the *original autographs* — the initial documents as actually written by the biblical authors — not to every subsequent copy or translation made since. None of those original autographs survive; what survives is thousands of manuscript copies, made and recopied over centuries, which sometimes disagree with one another in ways this book documents at length.

This distinction is exactly what allows this book to exist without contradicting the doctrine it's built on. A claim that the originals contained no errors says nothing about whether a given copy or translation perfectly preserves those originals — that's a separate, empirical question, addressed by the discipline of textual criticism, which is what the *Manuscript and Translation Differences* section of this book is actually about. The scholars behind the translations and manuscript work this book surveys are, in the great majority, working from exactly this premise: reconstructing, as closely as the evidence allows, what the now-lost originals most likely said.

## What Inerrancy Does Not Claim

Serious defenders of inerrancy, including the Chicago Statement itself, are explicit that the doctrine does not require:

- **Modern standards of scientific or technical precision.** Scripture describing the sun as "ris[ing]... and hurr[ying] back to where it rises" (Ecclesiastes 1:5) is ordinary observational language, not a claim about astronomy.
- **Exhaustive or verbatim detail.** Ancient writers routinely rounded numbers, paraphrased rather than quoted verbatim (an accepted convention of ancient citation, addressed throughout this book's own variant entries), and were not held to modern rules of grammar or genre.
- **Strict chronological ordering.** Ancient biographical and historical writing often organized material topically rather than sequentially — a convention, not an error, when the text itself gives no indication it intends strict chronology.
- **Endorsement of everything reported.** A text can accurately record that a false statement was made (by a liar, a deceived character, or an adversary) without affirming that statement as true. Inerrancy concerns what Scripture itself teaches or asserts, not everything it merely describes.

## A Term With a History, and Real, Ongoing Disagreement

Something like this doctrine has old roots — Augustine wrote in the fourth century that he held the Scriptures alone to be free of error, and that any apparent difficulty was due to a faulty manuscript, a poor translation, or his own misunderstanding, not an actual error in what the author wrote. But "inerrancy" as a specific technical term, and the Chicago Statement's detailed formulation of it, are largely a twentieth-century development, arising from debates within American evangelicalism (sometimes called the "battle for the Bible") about how precisely the doctrine should be stated.

Christian traditions do not use this vocabulary uniformly:

- Catholic and Orthodox statements more often affirm that Scripture is without error specifically **in what it teaches for the sake of our salvation**, a formulation some call "limited inerrancy," without necessarily extending the same confidence to every incidental historical or scientific detail.
- Many mainline and moderate Protestant traditions prefer **infallibility** — a claim that Scripture reliably accomplishes what God intends it to accomplish (bringing people to faith, teaching truth about God) — over "inerrancy," which they may regard as claiming more precision than the text itself claims for its own incidental details.
- Even within evangelicalism, where "inerrancy" in the fullest sense is most widely affirmed, real debate continues over exactly how far it extends and how apparent difficulties should be handled.

This book does not adjudicate between these positions.

## Why This Matters for the Rest of This Book

The harmonizations offered throughout this book's *Reportedly Contradicting Passages* section generally proceed from some version of the confidence described above — that apparent tensions between passages are resolvable, given the right historical, linguistic, or literary context, without requiring that the original text erred. Readers should know that this confidence is a starting posture drawn from the sources this book surveys, not a conclusion independently proven case by case. A small number of entries in that section are presented candidly as unresolved, precisely because a manufactured harmonization would overstate what the evidence actually supports. How much weight a given apparent difficulty should carry, and how confidently it can be resolved, will look different depending on which of the positions above a reader already holds — this book aims to give the relevant facts plainly enough that readers of any of these views can weigh them for themselves.
