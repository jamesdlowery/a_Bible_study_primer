# How to Study a Passage

Most of this book is reference material: what the manuscripts say, how the translations differ, what the denominations teach. This short chapter is the "primer" part of the title -- a working method for sitting down with a passage, in any translation, and reading it well. None of it requires Hebrew or Greek, a seminary library, or a particular denomination's commentary. It requires a Bible, a second translation or two, and the habit of asking a few questions in order.

## Start with the passage, not the question

Read the whole unit before reading the verse. A verse is a numbering convenience added centuries after the text was written; the author wrote paragraphs, speeches, poems, and stories. Find the natural edges of the unit -- where a scene changes, a speaker changes, an argument reaches its conclusion -- and read from edge to edge at least twice before doing anything else. Many "hard" verses become plain once the sentence before and the sentence after are allowed to speak.

## Read it in context, at three distances

- **Immediate context:** the paragraph and chapter. Who is speaking, to whom, about what? What was just said, and what follows?
- **Book context:** where this passage sits in the book's own argument or story. Romans 3 reads differently after Romans 1-2; Job's friends' speeches read differently once Job 42:7 is in view.
- **Canonical context:** how the rest of Scripture treats the same theme. This is where cross-references help -- and where they can mislead, if a verse from a different book, genre, and century is quietly allowed to override the plain sense of the one in front of you. Use cross-references to inform a reading, not to replace it.

## Ask what kind of writing it is

Genre governs how words mean. Narrative reports what happened without always approving it. Law states obligations for a specific covenant community. Poetry and prophecy use image, hyperbole, and parallelism (Psalm 22:16's "dogs" and "lions" are not zoological). Wisdom literature states general truths, not guarantees (Proverbs 22:6 is a proverb, not a promise). Apocalyptic writing is symbolic by design. Letters answer situations the reader must partly reconstruct. Before deciding what a passage teaches, decide what kind of text it is, and read it by the rules of that kind.

## Compare translations -- and know what a difference means

Lay two or three translations side by side, ideally from different families (see Bible Translations and Their Source Manuscripts): one from the King James tradition, one modern critical-text translation such as the ESV, NIV, or NRSV-CE, and one that reads more freely, such as the NLT. Where they agree, the underlying text is almost certainly stable and the meaning is not in doubt. Where they differ, there are only three possible reasons, and this book's two catalogs exist to tell them apart:

1. **A manuscript difference** -- the translators were working from different Hebrew or Greek wording (Mark 16:9-20, 1 John 5:7, Psalm 22:16). The Manuscript and Translation Differences chapter for that book will say so, and will tell you which manuscript families read which way.
2. **A translation-philosophy difference** -- the same wording, rendered more literally or more freely, or with a different interpretive choice (Isaiah 7:14's "virgin" or "young woman"). The same chapter covers these.
3. **An interpretive tradition** -- the text is stable and the words are the same, but readers disagree about what they imply (most of Reportedly Contradicting Passages).

Knowing which of the three you are looking at is most of the work. A reader who knows that Mark 16:9-20 is a manuscript question, and that Isaiah 7:14 is a translation question, is already reading more carefully than most commentaries assume.

## Weigh a variant without knowing Greek

You do not need the languages to follow a textual argument; you need to know what the arguments are. When a footnote says "some manuscripts add ..." or "the earliest manuscripts do not include ...," the questions a textual critic asks are these, in roughly this order: How old are the witnesses on each side, and how many independent lines of transmission do they represent? Is one reading easier to explain as arising from the other (a copyist smoothing a difficulty, harmonizing with a parallel, or adding a familiar phrase)? Does the shorter or the harder reading better explain the existence of the longer or the easier one? Manuscript Traditions: Character, Relationships, and Weighing walks through a full example on Mark's ending. The point is not to settle the question yourself but to recognize that the footnote is reporting a reasoned judgment, not an arbitrary choice.

## Use the tools for what they are

- **A concordance** finds every occurrence of a word in one translation. It tells you how *that translation* used an English word, which is not the same as how the original used a Hebrew or Greek one. An exhaustive concordance keyed to Strong's numbers gets you one step closer; it still will not tell you what the word means in this sentence.
- **A study Bible's notes** are commentary, not text. They are written from a tradition (see Prominent English Study Bibles for which one), and the same verse will carry different notes in a Reformed, a Catholic, and a Wesleyan study Bible. Read the note as a knowledgeable friend's opinion, and check whether it is answering the question you asked.
- **Cross-references** were compiled by editors, not authors. They are prompts to look further, not proof that two passages mean the same thing.
- **A second translation** is the cheapest and most reliable study tool there is. If a reading surprises you, look it up in another family before building anything on it.

## Avoid the common traps

- **Reading a verse as a slogan.** Jeremiah 29:11 is addressed to exiles in Babylon; Philippians 4:13 is about contentment in want and plenty. Both remain true and useful, but their meaning is anchored to their sentences.
- **Parallelomania.** Two passages that share a word, an image, or a number are not thereby about the same thing. Ask whether the author could have intended the connection.
- **Letting the translation you grew up with settle a question.** Every translation in this book was made by capable, believing scholars working from real evidence. Where they differ, the difference is the interesting part.
- **Treating harmonization as the only option.** Reportedly Contradicting Passages gives the harmonization most commonly offered for each claim; it also says when no harmonization commands general agreement. Living with an unresolved tension is sometimes the more honest reading.
- **Treating "the manuscripts differ" as "the Bible is unreliable."** Background on Textual Transmission explains why the opposite is closer to the truth: the differences are known precisely because the evidence is so abundant.

## A short procedure

1. Read the whole unit, twice, in the translation you normally use.
2. Read it in one translation from a different family.
3. Write down, in one sentence, what the passage is about -- before consulting any note.
4. Note every place the translations differ, and classify each difference (manuscript, translation philosophy, or interpretation) using this book's catalogs.
5. Identify the genre, and ask whether the passage is reporting, commanding, praising, predicting, or arguing.
6. Read the immediate and book context; then, and only then, the cross-references.
7. Read one or two study-Bible notes from different traditions, and notice where they agree.
8. Revise your one-sentence summary. If it did not change, you understood the passage on first reading; if it did, you have learned something a slogan would have hidden.
