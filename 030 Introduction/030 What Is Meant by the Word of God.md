# What Is Meant by "the Word of God"?

Calling the Bible "the Word of God" is a claim this whole book takes for granted rather than argues for — but the phrase itself is used in more than one sense, and it's worth being precise about which sense is meant before going further.

## The Term in Scripture Itself

Scripture uses "the word of God" (or "the word of the LORD") in at least three related but distinct ways:

- **A specific prophetic message.** Throughout the Old Testament, "the word of the LORD came to" a prophet introduces a particular oracle delivered at a particular moment (e.g., 1 Samuel 15:10, Jeremiah 1:4) — a discrete act of communication, not yet a reference to a body of writings.
- **Christ himself.** John's Gospel opens by identifying Jesus as "the Word" (Greek *logos*) who "was with God, and was God," and who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:1, 14) — a title for a person, not a text.
- **The collected, written Scriptures.** This is the sense this book uses throughout: the Old and New Testaments together, understood by the traditions this book draws on as God's own communication to humanity, given through human authors.

Two texts are usually cited as the foundation for this third sense. 2 Timothy 3:16 states that "all Scripture is God-breathed" (the Greek term, *theopneustos*, appears only here in the New Testament). 2 Peter 1:20–21 adds that prophecy never came "by the will of man," but that human authors "spoke from God" while being "carried along by the Holy Spirit."

## Inspiration Through Human Authors, Not Instead of Them

The traditions this book draws on do not generally understand inspiration as dictation, in which human authors were passive scribes taking down words with no contribution of their own. The prevailing model, sometimes called "concursive" inspiration, holds that God worked through each author's own vocabulary, style, personality, historical situation, and process of research or reflection (Luke 1:1–4 describes exactly this kind of investigative process), such that what resulted was, in its fullest sense, both a genuinely human document and a genuinely divine one — not a blend of the two, but the whole of each at once. This is why Isaiah, Paul, and John sound nothing alike, even though all three are traditionally affirmed to be equally inspired.

Traditions differ on how far this inspiration extends. **Verbal-plenary inspiration** — the majority view among the evangelical sources this book draws on most heavily — holds that inspiration reaches to the actual words chosen, not merely the general ideas behind them. Other views hold that inspiration guarantees the truthfulness of the message conveyed without requiring that every individual word was itself divinely selected. This book does not adjudicate between these positions.

## Traditions Differ on the Details, Converge on the Function

Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions all affirm Scripture's divine origin and authority, though they differ on questions this book doesn't attempt to resolve — how the canon itself was recognized, what role church tradition plays alongside Scripture in interpretation, and how directly Scripture's authority is exercised apart from the community that reads it. What unites them, for this book's purposes, is functional: each tradition treats Scripture as carrying God's own authority for matters of faith and practice, distinct in kind from any other book.

## Why This Matters for the Rest of This Book

Everything else in this book — the manuscript traditions, the translation histories, the variants, the harmonizations — concerns how this text has been *transmitted* and *rendered*, not whether it originated as claimed. A reader who understands "the Word of God" as referring to what the biblical authors originally wrote, communicated through their own genuine human authorship, is already equipped to understand why questions about later manuscript copies and later English translations are a separate matter — addressed directly in the next entry, and throughout the rest of this book.
