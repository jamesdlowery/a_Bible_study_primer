# Popular Bible Translations

Introduction To Bible Translations

**As of early 2026, the most popular English-language Bible translations, ranked primarily by recent U.S. sales data (ECPA/Circana BookScan via Christian Book Expo and related reports for 2025–early 2026), are typically:**

1. **New International Version (NIV)**

2. **English Standard Version (ESV)**

3. **King James Version (KJV)**

4. **New Living Translation (NLT)**

5. **New King James Version (NKJV)**

Close contenders that frequently appear in the top 6–10 (and sometimes swap places depending on the month, channel, or region) include the **Christian Standard Bible (CSB)**, **New American Standard Bible (NASB)**, and **New Revised Standard Version (Updated Edition / NRSVue)**.

Key nuances on “popularity”

- **Sales rankings** (the most objective measure for physical and many digital units) consistently place the **NIV first** in the U.S. and overall English market for years. It balances readability and accuracy and has massive institutional use in churches, study resources, and digital platforms.

- The **ESV** is a very strong second (and sometimes first in certain months or the UK market). It is especially favored for serious study, preaching, and among Reformed and conservative evangelical circles because of its more formal (“essentially literal”) approach.

- The **KJV** remains remarkably durable more than four centuries after its 1611 publication. It holds a large traditional following, strong sales in gift/ceremonial editions, and high cultural recognition, even as everyday reading preference has shifted toward modern language.

- The **NLT** often leads or ranks very high in Christian retail channels and among newer readers because of its highly readable, meaning-based (thought-for-thought) style.

- Rankings can vary by channel: Christian bookstores sometimes show NLT stronger -- and 2025 Christian-retail unit data specifically has shown the NLT overtaking the NIV in that channel at points during the year, even though the NIV continues to lead the broader overall U.S. market reflected in the ranking above; broader retail and digital platforms reinforce NIV/ESV dominance. Google search interest frequently favors the KJV. Digital engagement (especially YouVersion) also elevates the NIV heavily. Because retail sales rankings shift from month to month and channel to channel, readers consulting this section more than a year or so after this book's stated version date should treat the ranking above as illustrative of relative popularity rather than a current snapshot, and check ECPA/Circana's own current reporting for up-to-date figures.

These rankings reflect sales and usage patterns rather than scholarly preference or theological endorsement. Different translations serve different purposes—devotional reading, public worship, deep study, outreach, or literary appreciation—and many serious readers keep more than one.

**The differences arise primarily from underlying Greek New Testament text bases and, secondarily, from translation philosophy.**

- **KJV** and **NKJV** both print the **Textus Receptus** (TR) as their New Testament main text (the form of the text available in the 16th–17th centuries). NKJV's translators additionally consulted the Byzantine **Majority Text**, but that tradition appears only in footnotes alongside the critical-text (NU) variants—the Majority Text itself is not what stands in the NKJV's main text, and it is not identical to the TR.

- Most modern translations (NIV, ESV, NLT, CSB, NASB, NRSV/NRSVue, NET, etc.) follow the critical text (Nestle-Aland / UBS), which prioritizes the earliest extant manuscripts (e.g., Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, early papyri).

Scholars widely regard the omitted or bracketed material as later additions (scribal expansions, harmonizations with parallel passages, or marginal notes that entered the text). A common view among evangelical and critical scholars alike is that none of these differences removes any core Christian doctrine (Trinity, deity of Christ, atonement, resurrection, etc.), since parallel passages elsewhere in Scripture preserve the same teachings—but this is a theological and apologetic conclusion, not a manuscript fact, and it is worth being direct that some of these variants are not doctrinally trivial in themselves (the Comma Johanneum, for instance, is the most explicit Trinitarian formula anywhere in the NT tradition).

Below is a focused comparison for the major popular English translations. I have limited the list to the most frequently discussed cases for clarity; exhaustive word-level variants number in the thousands and are catalogued in critical apparatuses (e.g., NA28, UBS5, or Metzger’s **Textual Commentary**).

**Larger disputed passages**

- **Mark 16:9–20** (Longer Ending): Present in KJV/NKJV main text. In NIV, ESV, NLT, CSB, NRSV, etc., usually printed in double brackets or with a clear note that the earliest manuscripts end at 16:8. NASB often brackets it.

- **John 7:53–8:11** (Pericope Adulterae / woman caught in adultery): Present in KJV/NKJV. Nearly all modern critical-text translations place it in brackets or a footnote with a note that it is absent from the earliest manuscripts (though widely attested later and accepted as historically valuable by most scholars).

- **1 John 5:7b–8a** (Comma Johanneum): “For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one…” Present in KJV (and retained in NKJV with a note). Absent from virtually all modern translations; the earliest Greek evidence is extremely late (medieval). The shorter reading is the scholarly consensus.

2\. Notable Passages with Foundationally Different Wording (Textual or Translational)

These are not “missing” but involve significant wording differences that affect nuance, emphasis, or (in a few cases) apparent doctrine. Most are textual variants; a few are translation-philosophy choices.

| Verse Reference | KJV/NKJV Reading | Modern Critical-Text Reading | Nature of the Difference |
|-----------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Matt 1:25 | “her firstborn son” | “a son” / “her son” | Textual; “firstborn” absent in earliest mss |
| Matt 5:22 | “angry with his brother without a cause” | “angry with his brother” | Textual; “without a cause” later |
| Matt 6:13 | Lord’s Prayer ends with doxology (“For thine is the kingdom…”) | Doxology omitted or footnoted | Textual; absent from earliest mss (present in Didache and later tradition) |
| Matt 9:13 / Mark 2:17 | “sinners to repentance” | “sinners” | Textual; “to repentance” later addition |
| Luke 2:14 | “peace, good will toward men” | “peace among those with whom he is pleased” / “peace to those on whom his favor rests” | Textual (nominative vs genitive) |
| Luke 2:33 | “Joseph and his mother” | “his father and his mother” | Textual |
| John 1:18 | “only begotten Son” | “only God” / “the only God” (or “only Son”) | Major textual variant (μονογενὴς θεός vs υἱός) |
| John 6:47 | “believeth on me” | “believes” | Textual; “on me” later |
| Acts 20:28 | “church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood” | Same (“church of God”) in the standard critical text (NA28/UBS5); “church of the Lord” is a well-known but minority variant, not the usual critical-edition reading | Variant “God” / “Lord” |
| Col 1:14 | “redemption through his blood” | “redemption” (blood phrase omitted) | Textual; “through his blood” later expansion (cf. Eph 1:7) |
| 1 Tim 3:16 | “God was manifest in the flesh” | “He was manifested in the flesh” | Textual (Θεός vs ὅς); significant for some readers regarding explicit deity language |
| Isa 7:14 (OT) | “a virgin shall conceive” | “virgin” (NIV, ESV, CSB, NASB, NLT) or “young woman” (NRSV, some others) | Translation of Hebrew **almah**; Greek LXX has **parthenos** (“virgin”) |

Other frequent translational differences (not purely textual):

- Gender-accurate / inclusive language (especially NRSV/NRSVue and NIV 2011 in places where the original is generic).

- “Lucifer” (Isa 14:12 KJV) rendered “morning star” or “day star” in modern versions.

- Various Christological titles and the precise force of Greek articles/participles.

| Translation | Textual Basis | Treatment of Disputed Passages | Translation Philosophy |
|--------------------------|-----------------|----------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|

Pastoral perspective: These differences are real and worth studying, but the overwhelming majority of the New Testament text is identical across all major manuscript traditions, and the great majority of variants are recoverable with a high degree of confidence. The claim that "no essential doctrine hangs on any single disputed verse" is the standard evangelical-critical position and holds up reasonably well if one reasons from the whole canon rather than any one verse in isolation—but it is a theological judgment, not a neutral historical fact, and readers should be aware that a handful of the variants above (the Comma Johanneum, the ending of Mark, Isaiah 7:14) are exactly the passages where that judgment has been most contested historically. Comparing multiple good translations (one more formal, one more readable) and consulting a study Bible with textual notes remains the best practice for careful readers.
