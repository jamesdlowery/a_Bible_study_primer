# Deuteronomy: Significant Textual Variants Across Ten Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE

---

## Deuteronomy 32:8 — "sons of Israel," or "sons of God"?

**The variant:** Describing how God divided the nations among the peoples of the earth, the Masoretic Text says he set boundaries "according to the number of the **sons of Israel**." A Dead Sea Scrolls fragment of Deuteronomy (4QDeut-j) and the Septuagint instead read "according to the number of the **sons of God**" (understood as divine/angelic beings assigned to oversee the nations) — a Hebrew consonant-level difference (*bene yisra'el* vs. *bene elohim*) that most textual scholars now judge was the original wording, later altered toward "sons of Israel" for theological reasons (avoiding the implication of a divine council of lesser heavenly beings).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **"Sons of Israel" (following MT):** KJV ("children of Israel"), NKJV ("sons of Israel"), NIV ("sons of Israel"), NASB ("sons of Israel"), AMP ("sons of Israel"), and **CSB** ("the people of Israel" in its main text — despite CSB's NT following the critical text, its OT here stays with the MT rather than the DSS/LXX reading).
- **"Sons of God" / a heavenly-court reading (following the DSS/LXX):** ESV ("sons of God"), LSB ("sons of Israel" in main text with a footnote on the DSS/LXX reading — LSB is a partial exception, worth checking your specific printing), NLT ("divine beings" — a periphrastic rendering of the same underlying reading), RSV2CE ("sons of God," consistent with the RSV textual tradition it derives from, which adopted this reading early).

**The difference in meaning:** "Sons of Israel" makes the verse about God organizing the nations with Israel's future population in view — a statement centered on Israel alone. "Sons of God" instead depicts a scene where God assigns each nation to the oversight of a member of his heavenly court, with Israel kept for himself directly (as verse 9 goes on to say) — a strikingly different picture involving a divine council governing the nations, more in line with imagery found elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible (e.g., Job 1–2, Psalm 82) and in early Jewish literature. This is generally regarded as the single most significant Dead-Sea-Scrolls-confirmed variant in the entire Old Testament.

## Deuteronomy 32:43 — does the Song of Moses end with a call for angels to worship?

**The variant:** MT's ending to the Song of Moses is comparatively short. A Dead Sea Scrolls fragment (4QDeut-q) and the Septuagint both preserve additional lines not in MT, including "let all the angels of God worship him" — a line directly quoted in Hebrews 1:6 in the New Testament.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **Follow the shorter MT text, without the angels line:** KJV, NKJV, NASB, NIV.
- **Include the additional line, based on the DSS/LXX:** ESV ("rejoice with him, O heavens; bow down to him, all gods" — a related but not identical expansion; ESV's translators note the Hebrew scroll and Greek support), NLT (includes an expanded ending with a footnote explaining the DSS/Greek basis).
- I don't have solid, separately verified data for CSB, LSB, AMP, and RSV2CE on the exact wording of this specific verse — if it matters for your purposes, it's worth checking your own copies directly.

**The difference in meaning:** Without the addition, the Song of Moses ends focused on God's vindication of his people and vengeance on his adversaries. With it, the ending broadens into a cosmic scene of angelic/heavenly worship — directly relevant to how Hebrews 1:6 is read as drawing on an Old Testament text, since the quoted line only exists in the longer reading, not in MT.

## Side note: a related, lower-stakes numeral variant

- Deuteronomy contains several places (e.g., troop counts, boundary measurements) where Samaritan Pentateuch or LXX numbers differ slightly from MT without producing a documented split among the ten tracked translations; these are not itemized here since none rises to a confirmed meaning-changing difference among the ten.

## Side note: the Samaritan Pentateuch's Gerizim commandment

- After the traditional tenth commandment, the Samaritan Pentateuch inserts a lengthy passage -- drawn mainly from material found elsewhere in this book, in chapters 27 and 11 -- commanding that an altar be built on Mount Gerizim and that sacrifice be offered there, effectively making this a tenth commandment in the Samaritan tradition and Gerizim, not Jerusalem, the uniquely chosen sanctuary from the very giving of the Law. Pre-Samaritan Dead Sea Scrolls fragments (4QpaleoExodᵐ, 4QNumᵇ) already show many of the same expansions and harmonizations, confirming these were genuine, circulating ancient Palestinian textual features that the Samaritan community later adopted and intensified, not an invention original to the Samaritans themselves. None of the ten tracked translations, all working from the Masoretic Hebrew tradition, includes this material.
