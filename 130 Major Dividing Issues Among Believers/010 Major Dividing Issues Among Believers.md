# Major Dividing Issues Among Believers

Christians who read the same Bible have divided, and still divide, over a fairly stable set of questions. This section takes the 31 positions tracked for every body in "Major U.S. Christian Denominations" and every volume in "Prominent English Study Bibles" -- the eight doctrinal positions, the ten ethical and lifestyle positions, and the thirteen other divisive positions -- and gives each one a short chapter of its own: what the question is and why it divides, the main answers given, the passages each side rests on (pointing into this book's two catalogs wherever an entry bears on the question), where the 27 denominations and 27 study Bibles actually fall, the dated splits the question has produced, and how to study it for yourself.

Two things about the tables. First, the "where the bodies in this book fall" tables are generated at build time from the position tables in the two sections named above: each row shows the opening sentence of that body's or volume's position cell and the source label the text gives for it, so they can never drift from the fuller entries, and the fuller entries -- not these summaries -- are the place to read before quoting anyone. Second, the Source column means what it means in those sections: an official document, a general pattern of a tradition, or an honest "not addressed." The remaining-verifications workbook published with every release records which of these cells have been checked against the primary source and which have not.

A note on posture, repeated from the Denominations section because it matters more here: this section describes the positions and the reasons their holders give, not a verdict on which is right. Where the passages are difficult, it says so. Where a split has a date, the date is given so the reader can see that most of these divisions are old, and that the people on every side of them have been reading the same verses for a long time.

The chapters are grouped as the position tables are grouped, and numbered straight through.

## Doctrinal positions (chapters 1--8)

## 1. View of Scripture

**What divides.** Every body profiled in this book calls the Bible the Word of God; they divide over what that commits them to. Is Scripture *inerrant* -- without error in everything it affirms, including history and the natural world -- or *infallible* only in what it teaches for salvation, or authoritative as the church's normative witness without either claim? And is its meaning fixed by the text alone, or read rightly only within a living tradition (chapter 19 takes up that second question on its own)?

**The main positions.**
- *Full inerrancy* in the terms of the Chicago Statement (1978): the autographs are without error in all they affirm. Held by the Southern Baptist Convention, the Lutheran Church--Missouri Synod, the Presbyterian Church in America, the Assemblies of God and most of the evangelical study Bibles.
- *Infallibility in matters of faith and practice* (soteriological inerrancy): Scripture "inerrantly reveals the will of God concerning us in all things necessary to our salvation" (Church of the Nazarene); the Catholic formula of *Dei Verbum* 11 is similar in effect.
- *Normative witness*: Scripture is the authoritative source and norm of the church's proclamation, read critically; the mainline bodies (ELCA, PC(USA), Episcopal Church, UCC, UMC) and the historical-critical study Bibles.
- *Scripture plus further revelation*: the Latter-day Saints' additional scriptures and living prophets; Jehovah's Witnesses' Governing Body as the sole authorized interpreter; Seventh-day Adventists' reception of Ellen G. White's writings as a subordinate but authoritative source.

**Passages each side rests on.** 2 Timothy 3:16 ("All Scripture is breathed out by God") and 2 Peter 1:21 are the inspiration texts every side cites; this book's catalog entries 2TIM-001 and 2PET-003 take up what "all Scripture" could have referred to when it was written. The tensions that drive the inerrancy debate are the subject of the whole Reportedly Contradicting Passages catalog; readers wanting the sharpest test cases can start with the creation-order questions (GEN-001, GEN-002), the Goliath doublet (2SAM-002, 1CHR-015) and the regnal-age discrepancies (2KGS-012). Jude's quotation of 1 Enoch (JUDE-001) and the deuterocanonical books' status (the Apocrypha divider in that catalog) are where the canon question bites.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: View of Scripture -->

**Dated splits.** The Fundamentalist--Modernist controversy in the Presbyterian Church in the U.S.A. (1920s; Machen's departure and the founding of the Orthodox Presbyterian Church, 1936); the Southern Baptist "Conservative Resurgence" (1979--1990s), fought explicitly over inerrancy; the Lutheran Church--Missouri Synod's Seminex walkout (1974), which produced the body that later joined the ELCA; the Chicago Statement itself (1978) as the rallying document of the inerrantist side.

**How to study it.** Read the inspiration texts in context before deciding what they claim; then take a handful of the catalog's hardest entries and ask what each view of Scripture would have to say about them. The Introduction's chapter "What Is Meant by an 'Inerrant' Word of God?" sets out the vocabulary.

## 2. Salvation

**What divides.** How a person is put right with God: by grace through faith alone, with works as fruit (the Reformation position); by grace received through faith and the sacraments and expressed in cooperation with grace, so that final salvation is not settled by one past decision (Catholic and Orthodox); or by faith plus obedience to specific ordinances (Latter-day Saints, Churches of Christ on baptism). And can it be lost? Chapter 21 (predestination) and chapter 22 (sanctification) take up the two questions that sit behind this one.

**The main positions.**
- *Justification by faith alone*, imputed righteousness, with assurance: Lutheran, Reformed and Baptist bodies, and most evangelical study Bibles. The Reformed and most Baptists add that true believers persevere ("eternal security"); Lutherans, Methodists, Pentecostals and Nazarenes hold that grace can be forfeited.
- *Justification as transformation*: righteousness infused and increased through the sacraments and good works done in grace; Catholic (Trent, 1547), and in different vocabulary Orthodox *theosis*.
- *Faith plus ordinance*: baptism as the point of forgiveness (Churches of Christ, Christian Churches, United Pentecostal Church); faith, repentance, baptism and continued obedience (Latter-day Saints).
- *Universal or near-universal outcomes*: the Latter-day Saints' three degrees of glory; the hopeful universalism common in the United Church of Christ and parts of the Episcopal Church.

**Passages each side rests on.** Romans 3:28 and Ephesians 2:8--9 against James 2:24 is the oldest exchange, and the catalog's ROM-001, JAS-001 and EPH-002 walk through it; Philippians 2:12--13 (PHIL-003) is where both sides meet. Psalm 62:12's "renders to each according to his works" (PS-015) and Proverbs 17:15 against Romans 4:5 (PROV-003) are the Old Testament versions. Romans 5:18 (ROM-006) and Titus 2:11 (TITUS-004) are the universalist proof-texts; Hebrews 6:4--6 (HEB-002) is the apostasy warning that divides the security question. Acts 2:38 and Titus 3:5 (TITUS-003) carry the baptism-for-forgiveness reading; 1 Peter 3:21 (1PET-002) is its companion.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Salvation -->

**Dated splits.** Luther's *Ninety-five Theses* (1517) and the Council of Trent's Decree on Justification (1547); the Remonstrance (1610) and the Synod of Dort (1618--1619) on perseverance; the Lutheran--Catholic *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification* (1999) as the most significant attempted convergence, later affirmed by the World Methodist Council (2006) and others.

**How to study it.** Read Romans 3--5 and James 2 as whole arguments rather than as dueling verses, note what each author is arguing *against*, and only then compare their vocabulary. The Introduction's "How to Study a Passage" chapter is built for exactly this kind of comparison.

## 3. Baptism

**What divides.** Who should be baptized (infants or professing believers), how (immersion, pouring or sprinkling), what it does (confers regenerating grace, seals a covenant, or testifies to a salvation already received), and in whose name (the Trinitarian formula or "in the name of Jesus"). The denominations in this book disagree on every one of these.

**The main positions.**
- *Infant baptism as a means of grace*: Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran -- baptism regenerates.
- *Infant baptism as covenant sign*: Reformed and Presbyterian -- a sign and seal, not itself regenerating; Methodists and Episcopalians baptize infants with a range of understandings.
- *Believer's baptism by immersion as testimony*: Baptists, Pentecostals, most non-denominational churches, Seventh-day Adventists -- an ordinance, not a sacrament.
- *Believer's baptism by immersion for the remission of sins*: Churches of Christ, Christian Churches, United Pentecostal Church -- the point at which sins are forgiven.
- *Baptism in Jesus' name only*: the United Pentecostal Church, on Acts 2:38 against Matthew 28:19.
- *Proxy baptism for the dead*: Latter-day Saints, on 1 Corinthians 15:29.

**Passages each side rests on.** Matthew 28:19 against Acts 2:38, 8:16 and 19:5 (ACTS-009) is the formula question. Acts 2:38, Titus 3:5 (TITUS-003) and 1 Peter 3:21 (1PET-002) carry the regeneration readings; Acts 16:15, 33 (household baptisms) and Colossians 2:11--12 (circumcision and baptism) carry the covenant case for infants; Mark 16:16 sits inside the disputed longer ending (Manuscript and Translation Differences, Mark §1), and Acts 8:37's confession of faith before baptism is itself a textual variant (Acts §1). 1 Corinthians 15:29 (1COR-004) is the proxy-baptism text.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Baptism -->

**Dated splits.** The Anabaptist rebaptisms in Zurich (1525); the English Baptists (1609, 1638); the "Christian Baptist" break of Alexander Campbell from the Baptists over baptism's purpose (1830s); the Oneness Pentecostal "New Issue" at Arroyo Seco (1913) and the Assemblies of God's expulsion of Oneness ministers (1916).

**How to study it.** Lay every New Testament baptism narrative side by side (there are about a dozen) and record who was baptized, when, and what was said; the pattern you find is the pattern each tradition is appealing to.

## 4. The Lord's Supper

**What divides.** What happens to the bread and wine, who may receive, and how often. The sharpest early-Reformation break between Luther and Zwingli (Marburg, 1529) was on this question, and it has never been repaired.

**The main positions.**
- *Transubstantiation*: the substance of bread and wine becomes Christ's body and blood (Catholic).
- *Real presence without a defined mechanism*: Orthodox (a "mystery"); Lutheran ("in, with and under" the elements, often called sacramental union).
- *Spiritual presence*: Christ truly received by faith, the elements unchanged (Reformed, Presbyterian; Anglican formularies).
- *Memorial*: an ordinance remembering Christ's death (Baptist, Pentecostal, Churches of Christ, most non-denominational); the Churches of Christ add weekly observance as a command.
- *Latter-day Saint sacrament*: bread and water, weekly, in remembrance.
- *Jehovah's Witnesses*: an annual Memorial at which only the "anointed" partake.

**Passages each side rests on.** "This is my body" (Matthew 26:26 and parallels) read literally or figuratively; John 6:53--58 ("eat my flesh") read eucharistically or not; 1 Corinthians 10:16 and 11:23--29 on participation and discernment; Acts 20:7 for weekly observance. Luke 22:19b--20 is absent from one early manuscript stream (the "shorter" Lukan text), a variant not among those the catalog tracks; the catalog's JOHN-002 (was the Last Supper a Passover meal?) and LUKE-013 (did Judas receive communion?) bear on what the meal was and who was at it.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: The Lord's Supper -->

**Dated splits.** Fourth Lateran Council's definition of transubstantiation (1215); the Marburg Colloquy (1529); the Anglican Articles' rejection of transubstantiation (1563); the Lutheran--Reformed *Leuenberg Agreement* (1973) and the ELCA's full-communion agreements (1997 onward) as the main modern repairs on the Protestant side.

**How to study it.** Read the four institution accounts and 1 Corinthians 10--11 together, then John 6 on its own terms, asking whether John intends the Supper at all; the answer to that last question decides more than any other.

## 5. Church government

**What divides.** Who holds authority in the church and how it is transmitted: bishops in succession, elected elders in graded assemblies, or the local congregation itself. Behind the structure lies the question of whether any structure is commanded.

**The main positions.**
- *Episcopal*: bishops in apostolic succession (Catholic, with the papacy as its center; Orthodox, without it; Anglican/Episcopal; Methodist bishops without the claim of succession; Latter-day Saint hierarchy under a prophet).
- *Presbyterian*: elders in session, presbytery and general assembly (PC(USA), PCA).
- *Congregational*: the local church governs itself, often with elders or deacons (Baptists, Churches of Christ, Christian Churches, UCC, most non-denominational churches); denominations in this stream are voluntary associations.
- *Hybrid*: the Assemblies of God and Church of the Nazarene combine congregational self-government with district and general oversight; Pentecostal bodies such as COGIC use bishops.

**Passages each side rests on.** Matthew 16:18--19 ("on this rock," MATT-042) and the keys; Acts 15 (the Jerusalem council) and Acts 20:17, 28 (elders who are also "overseers"); 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1:5--7 on the offices; 1 Corinthians 3:11 against Ephesians 2:20 on the church's foundation (1COR-007); Galatians 2:11 on Paul's rebuke of Peter (GAL-001). Whether "elder" and "bishop" name one office or two is the exegetical hinge.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Church government -->

**Dated splits.** The Great Schism between Rome and Constantinople (1054), with papal primacy at its center; the Church of England's break with Rome (1534); the Westminster Assembly's presbyterian settlement (1640s) and the ejection of its ministers (1662); the Methodist Episcopal Church's organization with bishops (1784) and later splits over episcopal power (Methodist Protestant Church, 1830); the First Vatican Council's definition of papal infallibility (1870) and the Old Catholic separation that followed.

**How to study it.** Trace every use of *episkopos*, *presbyteros* and *diakonos* in the New Testament before reading any church's polity into them; a concordance does most of the work.

## 6. Eschatology

**What divides.** The shape of the end: whether Christ returns before a literal thousand-year reign (premillennialism), after a gospel-transformed age (postmillennialism), or at a single end with the "thousand years" symbolic of the present age (amillennialism); whether the church is removed before a tribulation (the pretribulation rapture of dispensationalism); and whether Israel and the church have separate futures.

**The main positions.**
- *Amillennial*: Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, most mainline bodies.
- *Historic premillennial*: many Baptists and evangelicals; the Christian and Missionary Alliance.
- *Dispensational premillennial* with a pretribulation rapture: the Assemblies of God, many Southern Baptists and non-denominational churches, the Scofield, Ryrie and MacArthur study Bibles.
- *Postmillennial*: a minority in Reformed circles, historically dominant in American Puritanism (Jonathan Edwards).
- *Distinctive systems*: Seventh-day Adventists' investigative judgment and premillennial return; Jehovah's Witnesses' 1914 chronology and earthly paradise; the Latter-day Saints' premillennial expectation with a Zion in America.

**Passages each side rests on.** Revelation 20:1--6 (the thousand years) and the four interpretive frameworks for the whole book (REV-001); Daniel 9:24--27 (DAN-007); 1 Thessalonians 4:17 (1THESS-001) on the rapture's timing; Matthew 24:34 ("this generation") and the Olivet Discourse; Romans 11:26 ("all Israel will be saved," ROM-007); 1 John 2:18's "many antichrists" against a single Antichrist (1JOHN-003); 2 Peter 3:10's "burned up" or "found," itself a textual variant (2PET-004; Manuscript and Translation Differences, 2 Peter). Luke 17:36 is a missing verse in the Second Coming discourse (Luke §4).

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Eschatology -->

**Dated splits.** The Millerite "Great Disappointment" (1844) and the Seventh-day Adventist Church that grew from it (1863); Darby's dispensationalism carried to America (1860s--1870s) and the Scofield Reference Bible (1909); the Jehovah's Witnesses' 1914 expectation and its reinterpretation; the Niagara and prophecy conferences (1875--1900) that made premillennialism an evangelical marker.

**How to study it.** Read Revelation 20 in the context of chapters 19--22 before consulting any chart, and settle for yourself how the book is to be read as a whole (REV-001) before deciding what any one passage of it means.

## 7. Spiritual gifts

**What divides.** Whether the miraculous gifts -- tongues, prophecy, healing -- continue in the church today (continuationism) or ceased with the apostolic age (cessationism), and, among those who say they continue, whether speaking in tongues is the necessary initial evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit (classical Pentecostal doctrine).

**The main positions.**
- *Classical Pentecostal*: the gifts continue and tongues are the initial evidence of Spirit baptism (Assemblies of God, COGIC, UPCI).
- *Charismatic / continuationist*: the gifts continue; no required evidence (the Catholic Charismatic Renewal, the Christian and Missionary Alliance's "seek not, forbid not," many non-denominational churches, the ESV Study Bible's general editor).
- *Open but cautious*: the gifts are not denied in principle but not expected or emphasized (Catholic and Orthodox mainstream, most mainline bodies, Nazarenes).
- *Cessationist*: the sign gifts ceased with the apostles or the completed canon (LCMS, PCA, Reformed tradition broadly, MacArthur Study Bible, Churches of Christ).

**Passages each side rests on.** 1 Corinthians 12--14, and especially 13:8--10 ("when the perfect comes") and 14:5, 39 (1COR-011); Acts 2 (JOEL-002 on Joel's promise), 8, 10 and 19 as the Pentecostal pattern texts; Mark 16:17--18 inside the disputed ending (Manuscript and Translation Differences, Mark §1); Ephesians 2:20 on apostles and prophets as foundation; 1 Maccabees 4:46 and 9:27 on the "ceasing" of prophets (1MACC-004) as an Old Testament analogue.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Spiritual gifts -->

**Dated splits.** The Azusa Street revival (1906) and the Pentecostal denominations formed in its wake (COGIC's Pentecostal reorganization 1907, the Assemblies of God 1914); the Charismatic Renewal in mainline and Catholic churches (from 1960 and 1967); the Christian and Missionary Alliance's "seek not, forbid not" formulation (1963); the "Strange Fire" conference (2013) as the cessationist side's most public modern statement.

**How to study it.** Read 1 Corinthians 12--14 as one argument about a specific congregation's problems, and ask what Paul is correcting before asking what he is commanding.

## 8. Women's ordination

**What divides.** Whether women may be ordained to the pastoral or priestly office, and if not, which ministries remain open to them. Chapter 31 takes up the wider question of men's and women's roles in the home; this chapter is about office.

**The main positions.**
- *Ordination closed to women as a matter of doctrine*: Catholic (*Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*, 1994), Orthodox, LCMS, PCA, Southern Baptist Convention (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000), COGIC, Churches of Christ, Latter-day Saints (priesthood), Jehovah's Witnesses.
- *Ordination open to women*: Methodist bodies (UMC, AME, AME Zion, GMC), Episcopal, ELCA, PC(USA), UCC, ABCUSA, Assemblies of God, Church of the Nazarene, Seventh-day Adventists in some regions (though the 2015 General Conference vote declined to authorize it), UPCI (licensing).
- *Divided or local*: the Christian and Missionary Alliance (women may be "pastors" but not elders, 2023), the National Baptist conventions (left to local churches), non-denominational churches.

**Passages each side rests on.** 1 Timothy 2:11--15 (and 1TIM-002 on "saved through childbearing"), 1 Corinthians 14:34--35 and 11:2--16 (1COR-005 on "because of the angels") against Galatians 3:28, Romans 16:1--7 (Phoebe the deacon, Junia "among the apostles"), Acts 18:26 (ACTS-008 on Priscilla), Judges 4--5 (JUDG-008 on Deborah) and Acts 2:17--18 (daughters prophesying). Sirach 25:24's "from a woman sin had its beginning" (SIR-002) is the deuterocanonical text behind one reading of 1 Timothy 2:14.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Women's ordination -->

**Dated splits.** Antoinette Brown's Congregational ordination (1853); AME Zion's ordination of Julia Foote (1894) and Mary Small as elder (1898); full clergy rights in the Methodist Church (1956) and PC(USA) predecessors (1956); the Episcopal "Philadelphia Eleven" (1974) and regularization (1976); the Southern Baptist Convention's 2000 confession and its 2023 expulsion of churches with women pastors; the Seventh-day Adventist General Conference vote (2015).

**How to study it.** Read 1 Timothy 2 and 1 Corinthians 14 alongside Romans 16 and Acts 18, and decide what each passage is addressing -- a local situation, a creation order, or both -- before deciding which governs the others.

## Ethical and lifestyle positions (chapters 9--18)

## 9. Abortion

**What divides.** Whether abortion is always, sometimes or never morally permissible, and what the church should say to the state about it. Every body in this book takes a position, and the positions range from "intrinsically evil in all cases" to "a matter of conscience."

**The main positions.**
- *Opposed in all cases where the direct intent is to end the unborn life*: Catholic, Orthodox, LCMS, PCA, Southern Baptist, Assemblies of God, Nazarene, Latter-day Saints (with stated exceptions), most evangelical bodies.
- *Opposed in principle with exceptions and pastoral latitude*: the ELCA's 1991 social statement; the Seventh-day Adventist guidelines (revised 2019); the UMC's 2024 Social Principles.
- *Support for legal access as a matter of conscience*: PC(USA), Episcopal Church, UCC, ABCUSA resolutions.
- *Internal division acknowledged*: the historically Black Baptist and Methodist bodies, where official statements are few and congregational practice varies.

**Passages each side rests on.** Psalm 139:13--16, Jeremiah 1:5 and Luke 1:41--44 on life in the womb; Exodus 21:22--25 on the penalty for causing a miscarriage, whose translation ("harm" to whom) is itself contested and is the one Old Testament text both sides read closely; Exodus 20:13 (EXOD-022 on "kill" and "murder"). The catalog has no entry devoted to abortion, because no passage addresses it by name -- which is itself part of the dispute.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Abortion -->

**Dated splits.** The Southern Baptist Convention's resolutions shifting from a 1971 position allowing exceptions to its post-1980 opposition; the Episcopal Church's resolutions from 1967 onward; the UCC's 1971 resolution; *Roe v. Wade* (1973) and *Dobbs* (2022) as the civil dates around which the denominational statements cluster; the Catholic Church's teaching restated at every stage (Catechism §2270--2275).

**How to study it.** Begin with Exodus 21:22--25 in several translations and note how the rendering decides the reading; then read the womb passages as poetry and prophecy, asking what each genre can and cannot establish.

## 10. Homosexuality

**What divides.** Whether same-sex sexual relationships are sinful, whether same-sex marriage can be blessed or solemnized, and whether openly gay or lesbian people may be ordained. This has been the most denomination-splitting question in American Christianity since 2000.

**The main positions.**
- *Same-sex acts sinful; persons to be treated with respect*: Catholic (Catechism §2357--2359), Orthodox, Southern Baptist, LCMS, PCA, Assemblies of God, Nazarene, COGIC, Latter-day Saints, Jehovah's Witnesses, Seventh-day Adventists, most Black Baptist and Methodist bodies, the Global Methodist Church.
- *Full inclusion, including marriage and ordination*: UCC (2005), Episcopal Church (2003 onward), ELCA (2009), PC(USA) (2011, 2014), UMC (2024), ABCUSA in some regions.
- *Divided*: the AME, AME Zion and National Baptist bodies hold traditional teaching with acknowledged internal discussion; the ABCUSA's General Board resolution (1992) calls the practice incompatible with Christian teaching but binds no congregation or region.

**Passages each side rests on.** Genesis 19 and the question of Sodom's sin (ISA-011; GEN-042; JUDG-013 on Gibeah); Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 and the question of which Levitical laws continue (LEV-015 on the dietary laws is the parallel case); Romans 1:26--27; 1 Corinthians 6:9--10 and 1 Timothy 1:10 and the meaning of *arsenokoitai*; Matthew 19:4--6 on the creation pattern; and, on the inclusive side, Acts 10--15 on the Gentile precedent and Galatians 3:28.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Homosexuality -->

**Dated splits.** The Episcopal Church's consecration of Gene Robinson (2003) and the Anglican Church in North America's formation (2009); the ELCA's 2009 decision and the North American Lutheran Church (2010); PC(USA)'s ordination change (2011) and ECO's formation (2012); the UMC's 2019 Special General Conference, the 2022 Global Methodist Church and the 2024 removal of the prohibitions; the UCC's marriage resolution (2005).

**How to study it.** Read the six commonly cited passages in their own contexts and then the Acts 15 precedent; the dispute is as much about how the second bears on the first as about the first themselves.

## 11. Alcohol

**What divides.** Whether Christians may drink at all. Nearly every body distinguishes moderate use from drunkenness; the division is over whether abstinence is required, expected or merely permitted.

**The main positions.**
- *Sacramental and moderate use accepted*: Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Episcopal, Presbyterian, most mainline bodies.
- *Abstinence as an official expectation*: Southern Baptist resolutions, Assemblies of God, Church of the Nazarene, Seventh-day Adventists (health doctrine), Latter-day Saints (Word of Wisdom), COGIC and UPCI (Holiness tradition).
- *Historic temperance, now a matter of conscience*: Methodist bodies (abstinence still commended as "a faithful witness" in the UMC Book of Resolutions; unfermented grape juice remains customary at Communion), Churches of Christ, most Baptist bodies outside the SBC.

**Passages each side rests on.** Psalm 104:15 and John 2:1--11 (Cana) on wine as gift; Numbers 15:5--10 on wine in the drink offerings (EXOD-026); Proverbs 20:1 and 23:29--35 (PROV-015); Ephesians 5:18; 1 Timothy 5:23; Romans 14:21 on not causing a brother to stumble, the key text for voluntary abstinence; and the "two-wine" theory that New Testament *oinos* could mean grape juice, which the abstinence side has sometimes argued and most lexicons reject.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Alcohol -->

**Dated splits.** The American temperance movement (1826 onward) and the Methodist and Baptist embrace of total abstinence; Welch's unfermented grape juice for Communion (1869); Prohibition (1920--1933); the Southern Baptist Convention's reaffirmation of abstinence (2006 resolution) against the Convention's younger ministers' drift.

**How to study it.** Read the wine passages in both testaments without deciding in advance whether *yayin* and *oinos* are fermented; the lexical evidence settles that, and the ethical question (Romans 14) remains after it does.

## 12. Divorce and remarriage

**What divides.** Whether a valid marriage can be dissolved, on what grounds, and whether the divorced may remarry. The denominations in this book differ on all three, and the difference is as old as the difference between Matthew's and Mark's wording.

**The main positions.**
- *Indissoluble; no remarriage without an annulment*: Catholic.
- *Indissoluble in principle; divorce and remarriage permitted by economy up to a limit*: Orthodox (traditionally up to three marriages).
- *Permitted on biblical grounds (sexual immorality, abandonment); remarriage then permitted*: the majority Protestant position (Lutheran, Reformed, Baptist, Pentecostal, most evangelical study Bibles).
- *Permitted as a pastoral matter without fixed grounds*: most mainline bodies.
- *Remarriage after divorce treated as adultery*: a minority in the Churches of Christ and Holiness bodies.

**Passages each side rests on.** Deuteronomy 24:1--4 and Matthew 19:3--9 against Mark 10:2--12 and Luke 16:18 (DEUT-012 on the differing wording across the Gospels); Matthew 5:32's "except for sexual immorality"; 1 Corinthians 7:10--16 (the "Pauline privilege"); Malachi 2:16, whose "I hate divorce" is a real translation crux (MAL-005); Ezra 10's forced divorces (EZRA-006). The woman caught in adultery (JOHN-001; Manuscript and Translation Differences, John §1) is often cited and is itself a disputed text.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Divorce and remarriage -->

**Dated splits.** Henry VIII's annulment and the Church of England (1534); the Council of Trent's canons on marriage (1563); the Episcopal Church's relaxation of its remarriage canons (1973); the Catholic Church's 2015 reform of the annulment process (*Mitis Iudex Dominus Iesus*) and the *Amoris Laetitia* debate (2016) over Communion for the divorced and remarried.

**How to study it.** Lay the four Gospel passages side by side and account for Matthew's exception clause before building a rule; then read 1 Corinthians 7 as Paul's own extension of the teaching to a situation Jesus had not addressed.

## 13. Contraception

**What divides.** Whether married couples may deliberately prevent conception. Until 1930 every Christian body opposed it; the Anglican Lambeth Conference's permission that year began a change that nearly all Protestant bodies followed and the Catholic Church refused.

**The main positions.**
- *Artificial contraception opposed; natural family planning permitted*: Catholic (*Humanae Vitae*, 1968).
- *Opposed by a minority tradition*: some Orthodox teaching, the "Quiverfull" movement in evangelicalism; the Latter-day Saints discourage limiting family size while leaving it to couples.
- *A matter of conscience within marriage*: nearly all Protestant bodies, which is why so many entries in this book read "not addressed" with that explanation.
- *Methods that may act after fertilization treated as abortion*: an evangelical and Catholic concern that divides the "conscience" camp internally.

**Passages each side rests on.** Genesis 1:28 ("be fruitful and multiply"); Genesis 38:8--10 (Onan), the one passage traditionally read as condemning contraception and now generally read as condemning his refusal of levirate duty; Psalm 127:3--5; 1 Corinthians 7:3--5 on marital relations apart from procreation. The catalog's levirate entries (RUTH-002, RUTH-003) bear on how Onan's duty was understood.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Contraception -->

## 14. Gambling

**What divides.** Whether games of chance for money are sinful in themselves, sinful by their effects, or morally neutral. Few bodies treat it as doctrine; several treat it as a serious matter of stewardship.

**The main positions.**
- *Neutral in itself, sinful when excessive or harmful*: Catholic (Catechism §2413), Orthodox, Lutheran, Episcopal.
- *Opposed as poor stewardship and covetousness*: Southern Baptist resolutions, Methodist Social Principles, PC(USA) General Assembly statements (2000: "refuse to participate ... as a matter of faith"), American Baptist General Board resolution against state-sponsored gambling (1996), Assemblies of God, Nazarene, Seventh-day Adventist, Latter-day Saint (official opposition, including to lotteries), most Holiness and Pentecostal bodies.
- *Not addressed*: many congregational bodies.

**Passages each side rests on.** The Bible's approving use of casting lots (Proverbs 16:33; Acts 1:26) against its warnings about wealth (1 Timothy 6:9--10, 1TIM-003 on the misquotation; Proverbs 13:11; Ecclesiastes 5:10) -- the catalog's PROV-017 is devoted to exactly this question. Exodus 20:17 on covetousness and Ephesians 4:28 on honest work are the stewardship texts.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Gambling -->

**Dated splits.** No denomination has split over gambling; the dates that matter are civil -- the spread of state lotteries (New Hampshire, 1964) and casino gaming (Nevada 1931; Atlantic City 1976; tribal gaming after 1988) -- against which the Methodist and Southern Baptist resolutions were written.

**How to study it.** Separate the three questions -- lots as a method, wealth as a motive, and harm as a result -- and read the passages for each; the Bible says a good deal about the second and third and nothing direct about modern gambling.

## 15. Dancing

**What divides.** Whether social dancing is permissible for Christians. Almost no body treats it as doctrine today, but it was a marker of Holiness, Baptist and Churches of Christ separation from "worldliness" for a century, and some bodies still discourage it.

**The main positions.**
- *Not a moral category*: Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, mainline bodies, most evangelicals.
- *Discouraged as worldly amusement*: the Holiness tradition (Nazarene membership expectations, COGIC, UPCI), Seventh-day Adventists, some Baptist and Churches of Christ congregations; the Latter-day Saints, by contrast, have historically encouraged dancing.
- *Not addressed*: most study Bibles.

**Passages each side rests on.** Exodus 15:20 (Miriam) and 2 Samuel 6:14 (David) against Exodus 32:19 (the golden calf) -- the catalog's EXOD-011 asks whether dancing is approved or condemned; Ecclesiastes 3:4 ("a time to dance"); Psalm 149:3 and 150:4; Mark 6:22 (Herodias's daughter); Romans 14 and 1 Thessalonians 5:22 ("abstain from every form of evil") on the side of caution.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Dancing -->

**Dated splits.** None at the denominational level; the relevant history is the Holiness movement's codes of conduct (1880s--1950s) and their gradual relaxation (the Nazarene *Manual* revisions of the 1990s and after; Baptist colleges lifting dance bans, e.g., Baylor 1996).

**How to study it.** Read every dance passage in the Old Testament; note that the only condemned dance is condemned for its idolatry, not its steps, and ask what the New Testament's "worldliness" texts actually name.

## 16. Premarital sex/cohabitation

**What divides.** Not whether the Bible reserves sex for marriage -- every body in this book says it does -- but how the teaching is held: as a firm expectation of membership, as pastoral counsel, or as a norm acknowledged while cohabiting members are quietly accommodated.

**The main positions.**
- *Chastity outside marriage required; cohabitation incompatible with membership in good standing*: Catholic, Orthodox, Southern Baptist, LCMS, PCA, Pentecostal and Holiness bodies, Latter-day Saints, Jehovah's Witnesses, Seventh-day Adventists.
- *The same norm, held pastorally with acknowledged accommodation*: most mainline bodies; the ELCA's 2009 statement says the church "does not favor cohabitation arrangements outside of marriage" while asking pastors to accompany such couples.
- *Not addressed as doctrine*: a few congregational bodies and the background study Bibles.

**Passages each side rests on.** 1 Corinthians 6:12--20 and 7:1--9, 1 Thessalonians 4:3--8, Hebrews 13:4 and the New Testament's *porneia* vocabulary; Deuteronomy 22:13--29 on the Old Testament's treatment of premarital relations; Genesis 2:24 and Matthew 19:5 on the one-flesh union; John 4:16--18 on the Samaritan woman's "husband." The catalog's GEN-014 (Lamech's two wives) and GEN-048 (Jacob's two sisters) are where the Old Testament's marriage practice is examined.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Premarital sex/cohabitation -->

**Dated splits.** None by denomination; the relevant dates are the ELCA's 2009 social statement and the Church of England's 2023 debates on blessing couples, each of which touched cohabitation as a side question, and the Catholic *Amoris Laetitia* (2016) on accompanying couples in "irregular" situations.

**How to study it.** Read 1 Corinthians 6--7 as a single discussion and define *porneia* from its New Testament uses before applying it; the word's scope is where the disagreement actually lives.

## 17. War/pacifism

**What divides.** Whether Christians may bear arms and whether the state's wars can be just. Every body in this book takes some position, from historic peace-church pacifism to the just-war tradition, with the middle ground of individual conscience.

**The main positions.**
- *Just war*: Catholic (Catechism §2309), Orthodox (with a stronger reluctance), Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Baptist, most evangelical bodies.
- *Pacifism or non-combatancy*: Jehovah's Witnesses (absolute); Seventh-day Adventists (non-combatancy as the recommended position); COGIC historically; Churches of Christ in the Lipscomb tradition until the Second World War; the Assemblies of God (1917) and the UPCI's conscientious-scruples article, each now leaving it to individual conscience; the Latter-day Saints, who teach obedience to the state in war.
- *Conscience with strong peace emphasis*: Methodist Social Principles (the 2024 text "deplores war and all other forms of violent conflict"; the earlier wording called war "incompatible with the teachings and example of Christ"), UCC, Episcopal Church, PC(USA).

**Passages each side rests on.** Matthew 5:38--48 (turn the other cheek; love your enemies) and Romans 12:17--21 against Romans 13:1--4 (the sword) and Luke 3:14 (soldiers not told to leave the army); Exodus 15:3 against Isaiah 9:6 (EXOD-015 on a warlike God and a God of peace; ISA-017); Exodus 20:13 and the Levites' killing (EXOD-022); the conquest commands (DEUT-010) and Isaiah 2:4's swords into plowshares (ISA-002); Luke 22:36--38 on the two swords; Luke 9:55--56 is itself a textual variant (Manuscript and Translation Differences, Luke §3).

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: War/pacifism -->

**Dated splits.** The Anabaptist Schleitheim Confession (1527) and the peace churches; the Assemblies of God's 1917 pacifist statement and 1967 revision; the Seventh-day Adventist non-combatancy position (Civil War era, 1864); Jehovah's Witnesses' refusals and the Supreme Court cases of the 1940s; the Methodist "incompatible with the teachings of Christ" language (1972 onward).

**How to study it.** Read the Sermon on the Mount and Romans 12--13 as a single stretch of teaching about enemies, then the Old Testament war narratives on their own terms; the question is how the two testaments relate, not what either says by itself.

## 18. Death penalty

**What divides.** Whether the state may rightly execute, and whether the church should oppose it. The Catholic revision of 2018 moved the largest body in this book from permission to opposition, and the Protestant bodies are split along familiar lines.

**The main positions.**
- *Opposed in all cases*: Catholic (Catechism §2267, 2018), Orthodox statements in the United States, ELCA, UMC, Episcopal, PC(USA), UCC, ABCUSA, AME, most mainline bodies; Seventh-day Adventists discourage it.
- *Permitted as within the state's authority, without advocacy*: Southern Baptist (2000 resolution supports it), LCMS, PCA, Assemblies of God, Latter-day Saints, most evangelical study Bibles.
- *Not addressed*: several congregational and Pentecostal bodies.

**Passages each side rests on.** Genesis 9:6 ("whoever sheds the blood of man"), Romans 13:4 (the sword) and the Mosaic capital statutes (LEV-005 on the Levitical death penalties against the woman caught in adultery; MATT-056 on Jesus' citation of the penalty for cursing parents) against Matthew 5:38--39, John 8:1--11 (JOHN-001, a disputed text), Ezekiel 33:11 ("no pleasure in the death of the wicked") and the mark of Cain (GEN-013). Joshua 7 (JOSH-007 on Achan's children) is where the Old Testament's practice is hardest.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Death penalty -->

**Dated splits.** The Catholic Catechism's 1992 text, John Paul II's *Evangelium Vitae* (1995), and the 2018 revision; the Southern Baptist Convention's 2000 resolution; the mainline resolutions clustering around *Furman v. Georgia* (1972) and *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976).

**How to study it.** Read Genesis 9:6 and Romans 13:4 closely for what they permit and what they command, which are not the same thing; then ask whether the Old Testament's capital statutes were for Israel's theocracy or for every state.

## Other divisive positions (chapters 19--31)

## 19. Scripture and Tradition

**What divides.** Whether Scripture alone is the final authority (*sola scriptura*), or Scripture read within and alongside an authoritative Tradition -- the councils, creeds, Fathers and the church's teaching office -- or Scripture plus continuing revelation. This is the question that lies beneath most of the others in this section, since it decides who gets to settle them.

**The main positions.**
- *Scripture and Tradition as one deposit, interpreted by the Magisterium*: Catholic (*Dei Verbum*).
- *Scripture within Holy Tradition*: Orthodox -- Tradition is the whole life of the church, of which Scripture is the central part.
- *Scripture as sole final authority, with creeds and confessions as subordinate standards*: Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican (with reason and tradition as aids), Methodist (the "quadrilateral").
- *Scripture alone with no binding creeds*: Baptist, Churches of Christ, Pentecostal and most non-denominational bodies.
- *Scripture plus continuing revelation or an authorized interpreter*: Latter-day Saints, Jehovah's Witnesses, and in a qualified way Seventh-day Adventists.

**Passages each side rests on.** 2 Thessalonians 2:15 ("hold to the traditions... by word or by letter") and 1 Timothy 3:15 (the church as "pillar of truth") for Tradition; Mark 7:8--13 (the "tradition of men") and 2 Timothy 3:16--17 (sufficiency) for Scripture alone; Acts 17:11 (the Bereans); John 16:13 and Matthew 16:18--19 (MATT-042) for a teaching office; 2 Peter 1:20 on private interpretation. The catalog's entries on non-canonical citations (JUDE-001, JUDE-002, 2MACC-002) and on the deuterocanon's doctrinally weighted passages (2MACC-005) are where the canon and tradition questions meet.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Scripture and Tradition -->

**Dated splits.** The Leipzig Debate (1519), where Luther denied the infallibility of councils; the Council of Trent's decree on Scripture and Tradition (1546); the Restoration Movement's "no creed but Christ" (1809 onward); the First Vatican Council (1870); *Dei Verbum* (1965).

**How to study it.** Read 2 Thessalonians 2:15 and Mark 7 together and ask what each means by "tradition"; then ask how any reader decides what Scripture means when readers disagree -- which is the question every answer to this chapter is trying to settle.

## 20. The Trinity and the person of Christ

**What divides.** Whether God is one being in three co-equal, co-eternal persons (the Nicene faith shared by Catholic, Orthodox and nearly all Protestant bodies) or something else: one person manifesting in three modes (Oneness Pentecostalism), three separate beings united in purpose (Latter-day Saints), or one God with Jesus as his first creation (Jehovah's Witnesses). The *filioque* -- whether the Spirit proceeds from the Father "and the Son" -- divides East from West within the Nicene consensus.

**The main positions.**
- *Nicene Trinity with the filioque*: Catholic and all Western Protestant bodies.
- *Nicene Trinity without the filioque*: Orthodox.
- *Oneness*: the United Pentecostal Church International -- Jesus is the one God manifest in flesh; baptism in Jesus' name.
- *Social or tritheistic Godhead*: Latter-day Saints -- three distinct beings, the Father embodied.
- *Unitarian subordination*: Jehovah's Witnesses -- Jesus a created being, the Spirit an active force.

**Passages each side rests on.** John 1:1 (JOHN-011 on "the Word was God"), John 1:18 (Manuscript and Translation Differences, John §2, "only begotten Son" or "only God"), John 10:30 and 14:28 ("the Father is greater than I"), Colossians 1:15 ("firstborn of all creation," COL-001, named in the catalog as the central text of a live denominational dispute), Philippians 2:6--11, Hebrews 1:8, Matthew 28:19 against Acts 2:38 (ACTS-009), John 15:26 on who sends the Spirit (JOHN-020), Deuteronomy 6:4 and Isaiah 44:6--8 (ISA-010); 1 John 5:7--8, the "Comma Johanneum," whose explicit Trinitarian wording is a late addition (Manuscript and Translation Differences, 1 John §1); 1 Timothy 3:16, "God was manifested" or "He who was manifested" (1 Timothy §1).

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: The Trinity and the person of Christ -->

**Dated splits.** Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381); Chalcedon (451) and the Oriental Orthodox separation; the *filioque* and the Great Schism (1054); the Socinian and Unitarian movements (16th--19th centuries); Joseph Smith's First Vision and the Latter-day Saint Godhead (1820s--1840s); Charles Taze Russell's Bible Students (1870s); the Oneness "New Issue" (1913) and the Assemblies of God's Trinitarian statement (1916).

**How to study it.** Read John 1, Philippians 2, Colossians 1 and Hebrews 1 in full, noting both the statements of Christ's deity and the statements of his subordination in the same chapters; every position in this chapter is an attempt to hold those two sets together, and the textual variants listed above are where the manuscript evidence enters the argument.

## 21. Predestination and free will

**What divides.** Whether God's choice of who will be saved is unconditional and prior to any human response (Augustinian, Calvinist), conditioned on foreseen faith or resting on a freedom that prevenient grace restores (Arminian, Wesleyan, Orthodox synergy), or a question the church leaves open (Catholic, Lutheran single predestination). The Southern Baptist Convention's own unresolved debate shows the question is as live inside denominations as between them.

**The main positions.**
- *Unconditional election and reprobation*: PCA and the Reformed confessions; the Reformation, MacArthur and Edwards study Bibles.
- *Single predestination*: Lutheran (elect by grace, none predestined to damnation).
- *Prevenient grace and free response*: Methodist bodies, Nazarene, Assemblies of God, Pentecostal and Holiness bodies, Seventh-day Adventists, Churches of Christ (strongly), Latter-day Saints (moral agency).
- *Grace primary, free will cooperating, the mechanism undecided*: Catholic; Orthodox synergy.
- *Deliberately unsettled*: Southern Baptist Convention (*BF&M* Art. V), Christian and Missionary Alliance, most non-denominational churches.

**Passages each side rests on.** Romans 9 (ROM-002, which the catalog treats as the center of the question), Ephesians 1:4--11, John 6:44 and 10:26--29 on the Reformed side; 1 Timothy 2:4, 2 Peter 3:9 (2PET-002), John 3:16, Deuteronomy 30:19 (DEUT-023) and Joshua 24:15 on the other; Malachi 1:2--3 on Jacob and Esau (MAL-001) and Exodus 4--14 on Pharaoh's heart (EXOD-010) as the Old Testament sources of Romans 9; Philippians 2:12--13 (PHIL-003) and Hebrews 6:4--6 (HEB-002) on perseverance. Romans 8:1's qualifying clause is a textual variant that bears on assurance (Manuscript and Translation Differences, Romans §1).

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Predestination and free will -->

**Dated splits.** Augustine against Pelagius (411--431) and the Second Council of Orange (529); the Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577); the Remonstrance and Dort (1610, 1618--1619); Wesley and Whitefield's breach (1741); the Missouri Synod's Predestinarian Controversy (1880s); the Southern Baptist Calvinism Advisory Committee (2013).

**How to study it.** Read Romans 9--11 as one argument, from the question of Israel in 9:1--5 to the doxology in 11:33--36, before extracting any verse from it; the catalog's ROM-002 and ROM-007 are the two ends of that argument.

## 22. Sanctification and holiness

**What divides.** How a Christian becomes holy: progressively, never completely in this life (Lutheran, Reformed, Baptist); through a distinct second work of grace that cleanses from inward sin ("entire sanctification," the Wesleyan-Holiness distinctive); as *theosis*, participation in the divine life (Orthodox); or as one continuous work of grace with justification (Catholic). The Pentecostal bodies divide among themselves over whether Spirit baptism is a second or a third stage.

**The main positions.**
- *Progressive, imperfect in this life*: Lutheran, Reformed, Baptist, most evangelical bodies; "lordship" versus "free grace" is a dispute within this camp over how much obedience assurance requires (the MacArthur and Ryrie study Bibles are its two poles).
- *Entire sanctification as a second work*: Church of the Nazarene (its defining doctrine), the Methodist bodies formally, COGIC (as the second of three stages).
- *Spirit baptism without a prior sanctification crisis*: Assemblies of God ("finished work" Pentecostalism).
- *Theosis*: Orthodox.
- *Continuous with justification; purgatory completes it*: Catholic.
- *Holiness expressed in required outward standards*: UPCI, and historically the Holiness bodies.

**Passages each side rests on.** 1 Thessalonians 5:23 ("sanctify you completely") and 1 John 3:6--9 for the Holiness reading; Romans 7:14--25 (ROM-005 on whether the "wretched man" is the Christian) and Philippians 3:12 (PHIL-006, "not that I have already become perfect") for the progressive reading; 2 Peter 1:4 ("partakers of the divine nature") for *theosis*; Matthew 5:48 ("be perfect"); Hebrews 12:14; 1 John 4:18 against the fear of God (LEV-013); 1 Corinthians 3:15 ("saved, but only as through fire," 1COR-006) for purgatory.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Sanctification and holiness -->

**Dated splits.** Wesley's *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection* (1766); the Holiness movement's camp meetings (1867 onward) and the Methodist expulsions and separations that produced the Nazarenes (1908); the "finished work" controversy that split Pentecostalism (Durham, 1910--1914) and distinguished the Assemblies of God from COGIC; the "lordship salvation" debate (1988 onward).

**How to study it.** Read Romans 6--8 as Paul's own account of the Christian life, settle ROM-005 for yourself, and then test 1 John 3 and Philippians 3 against it; the answer to "who is the wretched man?" decides most of this chapter.

## 23. Hell, judgment and the fate of the lost

**What divides.** Whether the lost suffer eternal conscious punishment, are finally destroyed (annihilationism, conditional immortality), or are ultimately restored (universalism); and whether there is a purgatorial purification for the saved. Nearly every body in this book formally affirms eternal punishment; the differences are in the few that do not and in how much the rest say about it.

**The main positions.**
- *Eternal conscious punishment*: Catholic, Orthodox (as the experience of God's presence by those who reject him), Lutheran, Reformed, Baptist, Pentecostal, Holiness, Latter-day Saints for the "sons of perdition" only, and nearly all evangelical study Bibles.
- *Annihilation / conditional immortality*: Seventh-day Adventists, Jehovah's Witnesses; a minority among evangelicals (the Churches of Christ's Edward Fudge).
- *Near-universal salvation in graded glories*: Latter-day Saints.
- *Hopeful universalism, without formal teaching*: common in the UCC and parts of the Episcopal Church; a theological current in Orthodoxy.
- *Purgatory*: Catholic; rejected by all Protestant bodies, with the Orthodox praying for the dead without the doctrine.

**Passages each side rests on.** Matthew 25:46, Mark 9:43--48, Revelation 14:11 and 20:10--15 for eternal punishment; Matthew 10:28 ("destroy both soul and body"), Malachi 4:1--3 and Romans 6:23 ("the wages of sin is death") for annihilation; Ecclesiastes 9:5 ("the dead know nothing," ECCL-003) and Daniel 12:2 (DAN-012) on the state of the dead; Luke 16:19--31 and Luke 23:43's comma (LUKE-011) on the intermediate state; Romans 5:18 (ROM-006), 1 Corinthians 15:22 and Colossians 1:20 for universalism; 1 Corinthians 3:15 (1COR-006), 2 Maccabees 12:44--45 (2MACC-005) and Matthew 12:32 for purgatory; Joshua 24:29 and Ecclesiastes 3:19--21 (JOSH-019, ECCL-002) on whether death is the end at all.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Hell, judgment and the fate of the lost -->

**Dated splits.** The condemnation of Origen's *apokatastasis* (553); the Reformation's rejection of purgatory (1517 onward; Luther's *Ninety-five Theses* begin with it); the Adventist adoption of conditional immortality (1840s--1860s); Russell's rejection of hell (1870s); the evangelical annihilationism debate after John Stott's statement (1988); Rob Bell's *Love Wins* (2011) as the popular flashpoint.

**How to study it.** List every word the Bible uses for the fate of the lost (*Sheol*, *Hades*, *Gehenna*, "destruction," "eternal," "fire") and where each appears; much of the debate is about whether "eternal" modifies the punishing or the punishment, and whether "destroy" means end or ruin.

## 24. Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead

**What divides.** Whether Mary and the saints may be venerated and asked to intercede, whether icons and images belong in worship, whether the living may pray for the dead, and what is believed about Mary herself (perpetual virginity, Immaculate Conception, Assumption). The Catholic and Orthodox "yes" and the Protestant "no" is the most visible everyday difference between the traditions in this book.

**The main positions.**
- *Veneration, intercession, icons and prayer for the dead affirmed*: Catholic (with the Marian dogmas of 1854 and 1950 and the doctrine of purgatory), Orthodox (without the Western dogmas or purgatory).
- *Mary honored, invocation rejected, the dead commended without intercession*: Lutheran, Anglican (prayers for the dead retained in the Prayer Book), Methodist.
- *No veneration, no prayer to or for the dead*: Reformed, Baptist, Pentecostal, Holiness, Restorationist and non-denominational bodies.
- *Temple ordinances for the dead instead of prayer*: Latter-day Saints.

**Passages each side rests on.** Luke 1:28, 42, 48 ("all generations will call me blessed") and John 19:26--27 for Marian honor; Luke 11:27--28 (LUKE-015) on Jesus' redirection of praise of his mother; Matthew 12:46--50 and Mark 6:3 on Jesus' "brothers," read as siblings or kinsmen; Isaiah 7:14 (ISA-003 on the *almah*/virgin question); Revelation 5:8 and 8:3--4 (the prayers of the saints), Hebrews 12:1 (the "cloud of witnesses") and 2 Maccabees 15:12--14 for intercession; 2 Maccabees 12:44--45 (2MACC-005) for prayer for the dead, against Hebrews 9:27 and Luke 16:26; 1 Timothy 2:5 ("one mediator") and Exodus 20:4--5 (the image commandment; NUM-005 on the bronze serpent, JUDG-007 on Gideon's ephod) on the Protestant side; Colossians 2:18 on "worship of angels" (COL-003); 1 Corinthians 15:29 (1COR-004) for the Latter-day Saint practice.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead -->

**Dated splits.** Ephesus (431) and the title *Theotokos*; the Iconoclast controversy and the Second Council of Nicaea (787); the Reformation's rejection of invocation and purgatory (Augsburg Confession XXI, 1530; the Anglican Articles XXII, 1563); the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950) as dogmas; the Second Vatican Council's placement of Mary within *Lumen Gentium* (1964).

**How to study it.** Read Luke 1--2 and John 19 for what they say of Mary, then the "brothers" passages with a lexicon; then take the intercession question separately -- Revelation 5 and 8, Hebrews 12, 1 Timothy 2:5 -- since it does not depend on the Marian one.

## 25. Worship: day, style and liturgy

**What divides.** Which day is the day of worship (Sunday, or the seventh-day Sabbath), whether worship follows a fixed liturgy or a free order, what may be sung and with what accompaniment, and how the Regulative Principle (only what Scripture commands) or the Normative Principle (whatever Scripture does not forbid) governs the choice. Three bodies in this book worship on Saturday; one forbids instruments; several require a prayer book or missal; most leave the form to the congregation.

**The main positions.**
- *Sunday, fixed liturgy*: Catholic (the Mass), Orthodox (the Divine Liturgy), Lutheran, Episcopal, with Methodist and Presbyterian orders of worship that are standard but not mandatory.
- *Sunday, free order*: Baptist, Pentecostal, Holiness, non-denominational, Restorationist bodies; the Churches of Christ add *a cappella* singing as a command.
- *Saturday Sabbath*: Seventh-day Adventists; the Complete Jewish Study Bible's Messianic Jewish setting; the Jehovah's Witnesses' meetings fall on neither principle.
- *Sunday with the Sabbath command retained*: the Reformed "Christian Sabbath" (PCA; Westminster Confession XXI).

**Passages each side rests on.** Exodus 20:8--11 and Deuteronomy 5:12--15 (DEUT-001 on the two reasons given), Isaiah 66:23 and Matthew 24:20 for the Sabbath; Acts 20:7, 1 Corinthians 16:2, Revelation 1:10 (the "Lord's day") and the resurrection appearances for Sunday; Colossians 2:16--17 (COL-004 and LEV-017 on whether the Sabbath was fulfilled and set aside), Romans 14:5 and Galatians 4:10 for freedom; Ephesians 5:19 and Colossians 3:16 ("singing") for the *a cappella* argument, Psalm 150 against it; Deuteronomy 12 on the single place of worship (DEUT-003); John 4:21--24 on worship "in spirit and truth."

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Worship: day, style and liturgy -->

**Dated splits.** The Seventh Day Baptists (1650s) and the Adventist adoption of the Sabbath (1846); the Stone-Campbell division over instruments, formalized in the 1906 census separation of the Churches of Christ from the Disciples; the Anglican Prayer Book controversies (1549--1662; 1979 in the Episcopal Church); the Catholic liturgical reform (1970) and the Latin Mass restrictions (2007 permission, 2021 restriction); the "worship wars" over contemporary music in evangelical and Lutheran churches (1980s--2000s).

**How to study it.** Read the Sabbath commandment in both forms, then every New Testament mention of the first day and the Sabbath, and decide what Colossians 2:16 is saying; the instrument question is a separate exercise in how silence in Scripture is to be read.

## 26. Church and state / political engagement

**What divides.** How the church should relate to civil government: as partner (the Byzantine *symphonia*, the Reformation's magisterial churches), as a separated institution with a public voice (most American bodies), as a prophetic critic (the Black church tradition, the mainline advocacy offices), or as a body that withdraws from politics altogether (Jehovah's Witnesses; the historic Churches of Christ). Within the separation consensus, the bodies divide sharply over whether the church should lobby, endorse, or stay silent.

**The main positions.**
- *Active public advocacy through denominational offices*: Catholic (USCCB), UMC, ELCA, Episcopal, PC(USA), UCC, Southern Baptist (ERLC), Seventh-day Adventist (religious liberty).
- *Engagement as citizens with a deliberately non-political pulpit*: LCMS (two kingdoms), PCA ("spirituality of the church"), MacArthur's position, most non-denominational churches.
- *Prophetic engagement rooted in the civil-rights tradition*: AME, AME Zion, the National Baptist conventions, COGIC.
- *Partisan neutrality with positions on moral questions*: Latter-day Saints.
- *Withdrawal*: Jehovah's Witnesses; Lipscomb's historic Churches of Christ position.

**Passages each side rests on.** Romans 13:1--7 and 1 Peter 2:13--17 for submission; Acts 5:29 ("obey God rather than men") and Revelation 13 for resistance; Matthew 22:21 ("render to Caesar"); John 18:36 ("my kingdom is not of this world"); Jeremiah 29:7 (seek the welfare of the city); 1 Samuel 8 on Israel's request for a king (1SAM-021); Daniel 3 and 6 on civil disobedience; Amos 5 and Micah 6:8 (MIC-003) for the prophetic tradition. Exodus 20:13's "kill" or "murder" (EXOD-022) bears on the state's sword.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Church and state / political engagement -->

**Dated splits.** Constantine and the Edict of Milan (313); the Investiture Controversy (1075--1122); the Anabaptist separation (1527); the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (1786) and the First Amendment (1791), with the Baptists as its chief religious advocates; the Southern Baptist--Northern Baptist split over slavery (1845); the Progressive National Baptist separation over civil-rights activism (1961); the Religious Right's organization (Moral Majority, 1979); the Johnson Amendment debates (2017 onward).

**How to study it.** Read Romans 13 and Revelation 13 together -- the same empire, described two ways -- and ask what each author expected of Christians under it; then read 1 Samuel 8 for what the Old Testament thought of kings in the first place.

## 27. Race, ethnicity and the church

**What divides.** Not, today, whether racism is sin -- every body in this book that addresses it says so -- but how the churches account for their own histories, whether structural remedies (reparations, race-conscious policy) belong in church teaching, and how the inheritance of segregated denominations is to be handled. The denominational map of American Christianity was drawn by race: the historically Black bodies profiled here exist because white bodies excluded their founders.

**The main positions.**
- *Racism as sin with formal repentance and structural commitments*: Episcopal (2006 apology; reparations in some dioceses), ELCA (2019 declaration), PC(USA) (Belhar, 2016), UMC (2000 act of repentance), UCC, ABCUSA, Catholic (two pastoral letters).
- *Racism as sin with formal repentance but caution toward structural frameworks*: Southern Baptist (1995 apology; 2019--2021 CRT resolutions), PCA (2016 repentance; 2018 report), Assemblies of God (1989; the 1994 "Memphis Miracle"), LCMS (1994).
- *Racial justice as foundational identity*: AME, AME Zion, National Baptist bodies, COGIC.
- *Past exclusion disavowed*: Latter-day Saints (1978 revelation; 2013 essay).
- *Not formally addressed*: several congregational and Pentecostal bodies.

**Passages each side rests on.** Genesis 1:26--27 and Acts 17:26 ("from one man every nation") on the unity of humanity; Galatians 3:28, Ephesians 2:14--16 and Revelation 7:9 on the church; Acts 10 and 15 on Gentile inclusion (GEN-051); Genesis 9:20--27, the "curse of Canaan," whose misuse to justify slavery is recorded in the Scofield Reference Bible's own 1917 note (GEN-027 on inherited punishment); the slavery passages (LEV-009; Philemon; Ephesians 6:5--9) and their nineteenth-century use on both sides; Numbers 12:1 on Moses' Cushite wife (NUM-017); Amos 9:7 (AMOS-003) on God's dealings with other nations; Hosea 1--2 and Paul's application to the Gentiles (HOS-011).

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Race, ethnicity and the church -->

**Dated splits.** The AME walkout at St. George's (1787) and the AME (1816) and AME Zion (1821) churches; the Baptist (1845), Methodist (1844) and Presbyterian (1861) North--South divisions over slavery; the National Baptist Convention (1895); COGIC's separation from the ministers who formed the Assemblies of God (1914); the Methodist Central Jurisdiction (1939--1968); the Latter-day Saint revelation (1978); the Southern Baptist apology (1995) and the "Memphis Miracle" (1994).

**How to study it.** Read Acts 10--15 as the New Testament's own account of an ethnic division in the church and how it was handled; then read Genesis 9 and Philemon with the catalog's entries beside them, to see what was done with those texts and why it was wrong.

## 28. Creation and the age of the earth

**What divides.** Whether Genesis 1 describes six literal, recent days (young-earth creationism), long ages (day-age, progressive creation), a literary framework or ancient cosmology compatible with an old earth and evolution (theistic evolution, evolutionary creation), or a question the text does not address; and whether Adam and Eve were a historical pair. The study Bibles show the range more clearly than the denominations, because Genesis 1 is where a commentary must commit itself.

**The main positions.**
- *Young earth, six literal days, global Flood*: LCMS, Seventh-day Adventists (as doctrine since 2015), the Henry Morris and MacArthur study Bibles, the Evidence Study Bible, most Churches of Christ, many Baptists and Pentecostals.
- *Old earth, special creation of humanity, mechanism open*: Catholic (evolution compatible if God creates each soul), Nazarene (*Manual*), PCA (the 2000 report describes four views held in the church), the ESV Study Bible, Jehovah's Witnesses (long "days," no evolution).
- *Six-day format and a historical Adam affirmed, evolution rejected, the earth's age not addressed*: Assemblies of God (2010 paper as revised in 2014).
- *Evolution accepted as compatible with faith*: UMC, Episcopal (2006), UCC (2008), PC(USA), ELCA in practice, the Cultural Backgrounds and Oxford Annotated readings.
- *No position*: Orthodox, Latter-day Saints, most Black Baptist and Methodist bodies, and the eleven denominations whose cells read "not addressed."

**Passages each side rests on.** Genesis 1--2 and the catalog's creation-order entries (GEN-001, GEN-002, GEN-003 on "the day" of Genesis 2:4, GEN-005, GEN-006); Exodus 20:11 ("in six days"); Genesis 5 and 11 genealogies (GEN-035 on life spans); the Flood narratives (GEN-016, GEN-022, GEN-023, GEN-024); Psalm 90:4 and 2 Peter 3:8 ("a day is as a thousand years"); Romans 5:12--21 and 1 Corinthians 15:21--22, 45 on a historical Adam; Wisdom 11:17 against 2 Maccabees 7:28 on creation from nothing (WIS-004, 2MACC-004); 1 Samuel 2:8 on the earth's pillars (1SAM-022) as a test of how literally cosmological language is to be read.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Creation and the age of the earth -->

**Dated splits.** *The Genesis Flood* (1961) and the Creation Research Society (1963), which revived young-earth creationism; the Seventh-day Adventist amendment of Fundamental Belief 6 (2015); the Southern Baptist 1982 resolution; the Assemblies of God's 2010 revision of its 1977 statement; the PCA Creation Study Committee (2000); the Catholic *Humani Generis* (1950) and John Paul II's 1996 address; the Episcopal and UCC resolutions (2006, 2008).

**How to study it.** Read Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 as two accounts and settle the catalog's GEN-001 through GEN-006 for yourself before asking about geology; the literary question of how the two chapters relate is prior to every scientific one, and the study Bibles that commit on Genesis 1 do so there.

## 29. Tithing and the prosperity gospel

**What divides.** Whether the Old Testament tithe binds Christians, whether giving should be mandated or free, and -- the sharper question -- whether God promises material prosperity to the faithful giver (the "prosperity gospel" or Word of Faith teaching), which every body in this book that addresses it rejects, while several acknowledge that it is taught in some of their own congregations.

**The main positions.**
- *Tithe required as doctrine or membership expectation*: Latter-day Saints (a condition of temple recommend), Seventh-day Adventists, Nazarene ("storehouse tithing"), COGIC, UPCI, AME and AME Zion (membership expectations), Assemblies of God (expected of ministers).
- *Tithe commended as a guide; giving proportionate and free*: Catholic (support according to means), Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, Baptist, Methodist, Episcopal (the tithe affirmed as "the standard of giving" in 1982 and as the "minimum standard" in later reaffirmations), Churches of Christ (weekly giving, not a tithe), the dispensational study Bibles (the tithe as law, Christian giving as grace), MacArthur (tithing not a New Testament requirement).
- *No tithing, voluntary contributions only*: Jehovah's Witnesses.
- *Prosperity teaching*: no body in this book holds it officially; the Assemblies of God formally criticized it in 1980, and the AME, COGIC, National Baptist and non-denominational entries acknowledge its presence in some congregations.

**Passages each side rests on.** Genesis 14:20 and 28:22 (Abraham's and Jacob's tithes, before the Law), Leviticus 27:30--33, Numbers 18:21--28, Deuteronomy 14:22--29 (the tithes of the Law), Malachi 3:8--12 (MAL-003, named in the catalog as "a specific prosperity promise") for the tithe; Matthew 23:23 on the tithe under Jesus; 2 Corinthians 8--9 ("as he has decided in his heart") and 1 Corinthians 16:2 ("as he may prosper") for free giving; Deuteronomy 28 and Proverbs 3:9--10 (PROV-005) for prosperity promises, against Job (JOB-012, PS-014, PS-019 on the prosperity of the wicked), 1 Timothy 6:5--10 (1TIM-003 on the love of money) and 2 Corinthians 12:7--10; Ecclesiastes 10:19 on money as "the answer" (ECCL-006).

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Tithing and the prosperity gospel -->

**Dated splits.** The Latter-day Saint law of tithing (1838) and its reinforcement under Lorenzo Snow (1899); the Adventist tithe system (1859, 1876); the Word of Faith movement's rise (Kenneth Hagin, 1960s--1970s) and the Assemblies of God's 1980 position paper against positive confession; the Senate Finance Committee's inquiry into prosperity ministries (2007--2011); the Nazarene *Manual*'s storehouse-tithing article (continuously since the early twentieth century).

**How to study it.** Trace the tithe through the Law (there were arguably three tithes) and then through the New Testament (it is named twice, both times in the Gospels), and read 2 Corinthians 8--9 as Paul's own instruction on giving; the prosperity question is answered by reading Job and the Psalms of lament alongside Deuteronomy 28, not by either alone.

## 30. Bible translations and KJV-onlyism

**What divides.** Which translation a church uses, on what textual basis (the Received Text behind the KJV, the critical texts behind nearly every modern version, the Septuagint for the Orthodox Old Testament, the Vulgate historically for Catholics), and whether one translation is uniquely authoritative. Full "KJV-onlyism" -- the view that the KJV is itself inspired or the only valid English Bible -- is held by no body profiled in this book, but a strong KJV preference marks several (Latter-day Saints officially; UPCI, COGIC and the Black Baptist bodies by custom), and the textual question runs through this book's whole Manuscript and Translation Differences catalog.

**The main positions.**
- *Official translation or approval required*: Catholic (ecclesiastical approval; the NABRE lectionary), Latter-day Saints (the KJV as the official English Bible), Jehovah's Witnesses (the *New World Translation*), Orthodox (the Septuagint as canonical text).
- *A preferred translation by publishing or liturgical practice*: LCMS (ESV), ELCA, Episcopal (authorized list), PC(USA), UCC (NRSV), UMC (CEB/NRSV), SBC (CSB), Assemblies of God (NIV in the *Fire Bible*), Seventh-day Adventists (NKJV in the *Andrews Study Bible*).
- *KJV by strong custom*: UPCI, COGIC, the National Baptist and AME bodies, some Churches of Christ.
- *No preference*: most congregational and evangelical bodies.

**Passages each side rests on.** Psalm 12:6--7 ("thou shalt preserve them"), Matthew 5:18 and 24:35 and 1 Peter 1:25 as the preservation texts of the KJV-only argument; Revelation 22:18--19 (whose own text is a variant -- "book of life" or "tree of life," Manuscript and Translation Differences, Revelation §1); Nehemiah 8:8 and Acts 2:6--11 for translation as such. The whole of the Manuscript and Translation Differences catalog is this chapter's evidence: the longer ending of Mark (Mark §1), the woman caught in adultery (John §1), the Comma Johanneum (1 John §1), Acts 8:37 (Acts §1) and 1 Timothy 3:16 (1 Timothy §1) are the entries over which the Received Text and critical-text positions most clearly diverge. The Introduction's "Background on Textual Transmission" and the Biblical Source Manuscripts section lay out the manuscript evidence.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Bible translations and KJV-onlyism -->

**Dated splits.** The Vulgate declared authentic at Trent (1546); the KJV (1611) and the Revised Version (1881--1885), with Burgon's defense of the Received Text (1881 onward); the RSV controversy (1952), including public burnings over Isaiah 7:14; the Latter-day Saint KJV edition (1979) and First Presidency statement (1992); the *New World Translation* (1950--1961); the TNIV and NIV 2011 gender-language controversy and the LCMS's and SBC's responses (2011); the ESV (2001) as the conservative Protestant alternative.

**How to study it.** Pick five variants from the Manuscript and Translation Differences catalog and read the manuscript evidence for each before reading any argument about translations; the question of which text to translate is prior to the question of how to translate it.

## 31. Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian

**What divides.** Whether husbands hold a God-given headship in the home that wives are to honor by submission (complementarianism), or whether marriage is a partnership of equals with mutual submission and no fixed roles (egalitarianism), and how Ephesians 5 and 1 Peter 3 are to be read. Chapter 8 covers ordination; this chapter is about the home, where the two positions divide even bodies that agree on office.

**The main positions.**
- *Complementarian by confession or official statement*: Southern Baptist (*BF&M* XVIII), LCMS, PCA, Christian and Missionary Alliance, Jehovah's Witnesses ("headship"), Latter-day Saints (fathers "preside," spouses "equal partners"), Orthodox (headship within mutual self-sacrifice), COGIC, UPCI, most Churches of Christ.
- *Egalitarian by official statement*: UMC, ELCA, Episcopal, PC(USA), UCC, GMC in effect, ABCUSA in policy.
- *Equal dignity and mutual self-giving, neither label adopted*: Catholic (*Mulieris Dignitatem*), Nazarene, Seventh-day Adventist ("mutual love, honor, respect").
- *Women in ministry affirmed, home roles traditional in practice*: Assemblies of God, AME, AME Zion, ABCUSA congregations.

**Passages each side rests on.** Ephesians 5:21--33 (whether "submitting to one another" in 5:21 governs 5:22), Colossians 3:18--19, 1 Peter 3:1--7, 1 Corinthians 11:3 ("the head of the wife is her husband"; 1COR-005), Genesis 2:18--24 and 3:16 ("he shall rule over you" -- curse or command?), 1 Timothy 2:12--14, Titus 2:3--5 for the complementarian reading; Galatians 3:28, Genesis 1:26--28 (both made in God's image and given dominion), Proverbs 31, Acts 18:26 (ACTS-008), Romans 16 and the mutuality of 1 Corinthians 7:3--5 for the egalitarian; Sirach 25:24 (SIR-002) and the Old Testament's own range on women (SIR-005, PROV-014); 1 Timothy 2:15's "saved through childbearing" (1TIM-002). The Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible's handling of the Greco-Roman household codes is the clearest study-Bible treatment of the context both sides must account for.

**Where the bodies in this book fall.**

<!-- POSITIONS: Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian -->

**Dated splits.** The Danvers Statement and the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood (1987--1988), which coined "complementarian"; Christians for Biblical Equality (1987) on the other side; the Southern Baptist 1998 family amendment; John Paul II's *Mulieris Dignitatem* (1988); the Latter-day Saint *Proclamation on the Family* (1995); the Christian and Missionary Alliance's 2023 decision.

**How to study it.** Read Ephesians 5:15--6:9 as one passage -- it begins with being filled with the Spirit and ends with slaves and masters -- and decide how 5:21 relates to 5:22 before anything else; then read 1 Corinthians 7:3--5 and ask whether the mutuality there is the exception or the rule.

**A closing note.** Thirty-one chapters of disagreement can leave the impression that Christians agree on nothing. The position tables tell a different story when read whole: on the Trinity, the resurrection, the authority of Scripture, the reservation of sex for marriage, the sinfulness of racism and drunkenness, and the rejection of the prosperity gospel, the bodies in this book are nearly unanimous, and most of the disagreements above are disagreements about how far a shared conviction reaches. The sections that precede this one -- the manuscripts, the translations, the two catalogs -- are offered as the equipment for reading the disputed passages for yourself. That, and not any of the positions recorded here, is what this book is for.
