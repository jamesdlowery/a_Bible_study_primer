# 1 John: Significant Textual Variants Across Ten Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE

1 John contains what may be the most historically consequential single textual variant discussed anywhere in this entire project — a case where the manuscript evidence is unusually one-sided, and where the story of how the passage entered the English Bible is as significant as the textual question itself.

---

## 1. 1 John 5:7–8 — the "Comma Johanneum" and the three heavenly witnesses

**The variant:** The KJV reads: "For there are three that bear record **in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth,** the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one." Strip out the bolded material (known as the "Comma Johanneum," Latin for roughly "the Johannine clause"), and the verse simply reads: "For there are three that testify: the Spirit and the water and the blood; and these three agree" — a statement about three earthly witnesses to Christ, with no direct mention of the Trinity at all.

This is, by a wide margin, one of the most decisively resolved textual questions in the New Testament. The heavenly-witnesses clause is **absent from every Greek manuscript** of 1 John prior to the 14th century, absent from every ancient version (Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopic, Arabic), and never quoted by any Greek-speaking church father — including theologians who were writing at length about the Trinity and who would have had every reason to cite such a direct proof-text had they known of it. The clause's earliest secure appearance is in Latin, not Greek: it seems to have originated as a fourth-century Latin marginal gloss or homiletical aside, allegorically connecting the Spirit/water/blood of the genuine text to the three persons of the Trinity, which then gradually worked its way from the margin into the actual text of some Old Latin and Vulgate copies. Only much later, apparently by back-translation from Latin into Greek, does it start appearing in a small handful of very late Greek manuscripts (the earliest by most accounts from the 14th–15th century).

The clause's path into English Bibles runs through a specific and well-documented episode: Erasmus, compiling the first published Greek New Testament in 1516, omitted the clause because he could find it in no Greek manuscript. Facing criticism for the omission, he reportedly indicated he would include it if a single Greek manuscript containing it could be produced (the precise nature of any such "promise" is itself disputed by historians of Erasmus, but the outcome is not: a manuscript surfaced — Codex Montfortianus, apparently produced specifically to meet the challenge, likely by back-translating the clause from Latin into Greek) — and Erasmus, true to his word, included the clause in his third edition (1522), reportedly with his own footnote expressing doubt about its authenticity. From there it entered the Textus Receptus and, via that route, the KJV.

**Why it matters:** If genuine, this would be the single most direct and explicit statement of Trinitarian doctrine anywhere in the Bible — naming Father, Word, and Holy Spirit together as one in a single sentence. Its near-total absence from the Greek manuscript tradition, and total absence from patristic citation during the very centuries when Trinitarian doctrine was most fiercely debated and defended, is considered very strong evidence against its originality by virtually all textual scholars today, including many who are otherwise strongly committed to traditional Trinitarian theology — the doctrine of the Trinity itself does not depend on this verse, since it's affirmed on other, textually secure grounds throughout the New Testament (including, some argue, in the surviving, undisputed portion of this very passage's logic).

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the full Comma Johanneum:** KJV and NKJV (NKJV includes a footnote acknowledging the clause's absence from most Greek manuscripts, while still printing it as the main text).
- **Omit the clause, following the overwhelming manuscript evidence, with a footnote explaining its absence and Latin origin:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, and NLT.
- **AMP** renders the shorter reading in the main text.
- **RSV2CE** follows the same pattern as the other modern critical-text translations, omitting the clause with an explanatory note.

---

## Summary observations

- 1 John 5:7–8 stands out even within this NT-wide project as the case with the most lopsided manuscript evidence of any variant covered — essentially the entire Greek tradition on one side, and a documented, traceable, centuries-later Latin-to-Greek insertion on the other.
- It's also a rare case in this whole project where we can trace, in real historical detail, exactly how and why a specific reading entered a specific English translation tradition (via Erasmus and the pressures on his 1522 edition) — most other variants in this project are questions of ancient scribal transmission, not 16th-century publishing history.
