# Job: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

---

## Job 13:15 — "though he slay me, yet will I trust him," or "I have no hope"?

**The variant:** This is a Kethiv/Qere case. As written (Kethiv), the Hebrew reads *lo* spelled with an aleph (לא), "not" — giving "though he slay me, I have no hope." The traditional marginal correction (Qere) has *lo* spelled with a vav (לו), "in him" — giving "though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." The two words are pronounced identically and differ by one easily-confused letter.

**How the translations render it:**
- **"I will hope/trust in him" (following the Qere), in the main text:** KJV ("yet will I trust in him"), NKJV ("yet I will trust Him"), NASB ("I will hope in Him"), AMP ("yet will I trust Him"), and also ESV ("I will hope in him," with a footnote noting the alternative "I have no hope") and NIV and CSB (each with a footnote giving "I have no hope" as the Hebrew's literal alternative). **Douay-Rheims and Webster's Bible** are both confirmed directly in this camp as well, along with **Darby** ("yet would I trust in him"), **the ERV** (Easy-to-Read Version: "I will continue to trust God even if he kills me"), **the BSB** ("I will hope in Him"), and **the NET Bible** ("I will hope in him"). Notably, **the NABRE is also confirmed directly in this camp** ("I will wait for him"), with its own footnote explicitly acknowledging that "many translations adopt the Ketib reading, 'I have no hope'" -- meaning the NABRE's own editors were aware of, and specifically declined, the alternative reading. **AKJV** would be expected to match, given its explicit KJV-preservation method, though not separately confirmed. **The CPDV is now confirmed directly**, matching the expectation ("if he would kill me, I will hope in him"). Smith's Literal Translation offers a genuinely distinct grammatical solution, confirmed directly, that lands in this same general camp by a different route: "If he shall slay me shall I not hope? only I will prove my ways to his face" -- SLT's characteristic extreme literalism preserves the Kethiv's own written "not" (rather than silently adopting the Qere's "in him"), but converts the clause into a rhetorical question expecting the answer "yes, I will," which produces essentially the same affirmative, trusting sense as the Qere-following translations above, despite representing the Hebrew text's literal letters rather than its traditional marginal correction.
- **A reading closer to "I have no hope" (following the Kethiv):** confirmed directly, **the RSV itself** ("Behold, he will slay me; I have no hope; yet I will defend my ways to his face"), and RSV2CE most likely follows that same family reading, though its specific printing is worth checking directly. **ASV, the WEB, and YLT** are all confirmed directly in this camp as well, each rendering some form of "I have no hope" / "I wait not." The **NRSV-CE** would be expected to follow the same RSV-family reading, given the RSV's own confirmed reading here, though not separately confirmed.
- **NLT** takes a third path rather than a clean rendering of either Kethiv or Qere: "God might kill me, but I have no other hope" — a paraphrase that leans toward the despairing sense but is its own construction, not simply "I have no hope." **CJB** similarly takes its own distinct path: "I don't expect more" — closer in overall sense to the "no hope" camp, but its own specific wording rather than a direct match to either the RSV tradition or the Kethiv text.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** LSB and TRB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse.

**The difference in meaning:** One reading is among the most quoted expressions of faith-under-suffering in the Bible; the other has Job voicing raw despair while still defiantly planning to argue his case, but explicitly without hope.
