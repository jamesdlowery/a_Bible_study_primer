# Mark: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

As explained in the companion Matthew document, the New Testament's textual situation differs fundamentally from the Old Testament's: thousands of Greek manuscripts survive, and translations divide mainly between those based on the later Textus Receptus (KJV, NKJV) and those based on a modern critical text weighted toward the earliest manuscripts (most others), with Vulgate-tradition translations (Douay-Rheims, CPDV) forming a third lineage that agrees with each side at different points depending on what Jerome's own source manuscripts contained. Mark contains what is probably the single most significant textual question in the entire New Testament.

---

## 1. Mark 16:9–20 — how does Mark's Gospel actually end?

**The variant:** This is the most famous and most extensively studied textual variant in the New Testament. As printed in most Bibles, Mark ends with the risen Jesus appearing to Mary Magdalene and the disciples, commissioning them, and ascending (16:9–20) — including the well-known lines about believers "speak[ing] in new tongues," picking up serpents, and being unharmed by poison (16:17–18). But the two oldest complete Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus (both 4th century), both end the Gospel abruptly at 16:8, with the women fleeing the empty tomb "afraid" — a strikingly unresolved ending with no resurrection appearance narrated at all. Vaticanus even leaves a blank column at exactly this point, unusual for that manuscript, which some argue shows its scribe was aware of a longer ending he chose not to include (others argue the blank space is unrelated, reserved instead for how the page layout worked out). Early church historians Eusebius and Jerome (4th–5th century) both explicitly stated that the longer ending was absent from "almost all" the Greek manuscripts known to them at the time — direct ancient testimony that this was recognized as an unsettled question even in antiquity, not just a modern discovery. On the other hand, the longer ending is attested in the vast majority (some estimates put it around 99%) of the roughly 1,700 surviving Greek manuscripts of Mark, and appears to be known to writers as early as the 2nd century (Irenaeus quotes Mark 16:19 by name around 180 AD, and there are possible allusions in Justin Martyr and Tatian).

Beyond these two options, the Greek manuscript tradition actually preserves **four different endings** to Mark: (1) ending at 16:8; (2) the familiar Longer Ending (16:9–20); (3) a rarely-attested "Shorter Ending" (a brief, different paragraph found in only a handful of manuscripts, some of which include both the Shorter and Longer endings together); and (4) the Longer Ending with an additional passage inserted after 16:14, called the "Freer Logion" (found in a single manuscript, Codex Washingtonianus), in which the disciples' unbelief is blamed on Satan.

**Why it matters:** Beyond the question of how the book ends, verses 17–18 (about handling serpents and drinking poison without harm) have had real practical consequences in some Christian communities that treat them as calling for literal snake-handling in worship — making the passage's textual status considerably higher-stakes than most.

**A clarification before the split:** unlike most entries in this project, the actual wording of Mark 16:9–20 is essentially the same across the translations that include it at all — none of them prints substantially different words for these twelve verses. What differs is whether the passage is presented as ordinary, unflagged text or set off as textually uncertain. I'm still calling it out here, given how significant and well-known this variant is, but it's a presence/framing difference rather than a wording-changes-the-meaning difference within the passage itself.

**Translations — how the full set of 27 handle this:**

KJV, NKJV, ASV, WEB, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, and the AKJV would be expected to print the passage as ordinary, unflagged text, consistent with translations that either predate modern critical-text apparatus entirely or (for AKJV/WBT) explicitly preserve the KJV's underlying text without engaging that apparatus at all -- though only KJV and NKJV have been directly confirmed for this specific passage; the others are reasoned expectations based on stated translation method, not independently checked.

ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, and LSB set the passage off with brackets or a footnote noting its absence from the earliest manuscripts. NLT includes it with an explanatory footnote. RSV2CE follows the general modern-critical-text pattern of flagging it. The original 1952 RSV famously relegated the entire passage to a footnote rather than the main text -- a decision significant enough that it's already discussed in this book's own RSV/ESV history entry, and one that drew real public controversy at the time. The NRSV-CE, being a further revision in the same critical tradition as the RSV, would be expected to flag the passage similarly, though this was not separately confirmed.

**The Douay-Rheims Bible and the Catholic Public Domain Version, both translated from the Latin Vulgate, are confirmed directly printing the passage as ordinary, unflagged text** -- the Vulgate has always contained the longer ending (unlike, for instance, the Matthew 6:13 doxology discussed in the companion Matthew document, which the Vulgate never included), and both translations' own published text bears this out directly rather than merely being a reasoned expectation.

**Genuinely unconfirmed, and not safely predictable from translation family alone:** the NABRE, the ERV, the NET Bible, the BSB, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific passage. This entry should not guess at any of these — some (like the NET Bible, known for extensive translator notes on exactly this kind of textual question) could plausibly go either way, and asserting a prediction here would be exactly the kind of unverified claim this book tries not to make.

## 2. Mark 9:29 — "this kind can come forth by nothing, but by prayer and fasting"

**The variant:** Explaining the disciples' failed exorcism, the majority of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) have Jesus answer that this kind of spirit can only be driven out "by prayer and fasting." The earliest manuscripts (including Sinaiticus and the original hand of Vaticanus) instead read simply "by prayer," without "and fasting." Most textual scholars regard "and fasting" as a later addition, reflecting the early church's growing emphasis on fasting as a spiritual discipline — the same dynamic behind the closely related variant at Matthew 17:21, addressed in the companion Matthew document.

**Why it matters:** As with the Matthew parallel, the question is how much weight this specific pairing of prayer with fasting should carry as Jesus' own words describing what's needed for this kind of spiritual authority.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include "and fasting":** KJV, NKJV. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would be expected to include it as well, on the same Textus-Receptus-preservation grounds discussed in the companion Matthew entries. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible includes "and fasting" as well ("This kind can go out by nothing, but by prayer and fasting"), per multiple independent sources -- the tenth confirmed instance in this project's set of disputed-verse entries (see the companion Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Acts, Romans, and Colossians entries elsewhere in this document set) of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, material absent from the earliest manuscripts.
- **Read "prayer" alone, typically with a footnote noting that some manuscripts add "and fasting":** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT.
- **Given this now ten-times-confirmed Douay-Rheims pattern, the CPDV** would be expected to include "and fasting" as well, reversing this entry's prior expectation that it would side with the shorter reading.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** AMP and RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with ASV, WEB, Darby, Smith's Literal Translation, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, the ERV, the NET Bible, the BSB, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse.

## 3. Mark 11:26 — "if ye do not forgive, neither will your Father forgive"

**The variant:** After teaching on forgiveness in prayer, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) add: "But if ye do not forgive, neither will your Father which is in heaven forgive your trespasses." The verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal harmonization, copied in from the very similar statement at Matthew 6:15, which is undisputed there.

**Why it matters:** The teaching itself is undisputed as Matthew's own wording — the question here is narrower, whether Mark's Gospel also states it at this point, or whether the verse belongs only to Matthew.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible includes this verse unconditionally as well ("But if you do not forgive, neither will your Father in heaven forgive your transgressions"), with no bracketing -- consistent with the same pattern already documented at the companion Matthew 18:11 entry, where Douay-Rheims likewise included a similarly disputed verse rather than following the modern critical-text pattern of omission. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would again be expected to include it, on the same grounds as the entry above. **Given this confirmed pattern, the CPDV** would now be expected to include this verse as well, reversing this entry's prior expectation.
- **Omit it from the main text, typically with a footnote noting its absence from the earliest manuscripts and the parallel at Matthew 6:15:** ESV, NIV, CSB. **NASB** prints the verse in brackets rather than omitting it outright ("[But if you do not forgive, neither will your Father who is in heaven forgive your transgressions.]"), the same middle position documented at the companion Matthew 18:11 entry; **LSB and NLT** would be expected to follow either the clean-omission or bracketed pattern, though neither was separately confirmed for this specific verse.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** AMP and RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with ASV, WEB, Darby, Smith's Literal Translation, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, the ERV, the NET Bible, the BSB, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse.

## 4. Mark 15:28 — "he was numbered with the transgressors"

**The variant:** Describing the two criminals crucified alongside Jesus, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) add a note that this fulfilled Scripture: "And the scripture was fulfilled, which saith, And he was numbered with the transgressors" (quoting Isaiah 53:12). The verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal insertion drawn from the closely parallel statement at Luke 22:37, where Jesus himself quotes the same line from Isaiah in an undisputed context.

**Why it matters:** The Isaiah 53:12 connection to Jesus' crucifixion is undisputed as Luke's own wording — the question here is whether Mark's Gospel also draws out this explicit fulfillment citation at this point, or whether it belongs only to Luke's account.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible includes this verse unconditionally as well, though with its own distinct wording ("And the scripture was fulfilled, which saith: And with the wicked he was reputed") -- the third time in this document's set of disputed-verse entries that Douay-Rheims has been found to include rather than omit a verse absent from the earliest manuscripts (see also the companion Matthew 18:11 entry and Mark 11:26 above). Also confirmed directly in unqualified inclusion: **Webster's Bible** and **YLT** (the latter's own text showing an unusual double-bracket notation around a portion of its wording, though the verse itself is present rather than omitted). **The AKJV** would again be expected to include it, on the same grounds as the entries above. **Given this now-repeated pattern, the CPDV** would be expected to include this verse as well, reversing this entry's prior expectation.
- **Omit it from the main text, typically with a footnote noting its absence from the earliest manuscripts and the parallel at Luke 22:37:** ESV, NIV, CSB. **Darby and AMP** both print the verse in brackets rather than omitting it outright (Darby: "[And the scripture was fulfilled which says, And he was reckoned with the lawless.]"; AMP: "[And the Scripture was fulfilled which says, 'He was counted with the transgressors.']"), the same middle position documented at the companion Matthew 18:11 and Mark 11:26 entries; **NASB, LSB, and NLT** would be expected to follow either the clean-omission or bracketed pattern, though none was separately confirmed for this specific verse.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with ASV, WEB, Smith's Literal Translation, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, the ERV, the NET Bible, the BSB, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse.

## Summary observations

- Mark 16:9–20 is the paradigm case for the whole New Testament portion of this project: a passage overwhelmingly attested in the manuscript tradition by sheer count, but consistently absent from the very oldest and (in the judgment of most modern textual scholars) most reliable witnesses — and one where ancient testimony (Eusebius, Jerome) confirms the debate is not a modern invention.
- Worth noting, as several scholars point out: even if these verses are a later addition, nothing essential to Christian belief hinges on them uniquely — the resurrection itself is independently and abundantly attested elsewhere in Matthew, Luke, John, and Paul's letters.
- Mark 9:29, 11:26, and 15:28 all follow the same predictable Textus-Receptus-vs-critical-text pattern established in Matthew, with the KJV-preservation and Vulgate-tradition translations expected to fall on opposite sides for the reasons discussed there.
