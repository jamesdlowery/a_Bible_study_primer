# Deuteronomy: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

## Deuteronomy 32:8 — "sons of Israel," or "sons of God"?

**The variant:** Describing how God divided the nations among the peoples of the earth, the Masoretic Text says he set boundaries "according to the number of the **sons of Israel**." A Dead Sea Scrolls fragment of Deuteronomy (4QDeut-j) and the Septuagint instead read "according to the number of the **sons of God**" (understood as divine/angelic beings assigned to oversee the nations) — a Hebrew consonant-level difference (*bene yisra'el* vs. *bene elohim*) that most textual scholars now judge was the original wording, later altered toward "sons of Israel" for theological reasons (avoiding the implication of a divine council of lesser heavenly beings).

**How the translations render it:**
- **"Sons of Israel" (following MT):** KJV ("children of Israel"), NKJV ("sons of Israel"), NIV ("sons of Israel"), NASB ("sons of Israel"), AMP ("sons of Israel"), and CSB ("the people of Israel" in its main text — despite CSB's NT following the critical text, its OT here stays with the MT rather than the DSS/LXX reading). **Douay-Rheims** ("the children of Israel") is confirmed directly in this camp as well, consistent with both its 1610 and 1950 editions -- notably, this places Douay-Rheims with the Masoretic reading here despite its Vulgate basis, since Jerome's own Vulgate followed the Hebrew text available to him at this point rather than the Greek (confirmed directly: the Latin Vulgate itself reads "iuxta numerum filiorum Israhel," "according to the number of the sons of Israel"). Also confirmed directly in this camp: **the ASV** ("the children of men... the children of Israel"), **YLT** ("the sons of Israel"), **Darby** ("the children of Israel"), **the WEB** (World English Bible: "the children of Israel"), **Webster's Bible** ("the children of Israel," matching KJV's phrasing), and **the CJB** ("he assigned the boundaries of peoples according to Isra'el's population"). **The CPDV** can be stated with high confidence to fall in this same camp, given the Vulgate's own confirmed "filiorum Israhel" wording quoted above, though this specific translation's own text was not separately located in this research pass.
- **"Sons of God" / a heavenly-court reading (following the DSS/LXX):** ESV ("sons of God"), LSB ("sons of Israel" in main text with a footnote reading "Gr angels of God; DSS number of the sons of God" -- confirmed directly via the Legacy Standard Bible's own published text, verifying the original entry's description of LSB as a partial exception was accurate), NLT ("divine beings" — a periphrastic rendering of the same underlying reading), RSV2CE ("sons of God," consistent with the RSV textual tradition it derives from, which adopted this reading early). **RSV** and the **BSB** are both confirmed directly in this camp as well ("sons of God" in both cases). Also confirmed directly in this camp: **the NRSV** ("the gods," and by direct extension **the NRSV-CE**), **the NABRE** ("the divine beings"), and **the NET Bible** ("the heavenly assembly," with an extensive translator's note explaining the textual basis for this reading in detail).
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** Smith's Literal Translation, the AKJV, the ERV, and TRB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse, despite a further dedicated search in a follow-up research pass.

**The difference in meaning:** "Sons of Israel" makes the verse about God organizing the nations with Israel's future population in view — a statement centered on Israel alone. "Sons of God" instead depicts a scene where God assigns each nation to the oversight of a member of his heavenly court, with Israel kept for himself directly (as verse 9 goes on to say) — a strikingly different picture involving a divine council governing the nations, more in line with imagery found elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible (e.g., Job 1–2, Psalm 82) and in early Jewish literature. This is generally regarded as the single most significant Dead-Sea-Scrolls-confirmed variant in the entire Old Testament.

## Deuteronomy 32:43 — does the Song of Moses end with a call for angels to worship?

**The variant:** MT's ending to the Song of Moses is comparatively short. A Dead Sea Scrolls fragment (4QDeut-q) and the Septuagint both preserve additional lines not in MT, including "let all the angels of God worship him" — a line directly quoted in Hebrews 1:6 in the New Testament.

**How the translations render it:**
- **Follow the shorter MT text, without the angels line:** KJV, NKJV, NASB, NIV. Also confirmed directly using this same shorter form: **the Douay-Rheims Bible** ("Rejoice, O ye nations, with his people: For he will avenge the blood of his servants... And will make expiation for his land, for his people"), **the BSB** ("Shout for joy, ye nations, with his people... And maketh atonement for his land, for his people"), and **Darby** ("Praise his people, ye nations, for he will revenge the blood of his servants..."). Douay-Rheims' inclusion in this shorter-form camp is consistent with, rather than an exception to, the general pattern of Douay-Rheims including disputed New Testament material documented elsewhere in this project: this particular expansion is attested only in the Dead Sea Scrolls and Septuagint, not in the Latin Vulgate, so Douay-Rheims (translating the Vulgate) would not be expected to contain it in the first place, unlike the New-Testament-era scribal insertions discussed in this project's Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Acts, and other entries, many of which do appear in the Vulgate.
- **Include the additional line, based on the DSS/LXX:** ESV ("rejoice with him, O heavens; bow down to him, all gods" — a related but not identical expansion; ESV's translators note the Hebrew scroll and Greek support), NLT (includes an expanded ending with a footnote explaining the DSS/Greek basis), and confirmed directly, **LSB** ("Rejoice, O heavens, with Him, and let all God's angels worship Him"), joining ESV and NLT in this camp.
- **The CPDV**, given Douay-Rheims' confirmed shorter reading here and the shared absence of this material from the Vulgate itself, would be expected to follow the same shorter-form reading.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** CSB, AMP, RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with ASV, WEB, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, TRB, and CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse. This entry proved unusually difficult to source cleanly in an earlier research pass, given several aggregator sites' conflicting or seemingly mislabeled quotes; the three new confirmations above came from a single, more reliably and consistently labeled source, but the remainder is still left flagged honestly as unconfirmed -- if it matters for your purposes, it's worth checking your own copies directly.

**The difference in meaning:** Without the addition, the Song of Moses ends focused on God's vindication of his people and vengeance on his adversaries. With it, the ending broadens into a cosmic scene of angelic/heavenly worship — directly relevant to how Hebrews 1:6 is read as drawing on an Old Testament text, since the quoted line only exists in the longer reading, not in MT.

## Side note: a related, lower-stakes numeral variant

- Deuteronomy contains several places (e.g., troop counts, boundary measurements) where Samaritan Pentateuch or LXX numbers differ slightly from MT without producing a documented split among the tracked translations; these are not itemized here since none rises to a confirmed meaning-changing difference among them.

## Side note: the Samaritan Pentateuch's Gerizim commandment

- After the traditional tenth commandment, the Samaritan Pentateuch inserts a lengthy passage -- drawn mainly from material found elsewhere in this book, in chapters 27 and 11 -- commanding that an altar be built on Mount Gerizim and that sacrifice be offered there, effectively making this a tenth commandment in the Samaritan tradition and Gerizim, not Jerusalem, the uniquely chosen sanctuary from the very giving of the Law. Pre-Samaritan Dead Sea Scrolls fragments (4QpaleoExodᵐ, 4QNumᵇ) already show many of the same expansions and harmonizations, confirming these were genuine, circulating ancient Palestinian textual features that the Samaritan community later adopted and intensified, not an invention original to the Samaritans themselves. None of the 27 tracked translations, all working from the Masoretic Hebrew tradition rather than the Samaritan Pentateuch, includes this material.
