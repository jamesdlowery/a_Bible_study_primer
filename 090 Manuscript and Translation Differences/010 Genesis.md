# Genesis: Textual and Translation Differences

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, DR, CPDV, Darby, YLT, SLT, WBT, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

This chapter lists cases where the tracked translations actually render a verse with different words that change its meaning -- not just stylistic differences. Where a real manuscript variant exists but the translations happen to agree on the same reading, that's noted separately at the end as a side note, since it doesn't create a difference among them but is still useful background.

---

## Genesis 49:10 — does the scepter depart "until Shiloh comes," or "until tribute is brought to him"?

**The variant:** The Hebrew here (*'ad ki-yavo shiloh*) is genuinely obscure — it can be read as containing a proper name, "Shiloh" (understood by much of Jewish and Christian tradition as a title for the Messiah), or as a phrase meaning roughly "until he comes to whom it belongs" or "until tribute/what is his comes to him," depending on how the consonants are divided and vocalized. This isn't primarily a manuscript-vs-manuscript variant (the Masoretic consonantal text is stable) — it's a case where the same written Hebrew genuinely supports more than one reading, and ancient versions themselves split on it (the Septuagint and Peshitta lean toward "until what belongs to him comes," while the Vulgate and much later Jewish tradition read "Shiloh" as a name).

**How the translations render it:**
- **"Until Shiloh comes" (treating Shiloh as a name/title, in the main text):** KJV ("until Shiloh come"), NKJV ("until Shiloh comes"), AMP ("until Shiloh comes, to whom it belongs" — hedging both readings together), NASB, and LSB. NASB's main text still reads "until Shiloh comes" in both the 1995 and 2020 editions — the 2020 revision did not drop "Shiloh" — with the non-proper-name reading given as a footnote alternative rather than replacing it in the text. LSB likewise keeps "until Shiloh comes" in its main text, consistent with its general practice of tracking NASB's wording. **ASV and Darby** also render "until Shiloh come," confirmed directly against their own text. **The BSB is also confirmed directly in this camp** ("until Shiloh comes and the allegiance of the nations is his"). **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("till that Shiloh shall come: and to him the obedience of the nations"). **Webster's Bible is now confirmed directly** in this camp ("until Shiloh shall come: and to him shall be the gathering of the people"), matching KJV's wording almost exactly. **The AKJV is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("until Shiloh come; and to him shall the gathering of the people be"), matching KJV's wording exactly.
- **"Until he comes to whom it belongs" / "until tribute is brought to him" (reading it as a description, not a name):** ESV ("until tribute comes to him"), CSB ("until the one it belongs to comes"), NIV ("until he to whom it belongs shall come"), NLT ("until the coming of the one to whom it belongs"), and RSV2CE ("until he comes to whom it belongs"). **RSV** renders the same way ("until he comes to whom it belongs"), confirmed directly, and the **NRSV-CE** would be expected to follow this same RSV-tradition reading, though not separately confirmed. **CJB** also lands in this camp: "until he comes to whom [obedience] belongs." Also confirmed directly in this camp: **the NET Bible** ("until he comes to whom it belongs") and **the WEB** (World English Bible: "until he comes to whom it belongs"). **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text reads "Until tribute comes to him," with the footnote explaining "this translation is based on a slight change in the Hebrew text, which, as it stands, would seem to mean, 'until he comes to Shiloh.'"
- **A third, distinct reading — "until he comes who is to be sent":** the **Douay-Rheims Bible** renders "till he come that is to be sent," following the Latin Vulgate's own interpretive tradition (*qui mittendus est*) rather than either the "Shiloh" or "tribute" camps. **The Catholic Public Domain Version is now confirmed directly** in this same camp ("until he who will be sent arrives, and he will be the expectation of Gentiles"), matching this Vulgate-derived reading as expected.
- **A fourth, genuinely distinct paraphrase, not previously documented:** **the ERV** (Easy-to-Read Version) is confirmed directly rendering this verse without either "Shiloh" as a name or a clean "tribute"-style description, instead paraphrasing: "The sign that his family rules will not leave his family before the real king comes" -- a periphrastic rendering that names neither "Shiloh" nor uses "tribute"/"belongs to him" language directly.
- **A fifth, genuinely distinct rendering, confirmed directly and not previously documented for this entry:** **Young's Literal Translation** renders "Till his Seed come" -- avoiding "Shiloh" as a proper name (aligning in substance with the second camp's non-proper-name reading) but using its own distinct "Seed" language rather than "tribute" or "he to whom it belongs," a wording not matching any of the four camps above.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse, despite dedicated searches across multiple research passes.

**The difference in meaning:** If "Shiloh" is a name/title, the verse reads as a specific, personal messianic prediction — a named or titled figure to whom the scepter passes. If it's read as "until tribute/what belongs to him comes," the verse is a more general statement about the permanence of Judah's rule until it receives its due — still widely read as messianic in its ultimate fulfillment, but without a specific name embedded in the text. The Douay-Rheims/Vulgate reading ("he who is to be sent") represents yet a third interpretive tradition distinct from both. This is a case where the wording difference has had real downstream interpretive consequences for centuries of messianic prophecy discussion.

## Genesis 4:8 — does Cain say anything to Abel before killing him?

**The variant:** MT's text of Cain's words to Abel breaks off mid-sentence ("Cain said to his brother Abel..." with no reported speech at all in the strict Hebrew consonantal text). The Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch, Vulgate, and Peshitta all supply "Let us go out to the field," giving Cain an actual line of dialogue before the murder.

**How the translations render it:**
- **Supply "Let us go out to the field" (following the versions):** NIV, NLT, and CSB include the phrase in their main text. **YLT** ("Let us go into the field") and the **Douay-Rheims Bible** ("Let us go forth abroad") also supply it, confirmed directly against their own text — consistent with Douay-Rheims' Vulgate basis, which includes the line. Also confirmed directly supplying the phrase: **the WEB** ("Let's go into the field"), **the NET Bible** ("Let's go out to the field"), **the BSB** ("Let us go out to the field"), **the NABRE** ("Cain said to his brother Abel, 'Let us go out in the field,'" confirmed directly via its official USCCB text, with an accompanying footnote explaining the invitation's purpose: "to avoid detection"), and **the ERV** (Easy-to-Read Version: "Cain said to his brother Abel, 'Let's go out to the field.'"). **The CPDV is also now confirmed directly**, checked against its own actual text rather than merely inferred: "And Cain said to his brother Abel, 'Let us go outside.'" **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this camp, checked against multiple independent sources: "Cain said to Abel his brother, 'Let us go out to the field.'" **RSV2CE is also confirmed directly** in this same camp, checked against its own published text: "Cain said to Abel his brother, 'Let us go out to the field.'" **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: "Cain said to his brother Abel, 'Let us go out to the field.'"
- **Omit it, following the bare MT (which breaks off without reported speech):** KJV and NKJV omit it; ESV and NASB also omit it from their main text (ESV notes the versions' reading in a footnote). LSB, tracking NASB, also omits it. Also confirmed directly omitting it: **Webster's Bible** ("And Cain talked with Abel his brother"), **Darby** ("And Cain spoke to Abel his brother"), and **the CJB** ("Kayin had words with Hevel his brother"). **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("And Cain will speak to Abel his brother"), matching the same short, unsupplemented reading. **The AKJV is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("And Cain talked with Abel his brother"), matching KJV's own wording exactly, as expected given its preservation method. **The ASV is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("And Cain told Abel his brother"), its own distinct verb choice but the same short, unsupplemented reading. **AMP is also confirmed directly** in this camp, checked against multiple independent sources: "Cain talked with Abel his brother [about what God had said]" -- worth noting, the older AMPC (Classic Amplified) edition instead reads "Let us go out to the field," supplying the phrase; the current AMP tracked by this project has since moved to the shorter, unsupplemented reading.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse.

**The difference in meaning:** With the supplied line, Cain lures Abel out with a specific pretext, making the murder premeditated in an explicit, stated way. Without it, the Hebrew's abruptness itself becomes part of the narrative effect — Cain's words trail off and the text jumps straight to the field, which many readers take as its own kind of ominous silence.

## Side notes: cases where the tracked translations agree, but a real variant exists elsewhere in the manuscript tradition

- **Genesis 2:2** — MT reads God finished his work on the "seventh" day; the Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch, and Peshitta all read "sixth" instead (avoiding the implication that God was still "working" on the seventh/rest day). This is a case of the ancient versions themselves differing from MT, not a divide among English translations — the "sixth day" reading does not appear to be adopted by any mainstream English translation. Confirmed directly for Douay-Rheims and RSV, both of which follow MT's "seventh," consistent with every other translation checked in this project to date; no English translation was found rendering "sixth."
- **Genesis 46:27 / Exodus 1:5** — MT gives the number of Jacob's household who went to Egypt as "seventy"; the Septuagint and a Dead Sea Scrolls fragment give "seventy-five" (the number quoted in Acts 7:14). This is discussed at the relevant Exodus entry; no translation checked in this project renders "seventy-five" in the main text of either passage.
- **Genesis 47:31** — MT has Jacob worshiping "leaning on the head of his bed"; the Septuagint reads "staff" instead of "bed" (a one-letter/vowel-pointing difference in Hebrew), which is the version quoted in Hebrews 11:21. No translation checked in this project departs from MT's "bed" in this verse's main text.
