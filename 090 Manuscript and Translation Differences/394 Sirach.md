# Sirach (Ecclesiasticus): Textual and Translation Differences

**Comparison translations:** RSV2CE, NABRE, DR, CPDV, NRSV.

A genuine verse-level example does turn up with more targeted searching, resolving what an earlier research pass had left as an open gap.

## Sirach 33:1 (Douay-Rheims numbering; Ecclesiasticus 33:1 in the same Vulgate-based system) -- singular or plural "evil"?

**The variant:** Describing the protection afforded to one who fears the Lord, the Greek-based textual tradition uses the singular "evil," while the Vulgate-based tradition (and so Douay-Rheims, translating it) uses the plural "evils," appearing twice in the same verse. This is a genuine, specific wording difference between the two textual families tracked throughout this document, not merely a stylistic variation.

**How the translations render it:** **NABRE**, translating primarily from the Greek, reads "No evil will ever come to a person who fears the Lord; however often danger comes, the Lord will come to the rescue" -- singular "evil," matching the Greek textual tradition. **Confirmed directly, the Douay-Rheims Bible** instead reads "No evils shall happen to him that feareth the Lord, but in temptation God will keep him and deliver him from evils" -- plural "evils," used twice, matching the Vulgate's own distinct wording at this verse. **RSV2CE**, also translating primarily from the Greek like NABRE, would be expected to follow the same singular-"evil" pattern, though this was not separately confirmed against its own text for this specific verse. **The CPDV**, sharing Douay-Rheims' Vulgate source, would be expected to follow Douay-Rheims into the plural-"evils" reading, though this was not separately confirmed either.

**The difference in meaning:** The difference is narrow rather than doctrinally significant -- both readings affirm that the person who fears the Lord will be protected and delivered -- but it is a genuine, verse-level wording divergence of the same general kind documented at Tobit 6:2 and Judith 2:17/2:27 above, arising from the same underlying Greek-versus-Vulgate textual split that recurs throughout this book.

## Background: why Sirach translations are hard to compare verse-by-verse at all

Sirach was written in Hebrew around 180 BC and translated into Greek by the author's grandson around 132 BC. Roughly two-thirds of the Hebrew original has since been recovered (Cairo Geniza fragments, plus Masada and Qumran fragments), alongside the complete Greek translation, which itself exists in a shorter and a longer ("GII," roughly 200 extra lines) recension. On top of this, **Sirach's verse numbering is notoriously inconsistent across editions** — the Hebrew, the shorter Greek, and the Latin Vulgate traditions diverge from each other in versification at multiple points, so the same passage can carry different verse numbers depending on which translation and numbering convention is being used. This makes a simple "verse X reads differently across translations" comparison harder to do reliably for Sirach than for most other books in this project without checking each candidate verse very carefully against each translation's specific numbering scheme.

RSV2CE and NABRE both translate primarily from the Greek, consulting the Hebrew fragments where available, so the two are likely to agree closely in most places — Douay-Rheims, translated from the Vulgate, is the more likely source of a genuine divergence, following the general pattern seen in Tobit and Judith, and now confirmed directly at 33:1 above. The CPDV, sharing that same Vulgate source, would be expected to pair with Douay-Rheims for the same reason, though this was not separately confirmed.
