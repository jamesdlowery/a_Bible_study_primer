# John: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

As explained in the companion Matthew document, New Testament translations divide mainly between those based on the later Textus Receptus (KJV, NKJV) and those based on a modern critical text weighted toward the earliest manuscripts (most others). John contains the second most famous textual variant in the whole New Testament, after Mark's ending.

---

## 1. John 7:53–8:11 — the woman caught in adultery

**The variant:** This beloved story — Jesus confronted with a woman accused of adultery, responding "let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone," and ultimately telling her "neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" — is entirely absent from the earliest surviving manuscripts of John, including two of the oldest and most important papyri (P66 and P75, both roughly 2nd–3rd century), as well as Codex Vaticanus and the original hand of Codex Sinaiticus. It's also absent from the earliest commentators on John (including Origen, Chrysostom, and Cyril of Alexandria, none of whom shows any awareness of it in their verse-by-verse treatments of this section). Where it does appear in the manuscript tradition, it shows unusual behavior for a genuine part of the original text: some manuscripts place it not here but after John 7:36, after John 21:25 (the very end of the Gospel), or even inserted into the Gospel of Luke instead (after Luke 21:38 or 24:53) — a pattern of "floating" to different locations that textual critics generally take as a strong sign of a later addition to the text, since a passage original to a book from its composition doesn't typically wander between different locations across the manuscript tradition. The story is also written in a noticeably different Greek style from the rest of John (with a number of words and constructions that don't appear elsewhere in this Gospel).

That said, the case for later insertion isn't airtight: the story is attested quite early in substance — the 2nd-century writer Papias reportedly knew a very similar account (as reported by the church historian Eusebius), and it appears in the Old Latin tradition and in the influential (though textually unusual) Codex Bezae by the 5th century, showing it was in circulation, in some form, reasonably early — just apparently not as part of John's Gospel from the start.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the most beloved and frequently cited episodes in all four Gospels, central to how many Christians think about mercy and non-judgment — its textual status doesn't affect any point of Christian doctrine, but it is a case where the manuscript evidence and the passage's devotional importance pull in different directions more sharply than almost anywhere else in the New Testament.

**A clarification before the split:** as with Mark 16:9–20, the actual wording of this passage is essentially the same across every translation that includes it — none prints substantially different words for it. What differs is whether it's presented as ordinary text or marked as textually uncertain.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include without qualification:** KJV and NKJV. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would be expected to include it unflagged as well, given the same Textus-Receptus-preservation methods discussed throughout this NT section, though none was separately confirmed. **The ASV** is confirmed directly as including the passage (per a source cataloguing which historic translations contain the story), consistent with predating the modern double-bracket convention.
- **Include but clearly marked (double brackets and/or a heading noting the earliest manuscripts don't have it):** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, and LSB.
- **NLT** includes it with an explanatory footnote.
- **RSV2CE**, like most modern translations working from a critical Greek text, includes the passage with a note on its textual status. **The RSV** is confirmed directly as including the passage but setting it off in special print since its 1971 revision. **The NABRE** is confirmed directly as including the passage with an extensive footnote explicitly describing it as "a later insertion," making it one of the most direct, explicit textual-critical footnotes on this passage among any translation checked in this project.
- Every translation checked in this research pass does include the passage somewhere in the main body of the text (none relegates it purely to an appendix or omits it outright) -- the disagreement is about how prominently to flag the uncertainty, not about whether to print the story at all. **Not independently confirmed:** WEB, Darby, Smith's Literal Translation, Douay-Rheims, the CPDV, NRSV-CE, the ERV, the NET Bible, the BSB, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific passage.

## 2. John 1:18 — is Jesus "the only begotten Son," or "the only God"?

**The variant:** Describing Jesus' unique relationship to the Father, one important textual tradition reads "the only-begotten **Son**, who is in the bosom of the Father" (Greek: *monogenēs huios*) — the reading behind the traditional KJV rendering. A different, and arguably slightly better-attested, set of early manuscripts (including P66, P75, and both Vaticanus and Sinaiticus) instead reads "the only-begotten **God**" (*monogenēs theos*) — a strikingly direct affirmation of Christ's divinity if this is the original wording, and one of the more theologically loaded textual variants in the New Testament.

**Why it matters:** Some scholars argue "God" is the harder, less expected reading (since "Son" is the more natural, expected phrase, making it more likely that scribes would have smoothed "God" into "Son" than the reverse) and so more likely original by standard text-critical reasoning; others note that "Son" has extremely early and widespread support across multiple manuscript families, complicating a simple "shorter/harder reading wins" judgment. Notably, this exact wording was actively contested on both trinitarian and non-trinitarian sides during the 3rd-4th century Christological debates, which several scholars think shaped how later scribes handled the verse regardless of which reading came first.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **"Only begotten Son" / "only Son":** KJV and NKJV. **The Douay-Rheims Bible, YLT, the RSV, the ERV, and the CJB** are all confirmed directly in this camp as well. **The CPDV and Webster's Bible** would be expected to fall in this same camp -- CPDV given its general alignment with Douay-Rheims, Webster's given its KJV-preservation method -- though neither was separately confirmed. **The AKJV** would likewise be expected to match KJV here.
- **"Only God" / "God the only Son" / similar direct wording, with the Greek's force generally preserved in the main text:** ESV ("the only God"), NASB ("the only begotten God" in some editions, "the only God" in the 2020 update), CSB, and NIV (all with footnotes on the alternative).
- **AMP** renders in an expanded, amplified way that preserves both nuances ("the only unique Son, who is God").
- **NLT** renders "the unique One, who is himself God," reflecting the "God" textual tradition.
- **RSV2CE** follows the general modern-critical pattern, typically reading close to "the only Son, God" or similar, reflecting the *monogenēs theos* reading. **The NRSV-CE is confirmed directly** in a related but distinct hybrid form: "God the only Son" -- neither a clean "Son" reading nor a clean "God" reading, but a construction that names both terms together, worth noting as its own specific approach rather than assuming it matches either camp cleanly.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** ASV, WEB, Darby, Smith's Literal Translation, NABRE, the NET Bible, the BSB, and TRB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse.

## 3. John 5:3–4 — the angel who stirred the water at Bethesda

**The variant:** Describing the pool of Bethesda where a great crowd of the sick waited, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) explain why: "waiting for the moving of the water. For an angel went down at a certain season into the pool, and troubled the water: whosoever then first after the troubling of the water stepped in was made whole of whatsoever disease he had." This material is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as an early explanatory note, added to account for the paralyzed man's own comment two verses later (5:7) that he has no one to help him into the water "when it is troubled" — a comment that otherwise refers to a custom the text never explains.

**Why it matters:** Without this material, the paralyzed man's remark in 5:7 refers to a popular belief about the pool that the surrounding text simply doesn't spell out for the reader — a small but real gap in the narrative's own internal explanation, depending on which reading is followed.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the material in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would be expected to include it, on the same Textus-Receptus-preservation grounds discussed throughout this section.
- **Omit it from the main text (moving directly from 5:3's mention of the sick waiting to 5:5's introduction of the paralyzed man), typically with a footnote explaining the omission and its connection to 5:7:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT. **Douay-Rheims and the CPDV** would be expected to side with the shorter reading, matching the general pattern established elsewhere in this project for this type of scribal-explanatory addition, though not separately confirmed.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** AMP and RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with ASV, WEB, Darby, Smith's Literal Translation, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, the ERV, the NET Bible, the BSB, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific passage.

## Summary observations

- John 7:53-8:11 is, alongside Mark 16:9-20, one of the two most significant textual questions in the entire New Testament -- both involve substantial passages (not single words or phrases) whose absence from the earliest manuscripts is unusually well documented, and both are retained by every translation checked in this project despite that, showing how strongly historical usage and devotional importance weigh alongside pure manuscript evidence in translation decisions.
- John 1:18 is a good example of a variant where the *theological* stakes (how directly the text affirms Christ's deity) are unusually high for what looks, on the surface, like a small wording difference -- and this expansion pass turned up a genuinely new data point: the NRSV-CE's hybrid "God the only Son" rendering is its own distinct third approach, not simply belonging to either the "Son" or "God" camp.
