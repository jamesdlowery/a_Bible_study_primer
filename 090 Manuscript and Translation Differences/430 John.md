# John: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

As explained in the companion Matthew chapter, New Testament translations divide mainly between those based on the later Textus Receptus (KJV, NKJV) and those based on a modern critical text weighted toward the earliest manuscripts (most others). John contains the second most famous textual variant in the whole New Testament, after Mark's ending.

---

## 1. John 7:53–8:11 — the woman caught in adultery

**The variant:** This beloved story — Jesus confronted with a woman accused of adultery, responding "let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone," and ultimately telling her "neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" — is entirely absent from the earliest surviving manuscripts of John, including two of the oldest and most important papyri (P66 and P75, both roughly 2nd–3rd century), as well as Codex Vaticanus and the original hand of Codex Sinaiticus. It's also absent from the earliest commentators on John (including Origen, Chrysostom, and Cyril of Alexandria, none of whom shows any awareness of it in their verse-by-verse treatments of this section). Where it does appear in the manuscript tradition, it shows unusual behavior for a genuine part of the original text: some manuscripts place it not here but after John 7:36, after John 21:25 (the very end of the Gospel), or even inserted into the Gospel of Luke instead (after Luke 21:38 or 24:53) — a pattern of "floating" to different locations that textual critics generally take as a strong sign of a later addition to the text, since a passage original to a book from its composition doesn't typically wander between different locations across the manuscript tradition. The story is also written in a noticeably different Greek style from the rest of John (with a number of words and constructions that don't appear elsewhere in this Gospel).

That said, the case for later insertion isn't airtight: the story is attested quite early in substance — the 2nd-century writer Papias reportedly knew a very similar account (as reported by the church historian Eusebius), and it appears in the Old Latin tradition and in the influential (though textually unusual) Codex Bezae by the 5th century, showing it was in circulation, in some form, reasonably early — just apparently not as part of John's Gospel from the start.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the most beloved and frequently cited episodes in all four Gospels, central to how many Christians think about mercy and non-judgment — its textual status doesn't affect any point of Christian doctrine, but it is a case where the manuscript evidence and the passage's devotional importance pull in different directions more sharply than almost anywhere else in the New Testament.

**A clarification before the split:** as with Mark 16:9–20, the actual wording of this passage is essentially the same across every translation that includes it — none prints substantially different words for it. What differs is whether it's presented as ordinary text or marked as textually uncertain.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include without qualification:** KJV and NKJV. **The AKJV and YLT are now confirmed directly** including it unflagged as well ("a woman taken in adultery" flowing directly from 7:52 into 8:1 with no marking, in both cases). **The ASV** is confirmed directly as including the passage (per a source cataloguing which historic translations contain the story), consistent with predating the modern double-bracket convention. **Confirmed directly against the full text of each translation, the Douay-Rheims Bible, the CPDV, the WEB, Smith's Literal Translation, and Webster's Bible** all include the full passage as ordinary continuous text, with the narrative flowing directly from John 7:52 into 8:1 and on through 8:11 without any heading, footnote, or bracket marking its status as textually disputed in the text as consulted for this research pass -- consistent with the pattern already established for KJV, NKJV, and ASV. (This document can confirm the substance and structure of the passage is present and unmarked in the specific text consulted for each of these four translations; it cannot rule out that some print editions of the same translations use typographic conventions, such as italics or a printed note, not preserved in the text form checked here.)
- **Include but clearly marked (double brackets and/or a heading noting the earliest manuscripts don't have it):** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, and LSB. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this camp, enclosing the entire passage in double brackets from its opening ("[[7:53 And each one departed to his own house.") through its close ("...do not sin any more.]]"), immediately before the next section heading.
- **NLT** includes it with an explanatory footnote. **AMP is also confirmed directly** in this same footnote-only pattern, checked against its own text at bible.com: the passage flows as ordinary text, with a footnote at verse 53 reading "Most late mss include the story of the adulterous woman, numbering it as 7:53-8:11, though some mss place it elsewhere in the text" -- no double-bracket marking of the kind used by ESV/NIV/CSB/NASB/LSB.
- **RSV2CE**, like most modern translations working from a critical Greek text, includes the passage with a note on its textual status. **The RSV** is confirmed directly as including the passage but setting it off in special print since its 1971 revision. **The NABRE** is confirmed directly as including the passage with an extensive footnote explicitly describing it as "a later insertion," making it one of the most direct, explicit textual-critical footnotes on this passage among any translation checked in this project.
**The ERV is also confirmed directly** including the passage as ordinary continuous text from 7:53 through 8:11, under its own section heading ("A Woman Caught in Adultery") -- consistent with ERV's general editorial practice of adding descriptive section headings throughout its text rather than a textual-critical flag specific to this passage, since ERV uses similar headings at undisputed passages elsewhere in this project's other entries. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** including the passage the same way, under its own section heading ("The Woman Caught in Adultery"), consistent with BSB's own general practice of adding section headings throughout, as seen elsewhere in this project.
- Every translation checked in this research pass does include the passage somewhere in the main body of the text (none relegates it purely to an appendix or omits it outright) -- the disagreement is about how prominently to flag the uncertainty, not about whether to print the story at all. **Darby is now confirmed directly** including the full passage as ordinary continuous text, with no heading, footnote, or bracket marking its status as textually disputed. **NRSV-CE is now confirmed directly** in the double-bracket camp above, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: the passage closes with a visible "]]" bracket marker after verse 11, and a footnote reads "The most ancient authorities lack 7.53—8.11; other authorities add the passage here or after 7.36 or after 21.25 or after Luke 21.38, with variations of text; some mark the passage as doubtful" -- joining ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, and LSB's clearly-marked approach rather than the unflagged-inclusion or footnote-only camps. **The CJB is also now confirmed directly** joining Darby, KJV, NKJV, ASV, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, WEB, SLT, and Webster's Bible in the unflagged-inclusion camp above: checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com, the passage flows as ordinary continuous text from John 7:53 through 8:11, with no heading, footnote, or bracket marking its textual status. **Not independently confirmed:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific passage.

## 2. John 1:18 — is Jesus "the only begotten Son," or "the only God"?

**The variant:** Describing Jesus' unique relationship to the Father, one important textual tradition reads "the only-begotten **Son**, who is in the bosom of the Father" (Greek: *monogenēs huios*) — the reading behind the traditional KJV rendering. A different, and arguably slightly better-attested, set of early manuscripts (including P66, P75, and both Vaticanus and Sinaiticus) instead reads "the only-begotten **God**" (*monogenēs theos*) — a strikingly direct affirmation of Christ's divinity if this is the original wording, and one of the more theologically loaded textual variants in the New Testament.

**Why it matters:** Some scholars argue "God" is the harder, less expected reading (since "Son" is the more natural, expected phrase, making it more likely that scribes would have smoothed "God" into "Son" than the reverse) and so more likely original by standard text-critical reasoning; others note that "Son" has extremely early and widespread support across multiple manuscript families, complicating a simple "shorter/harder reading wins" judgment. Notably, this exact wording was actively contested on both trinitarian and non-trinitarian sides during the 3rd-4th century Christological debates, which several scholars think shaped how later scribes handled the verse regardless of which reading came first.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **"Only begotten Son" / "only Son":** KJV and NKJV. **The Douay-Rheims Bible, YLT, the RSV, the ERV, and the CJB** are all confirmed directly in this camp as well. Also confirmed directly: **the ASV** ("the only begotten Son"), **the WEB** ("The one and only Son"), **Darby** ("the only-begotten Son"), **Webster's Bible** ("the only begotten Son," matching KJV exactly, as expected given its preservation method), and **Smith's Literal Translation** ("the only born Son, he being in the bosom of the Father"). **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** in this camp, matching the expectation ("the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he himself has described him"). **The AKJV** would likewise be expected to match KJV here.
- **"Only God" / "God the only Son" / similar direct wording, with the Greek's force generally preserved in the main text:** ESV ("the only God"), NASB ("the only begotten God" in some editions, "the only God" in the 2020 update), CSB, and NIV (all with footnotes on the alternative). **LSB is also confirmed directly in this camp** ("the only begotten God who is in the bosom of the Father").
- **AMP** renders in an expanded, amplified way that preserves both nuances ("the only unique Son, who is God").
- **NLT** renders "the unique One, who is himself God," reflecting the "God" textual tradition. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same construction, nearly identical to NLT's own wording: "The only one, himself God, who is in closest fellowship with the Father, has made God known."
- **RSV2CE** follows the general modern-critical pattern, typically reading close to "the only Son, God" or similar, reflecting the *monogenēs theos* reading. **The NRSV-CE is confirmed directly** in a related but distinct hybrid form: "God the only Son" -- neither a clean "Son" reading nor a clean "God" reading, but a construction that names both terms together, worth noting as its own specific approach rather than assuming it matches either camp cleanly. **The BSB is confirmed directly in this same general hybrid family**, though its own specific wording differs again: "the one and only Son, who is Himself God" -- naming "Son" first and "God" second, the reverse order from NRSV-CE's "God the only Son," and matching the general approach the CJB also takes ("the only and unique Son, who is identical with God"). **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same hybrid family, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text reads "the only Son, God," with the footnote explicitly explaining the translators' choice: "while the vast majority of later textual witnesses have another reading, 'the Son, the only one' or 'the only Son,' the translation above follows the best and earliest manuscripts, monogenēs theos" -- yet another distinct word-order variant within this same hybrid family, matching neither NRSV-CE's "God the only Son" nor BSB's "Son...God" ordering exactly.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse.

## 3. John 5:3–4 — the angel who stirred the water at Bethesda

**The variant:** Describing the pool of Bethesda where a great crowd of the sick waited, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) explain why: "waiting for the moving of the water. For an angel went down at a certain season into the pool, and troubled the water: whosoever then first after the troubling of the water stepped in was made whole of whatsoever disease he had." This material is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as an early explanatory note, added to account for the paralyzed man's own comment two verses later (5:7) that he has no one to help him into the water "when it is troubled" — a comment that otherwise refers to a custom the text never explains.

**Why it matters:** Without this material, the paralyzed man's remark in 5:7 refers to a popular belief about the pool that the surrounding text simply doesn't spell out for the reader — a small but real gap in the narrative's own internal explanation, depending on which reading is followed.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the material in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **Confirmed directly** (see the fuller discussion of this same verse in the companion Reportedly Contradicting Passages chapter), **the Douay-Rheims Bible, YLT, Webster's Bible, Darby, the WEB, AMP,** and **Smith's Literal Translation** ("For a messenger at a time went down into the pool, and troubled the water," again using "messenger" as at Luke 22:43 and Psalm 8:5 elsewhere in this project) all include this material as well, without bracketing -- correcting this entry's prior assumption that Douay-Rheims would side with the shorter reading, and consistent with the broader pattern (documented at multiple points in this project's Matthew, Mark, and Luke entries) of Douay-Rheims including rather than omitting this general category of disputed explanatory or harmonizing material. **The AKJV** would be expected to include it as well, on the same Textus-Receptus-preservation grounds discussed throughout this section. **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this passage, matching the expectation ("Now at times an Angel of the Lord would descend into the pool, and so the water was moved. And whoever descended first into the pool, after the motion of the water, he was healed of whatever infirmity held him").
- **Omit it from the main text (moving directly from 5:3's mention of the sick waiting to 5:5's introduction of the paralyzed man), typically with a footnote explaining the omission and its connection to 5:7:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB. **LSB** prints the material in brackets rather than omitting it outright, the same middle position documented at several companion entries elsewhere in this project. **NLT is now confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against an independent published source: its main text reads simply "Crowds of sick people—blind, lame, or paralyzed—lay on the porches," with no verse 4 content. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its characteristic minimal marking documented repeatedly throughout this project: its main text shows only "4 ‡" with no verse content, moving directly from verse 3 into verse 5. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, going even further than ERV's approach: it omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 3 into verse 5. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same fully-omitted style, moving directly from verse 3 into verse 5 with no "4" shown at all. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against its own text: its main text moves directly from verse 3's list of ailments to verse 5's introduction of the paralyzed man, with no verse 4 content and no "waiting for the moving of the water" clause at the end of verse 3 either.
- **A distinct, fourth handling, confirmed directly:** **the American Standard Version** places the material in the margin with an explanatory note, rather than either the main running text or a conventional footnote.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific passage. **RSV2CE is now confirmed directly** in the omitting camp above, checked against its own published text at bible-history.com: its main text moves directly from verse 3's list of ailments to verse 5's introduction of the paralyzed man, with no verse 4 content and no "waiting for the moving of the water" clause at the end of verse 3 either -- identical to its RSV parent, and its footnote confirms the omitted material: "Other ancient authorities insert, wholly or in part, waiting for the moving of the water; 4 for an angel of the Lord went down at certain seasons into the pool, and troubled the water: whoever stepped in first after the troubling of the water was healed of whatever disease he had." **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: its main text likewise moves directly from verse 3 to verse 5, with no verse 4 content, and its footnote reads "Other ancient authorities add, wholly or in part, waiting for the stirring of the water; 4for an angel of the Lord went down at certain seasons into the pool, and stirred up the water; whoever stepped in first after the stirring of the water was made well from whatever disease that person had." **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text at bible.usccb.org: its main text moves directly from verse 3 through a bare "4*" marker with no content to verse 5, reading "In these lay a large number of ill, blind, lame, and crippled.* 4* 5One man was there who had been ill for thirty-eight years." **The CJB is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com: its main text ends verse 3 at "a crowd of invalids -- blind, lame, crippled" with no "waiting for the moving of the water" clause, and its note at verse 4 reads only "Some manuscripts have verses 3b-4: . . . , waiting for the water to move; for at certain times an angel of ADONAI went down into the pool and disturbed the water, and whoever stepped into the water first after it was disturbed was healed of whatever disease he had," moving directly into verse 5 without supplying the material as actual text.

## Summary observations

- John 7:53-8:11 is, alongside Mark 16:9-20, one of the two most significant textual questions in the entire New Testament -- both involve substantial passages (not single words or phrases) whose absence from the earliest manuscripts is unusually well documented, and both are retained by every translation checked in this project despite that, showing how strongly historical usage and devotional importance weigh alongside pure manuscript evidence in translation decisions.
- John 1:18 is a good example of a variant where the *theological* stakes (how directly the text affirms Christ's deity) are unusually high for what looks, on the surface, like a small wording difference -- and this expansion pass turned up a genuinely new data point: the NRSV-CE's hybrid "God the only Son" rendering is its own distinct third approach, not simply belonging to either the "Son" or "God" camp.
