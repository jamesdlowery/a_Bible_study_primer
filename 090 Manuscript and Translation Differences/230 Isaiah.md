# Isaiah: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

## Isaiah 53:11 — does the Servant "see [and be satisfied]," or "see light [and be satisfied]"?

**The variant:** MT reads, awkwardly, "out of the anguish of his soul he shall see... and be satisfied" — the verb "see" has no stated object. The Septuagint's underlying Hebrew, and three separate Dead Sea Scrolls Isaiah manuscripts (1QIsaᵃ, 1QIsaᵇ, 4QIsaᵈ), all supply the missing object: "he shall see **light**."

**How the translations render it:**
- **Include "light":** NIV ("he will see the light of life," with a footnote citing the DSS and Septuagint), CSB (similarly, in the main text).
- **Follow MT without "light":** ESV, KJV, NASB (main text omits it; ESV and NASB footnote the DSS/Septuagint alternative), NKJV (keeps MT's shorter reading in the main text but footnotes the DSS/Septuagint alternative explicitly). **The RSV is confirmed directly in this camp** ("he shall see the fruit of the travail of his soul"). **The Vulgate itself -- and, by direct consequence, the Douay-Rheims Bible and the CPDV -- also fall in this camp**: the Vulgate is explicitly documented among the ancient witnesses reading "he will see" without "light," alongside the Masoretic Text, Theodotion, Aquila, Symmachus, the Targum, and the Peshitta, standing opposite the Dead Sea Scrolls/Septuagint reading. This makes Douay-Rheims/CPDV's expected position here a matter of direct textual lineage (their shared Vulgate source), not just family resemblance to another English translation.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** AMP, LSB, NLT, and RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with ASV, WEB, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, and CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse.

**The difference in meaning:** With "light," the verse reads as a statement of vindication or renewed life after the Servant's suffering and death described in the surrounding verses — some read this as an implicit resurrection reference. Without it, the verse simply states the Servant "will see" (with the object left for the reader to infer from context) and be satisfied.

## Side note: a case where nearly all 27 translations agree, but the underlying Hebrew word is genuinely ambiguous rather than textually variant

- **Isaiah 7:14** — MT's word *almah* means a young woman of marriageable age, with virginity implied by context rather than stated outright (a distinct Hebrew word, *betulah*, means "virgin" specifically and isn't used here). The Septuagint's *parthenos* ("virgin," quoted in Matthew 1:23) reflects one legitimate reading of the ambiguous term, not a competing manuscript reading -- every preserved Hebrew manuscript, including the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ), agrees on *almah*; no Hebrew witness anywhere supports "virgin" directly. Whether the Septuagint's translators were making an interpretive choice or drawing on a separate, now-lost tradition is itself debated, but the surviving manuscript record shows no actual variant -- only two defensible readings of one stable word. The MT reading is more naturally understood, in its own immediate context, as a near-term sign involving a young woman of Isaiah's own day (often linked to the birth of a child named Immanuel as a contemporary figure), while the Septuagint's "virgin" became, centuries later, the reading Matthew draws on for the classic Christian messianic prophecy of the virgin birth.

  Among the 27 tracked translations, the picture splits as follows:

  **Render "virgin":** AMP (in its amplified style, alongside "young woman": "young woman who is unmarried and a virgin"), CSB, ESV, KJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NKJV, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, Darby, Young's Literal, and Webster's Bible -- confirmed directly against each translation's own published text, not assumed from family resemblance. Young's Literal is worth flagging specifically -- despite being the most aggressively literal translation in this entire set, it still renders "the Virgin," matching Douay-Rheims, Darby, and the KJV tradition rather than the more skeptical, meaning-focused choice one might expect from an extreme-literalist translator.

  **Render "young woman":** NLT, the RSV (whose choice here was, as described in this book's own history of that translation, the specific flashpoint of a major 1950s evangelical controversy), the NRSV-CE, the NABRE, the ERV, and the CJB -- confirming the CJB's own stated general practice of favoring the Masoretic reading, discussed at more length in that translation's own history entry.

  **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** the CPDV and the AKJV were not directly checked against their own published text for this specific verse, though both would be expected, on methodological grounds, to render "virgin" -- CPDV because it explicitly uses the Douay-Rheims (which reads "virgin") as its guide for traditional Catholic phrasing, and AKJV because its own stated method is to preserve the KJV's wording exactly, changing only spelling. Smith's Literal Translation, the NET Bible, and TRB were not confirmed either way and should not be assumed to follow the pattern of translations with a similar general character; each would need its own direct check before being stated with confidence.

  This wider picture shows that the "virgin"/"young woman" divide tracks translation philosophy and theological tradition more than any single manuscript-family line, since Vulgate-tradition, KJV-tradition, and even one extreme-literalist translation all land on "virgin," while the RSV/NRSV/NABRE line and the CJB's own Masoretic fidelity land on "young woman" despite otherwise having very little in common with each other.
