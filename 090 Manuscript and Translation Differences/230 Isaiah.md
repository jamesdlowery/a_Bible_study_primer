# Isaiah: Textual and Translation Differences

---

## Isaiah 7:14 — does "the virgin" conceive, or "the young woman"?

**The variant:** This is a translation split over a stable text, not a manuscript variant. MT's word *almah* means a young woman of marriageable age, with virginity implied by context rather than stated outright (a distinct Hebrew word, *betulah*, means "virgin" specifically and isn't used here). Every preserved Hebrew manuscript, including the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ), agrees on *almah*; no Hebrew witness anywhere reads "virgin" directly. The Septuagint's *parthenos* ("virgin," quoted in Matthew 1:23) reflects one legitimate reading of the ambiguous term. Whether the Septuagint's translators were making an interpretive choice or drawing on a separate, now-lost tradition is itself debated, but the surviving manuscript record shows two defensible readings of one stable word — and the tracked translations divide over which to print.

**How the translations render it:**
- **Render "virgin":** AMP (in its amplified style, alongside "young woman": "young woman who is unmarried and a virgin"), CSB, ESV, KJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NKJV, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV ("a virgin will conceive, and she will give birth to a son"), Darby, Young's Literal, Webster's Bible, Smith's Literal Translation ("Behold, the virgin conceiving, and she will bare a son, and call his name Immanuel"), and the AKJV ("a virgin shall conceive"), matching KJV's wording exactly — each confirmed against its own published text. Young's Literal and Smith's Literal are worth flagging specifically: despite being the two most aggressively literal translations in this entire set, both still render "the virgin," matching Douay-Rheims, Darby, and the KJV tradition rather than the more skeptical, meaning-focused choice one might expect from an extreme-literalist translator.
- **Render "young woman":** NLT, the RSV (whose choice here was, as described in this book's own history of that translation, the specific flashpoint of a major 1950s evangelical controversy), the NRSV-CE, the NABRE, the ERV, the NET Bible ("this young woman is about to conceive and will give birth to a son"), and the CJB — confirming the CJB's own stated general practice of favoring the Masoretic reading, discussed at more length in that translation's own history entry.
- **TRB** renders "virgin": "The virgin will conceive and give birth to a son, and she will name him Immanuel (i.e., God With Us)."

**The difference in meaning:** "Virgin" reads the verse as the prophecy of a miraculous birth that Matthew applies to Jesus; "young woman" reads it, in its own immediate context, as a near-term sign to Ahaz involving a woman of Isaiah's own day, with the child Immanuel a contemporary figure. The divide tracks translation philosophy and theological tradition more than any manuscript-family line: Vulgate-tradition, KJV-tradition, and even the two extreme-literalist translations all land on "virgin," while the RSV/NRSV/NABRE line and the CJB's Masoretic fidelity land on "young woman" despite otherwise having very little in common with each other.

## Isaiah 53:11 — does the Servant "see [and be satisfied]," or "see light [and be satisfied]"?

**The variant:** MT reads, awkwardly, "out of the anguish of his soul he shall see... and be satisfied" — the verb "see" has no stated object. The Septuagint's underlying Hebrew, and three separate Dead Sea Scrolls Isaiah manuscripts (1QIsaᵃ, 1QIsaᵇ, 4QIsaᵈ), all supply the missing object: "he shall see **light**."

**How the translations render it:**
- **Include "light":** NIV ("he will see the light of life," with a footnote citing the DSS and Septuagint), CSB (similarly, in the main text). Also including "light": **the NRSV** ("Out of his anguish he shall see light," and by direct extension **the NRSV-CE**), **the BSB** ("he will see the light of life and be satisfied"), and **the ERV** (Easy-to-Read Version: "After his suffering he will see the light"). **WEB** is also in this camp ("After the suffering of his soul, he will see the light and be satisfied"). **NABRE** is also in this same "light" camp Lectionary readings for Good Friday and other liturgical days: "Because of his affliction he shall see the light in fullness of days" -- a genuinely notable placement, since this puts NABRE with the DSS/Septuagint-following majority rather than with the Vulgate-derived Douay-Rheims and CPDV, despite NABRE otherwise sharing a broadly Catholic textual heritage with those two translations.
- **Follow MT without "light":** ESV, KJV, NASB (main text omits it; ESV and NASB footnote the DSS/Septuagint alternative), NKJV (keeps MT's shorter reading in the main text but footnotes the DSS/Septuagint alternative explicitly). **RSV** is also in this camp ("he shall see the fruit of the travail of his soul"). **RSV2CE** is also in this same camp, using identical wording to its RSV parent: "he shall see the fruit of the travail of his soul and be satisfied." **The Vulgate itself -- and, by direct consequence, the Douay-Rheims Bible and the CPDV -- also fall in this camp**: the Vulgate is explicitly documented among the ancient witnesses reading "he will see" without "light," alongside the Masoretic Text, Theodotion, Aquila, Symmachus, the Targum, and the Peshitta, standing opposite the Dead Sea Scrolls/Septuagint reading. This makes Douay-Rheims/CPDV's expected position here a matter of direct textual lineage (their shared Vulgate source), not just family resemblance to another English translation. Also in this MT-following camp: **YLT** ("Of the labour of his soul he seeth -- he is satisfied") and **Webster's Bible** ("He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied"), along with **Darby** (which uses a bracketed "[the fruit of]" rather than "light": "He shall see of [the fruit of] the travail of his soul"), **the NET Bible**, which paraphrases the whole clause distinctively without "light" at all ("Having suffered, he will reflect on his work, he will be satisfied when he understands what he has done"), and **Smith's Literal Translation** ("And be shall see of the labor of his soul, he shall be satisfied"). **AKJV** is also in this camp, matching KJV's wording exactly ("He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied"). **ASV** is also in this camp, matching KJV's wording exactly ("He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied"). **AMP** is also in this camp and studylight.org: "As a result of the anguish of His soul, He shall see it and be satisfied," matching NASB's pattern closely -- no "light." **LSB** is also in this camp: "As a result of the anguish of His soul, [My Servant] will justify the many" -- no "light," closely matching AMP's and NASB's phrasing.
- **The CJB** renders a further distinct paraphrase, also without "light": "After this ordeal, he will see satisfaction" -- its own wording, not a clean match for either the "light" camp or the more literal MT-following renderings above.
- **NLT** also falls in the MT-following, no-"light" camp: "When he sees all that is accomplished by his anguish, he will be satisfied" -- its own distinctive paraphrase, clearly omitting "light."
- **TRB** is in the "light" camp: "Because of the anguish of his soul, he will see light and be satisfied," with a footnote: "From the Dead Sea Scrolls and Septuagint; Masoretic text does not include the word 'light.'"

**The difference in meaning:** With "light," the verse reads as a statement of vindication or renewed life after the Servant's suffering and death described in the surrounding verses — some read this as an implicit resurrection reference. Without it, the verse simply states the Servant "will see" (with the object left for the reader to infer from context) and be satisfied.
