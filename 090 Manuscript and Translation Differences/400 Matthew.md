# Matthew: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

The New Testament's textual situation is fundamentally different from the Old Testament's. Instead of a few ancient versions (MT, LXX, DSS, Samaritan Pentateuch) to compare, we have well over 5,000 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, ranging from tiny 2nd-century papyrus scraps to complete 4th-century codices (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) to thousands of later medieval copies. The KJV (1611) was translated from a late medieval Greek compilation known as the **Textus Receptus**, assembled by Erasmus and later editors from the relatively young manuscripts available to them in the 16th century. Since then, the discovery of much older manuscripts has led most modern translations to work from a **critical text** (currently Nestle-Aland/UBS) that weighs the earliest and best manuscripts more heavily — which is why KJV and NKJV (which deliberately retains the Textus Receptus base) sometimes include material that ESV, NIV, NASB, CSB, and others flag as absent from the earliest manuscripts, or omit altogether. Translations based on the Latin Vulgate (Douay-Rheims, CPDV) form a third lineage in the New Testament, generally though not always agreeing with the critical text against the Textus Receptus at points like these, since Jerome's own source manuscripts predate the medieval Byzantine tradition Erasmus worked from.

## 1. Matthew 6:13 — does the Lord's Prayer end with a doxology?

**The variant:** The version of the Lord's Prayer most familiar from KJV usage ends: "For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen." This doxology is present in the great majority of later Greek manuscripts (and so in the Textus Receptus), but is absent from the earliest and generally most highly regarded manuscripts (including Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus), and from the earliest patristic commentators on the prayer (including Origen and Cyril of Jerusalem, both of whom comment verse-by-verse on the prayer without mentioning it). Most textual scholars think the doxology originated as a liturgical formula — the kind of line a congregation would add aloud after a scripture reading in worship — that eventually made its way from the margin into the text itself, a well-documented pattern in ancient manuscript transmission generally.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the most familiar single lines in Christian devotional life (recited weekly or daily by many congregations), so its textual status is worth knowing even though it doesn't change any core doctrine — the sentiment is thoroughly biblical elsewhere (compare 1 Chronicles 29:11), even if these specific words may not be Matthew's.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the doxology in the main text, without qualification:** KJV and NKJV. **The AKJV and Webster's Bible** would both be expected to include it as well, given their explicit method of preserving the KJV's text with only spelling or vocabulary modernized, though neither was separately confirmed. **YLT** would likewise be expected to include it, given Young's own stated adherence to the received/Textus Receptus tradition, though this was not separately confirmed. **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever. Amen"), matching KJV's wording closely. **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this camp as well ("For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen").
- **Omit it from the main text, typically with a footnote noting that some (later) manuscripts add it:** ESV, NASB, NIV, CSB, LSB, and NLT. **No Catholic-tradition translation includes the doxology** — this was directly confirmed for the Douay-Rheims Bible and the CPDV, both of which omit it entirely (the CPDV's omission independently confirmed again via a source specifically surveying every Catholic translation on this exact point, including Douay-Rheims 1582/1950, the 1970 St. Joseph NAB, and the 1985 New Jerusalem Bible, alongside the 2009 CPDV -- none contains the doxology), consistent with the Latin Vulgate itself never having included it. The **NRSV-CE and NABRE** would be expected to follow the same Catholic-tradition omission. Also confirmed directly omitting the doxology: **Darby** ("And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil"), **the ASV** ("And bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one"), and **the BSB** ("And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one"). **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp: "Don't let us be tempted, but save us from the Evil One," with no doxology following. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp ("And do not lead us into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one"), with no doxology following. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against an independent source specifically surveying this verse's translation history, which groups RSV with NASB (2020), NIV, ESV, Darby, and NET among the versions omitting the doxology.
- AMP includes the doxology but sets it off in a way that signals its textual status; RSV2CE's main text follows the shorter reading, consistent with its general Alexandrian-text-critical basis. **A genuinely surprising confirmed finding: the CJB includes a version of the doxology** ("For kingship, power and glory are yours forever"), despite CJB generally tracking the modern critical-text pattern elsewhere in this project -- a notable departure from the expectation that Catholic- and modern-critical-text-based translations uniformly omit this material.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse.

## 2. Matthew 27:16–17 — was Barabbas's first name "Jesus"?

**The variant:** A number of manuscripts (including some cited in Origen's commentary, and reflected in some Syriac and Armenian traditions) give Barabbas's given name as "Jesus" — so that Pilate's choice for the crowd is between "Jesus Barabbas" and "Jesus who is called Christ," a pointed irony (both men bear the same very common name, "Jesus/Yeshua," one meaning "salvation") that most later manuscripts lack, since Barabbas is elsewhere referred to simply by his surname. Origen himself, in the 3rd century, states he had seen manuscripts with this reading but suspected pious scribes might have originally included it and then it was later removed out of reverence, feeling it inappropriate for a criminal to share Jesus' name — though the text-critical judgment today generally favors the "Jesus Barabbas" reading as original, on the principle that scribes are more likely to have removed an uncomfortable reading than added one.

**Translations:** **NIV** includes "Jesus Barabbas" in its main text (with a footnote on the manuscript basis). **NLT's main text reads simply "Barabbas,"** not "Jesus Barabbas" — it does not join NIV here. ESV and CSB likewise keep "Barabbas" in the main text (ESV notes the variant in a footnote); KJV, NKJV, NASB, and AMP simply read "Barabbas" without any indication of the variant. RSV2CE's main text reads "Barabbas" as well. **Of the 17 additional translations, confirmed directly: the AKJV, ASV, CPDV, Darby, the Douay-Rheims Bible, Smith's Literal Translation, Webster's Bible, the WEB, and Young's Literal Translation all read plain "Barabbas,"** joining the majority and not adopting the "Jesus Barabbas" reading. **The BSB and the ERV also read plain "Barabbas,"** though each flags the variant with its own footnote marker rather than incorporating it into the main text. **The NET Bible, however, is a second confirmed instance of the "Jesus Barabbas" reading** ("a notorious prisoner named Jesus Barabbas... Jesus Barabbas or Jesus who is called the Christ"), joining NIV rather than the majority -- a genuinely new finding this entry's prior research had not established, since only NIV had previously been confirmed adopting this minority reading. **RSV is also confirmed directly** joining the plain-"Barabbas" majority, checked against multiple independent sources: "they had then a notorious prisoner, called Barab'bas," with no "Jesus" prefix.

## 3. Matthew 17:21 — "this kind goeth not out but by prayer and fasting"

**The variant:** After the disciples fail to cast out a demon and ask Jesus why, the majority of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) add his explanation: "Howbeit this kind goeth not out but by prayer and fasting." This verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts (including Sinaiticus and Vaticanus), and most textual scholars regard it as an early scribal harmonization, copied in from the nearly identical explanation at Mark 9:29 (itself also textually disputed — see the companion Mark document).

**Why it matters:** The verse has shaped a great deal of Christian teaching and practice around fasting as a spiritual discipline connected to answered prayer and spiritual authority, making its textual status directly relevant to how much weight that specific pairing of "prayer and fasting" should carry as Jesus' own recorded words at this point in the narrative.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would all be expected to include it as well, on the same Textus-Receptus-preservation grounds noted at 6:13. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible also includes this verse, per a source specifically surveying which translations contain it (listing "King James Version, New King James Version, World English Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Geneva Bible, and Douay-Rheims Bible" together as translations that "all include this verse"). This is the ninth confirmed instance in this project's set of disputed-verse entries (see the companion Mark, Luke, John, Acts, Romans, and Colossians entries elsewhere in this document set) of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, material absent from the earliest manuscripts. Also confirmed directly including the verse per that same source: **the WEB** and **YLT**, along with **the ASV** ("But this kind goeth not out save by prayer and fasting"), confirmed directly via a separate source, and **Smith's Literal Translation** ("But this kind goes not forth but by prayer and fasting"), confirmed directly, and **Darby**, confirmed directly ("But this kind goes not out but by prayer and fasting").
- **Omit it from the main text, typically with a footnote noting its absence from the earliest manuscripts and its relationship to Mark 9:29:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, in a genuinely minimal way not previously documented anywhere in this project: its main text shows only "21 †" at this point -- the bare verse number followed immediately by a footnote marker, with no verse content displayed at all, moving straight from verse 20 into verse 22's material. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, in fact more minimal even than ERV's approach: its main text omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 20's content straight into verse 22, with no "21" shown at all. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same fully-omitted style, moving directly from verse 20's content into verse 22 with no "21" shown at all. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against multiple independent sources: its main text shows only "21 * [No text]" -- a bare asterisk marker with no verse content, similar in minimalism to ERV's and BSB's approaches.
- **A middle position, confirmed directly and not previously documented for this entry: print the verse in the main text but enclosed in brackets, marking it as textually uncertain while still including it for the reader:** **AMP**, which reads "[But this kind of demon does not go out except by prayer and fasting.]" -- a genuinely different editorial choice from the clean omission this entry had previously assumed for AMP.
- **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this verse, matching the expectation ("But this kind is not cast out, except through prayer and fasting").
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse. **RSV2CE is now confirmed directly** in the omitting camp above, checked against its own published text at bible-history.com: its main text moves directly from verse 20 into verse 22 with no "21" shown, and its footnote at verse 20 reads "Other ancient authorities insert verse 21, 'But this kind never comes out except by prayer and fasting'" -- confirming the main text omits it. **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: its main text likewise moves directly from verse 20 into verse 22 with no "21" shown, and its footnote reads "Other ancient authorities add verse 21, But this kind does not come out except by prayer and fasting." **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text moves directly from verse 20 into verse 22, and its footnote reads "Some manuscripts add, 'But this kind does not come out except by prayer and fasting'; this is a variant of the better reading of Mk 9:29."

## 4. Matthew 18:11 — "the Son of man is come to save that which was lost"

**The variant:** Following the parable of the lost sheep, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) add a summarizing statement: "For the Son of man is come to save that which was lost." The verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal insertion drawn from the closely parallel statement at Luke 19:10 ("For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost"), where the wording is undisputed.

**Why it matters:** The sentiment itself is thoroughly biblical and undisputed as Luke 19:10's own wording — what's at stake here is narrower: whether Matthew's own text, at this specific point, states it as well, or whether the statement belongs only to Luke's Gospel.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible includes the verse unconditionally ("For the Son of man is come to save that which was lost"), with no bracketing marking it as disputed -- meaning the Vulgate-tradition Catholic translations do not follow the same general pattern of relegating this verse to a footnote or omitting it that most modern critical-text translations do. Also confirmed directly in this unqualified-inclusion camp: **the WEB, Darby, Webster's Bible, YLT,** and **Smith's Literal Translation** ("For the Son of man has come to save the lost"). **The AKJV** would be expected to include it as well, on the same grounds noted above. **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this verse, matching the expectation ("For the Son of man has come to save what had been lost").
- **Omit it from the main text entirely, typically with a footnote noting its absence from the earliest manuscripts and the parallel at Luke 19:10:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NLT. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this camp, again using its characteristic minimal marking: its main text shows only "11 *" at this point, the bare verse number and a footnote marker with no content, the same approach documented at 17:21 above. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its own more minimal style: it omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 10 into verse 12. **The ASV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using the same bare-number style later inherited by WEB: its main text shows only the number "11" with nothing following it. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using the same fully-omitted style as BSB: it moves directly from verse 10 into verse 12 with no "11" shown at all. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against its own text: its main text shows only "11 * [No text]," the same bare-marker pattern already confirmed at Matthew 17:21.
- **A middle position, not previously documented for this entry: print the verse in the main text but enclosed in brackets, marking it as textually uncertain while still including it for the reader:** confirmed directly, **NASB** and **LSB**, both of which print "[For the Son of Man has come to save that which was lost.]" with brackets rather than omitting the verse outright or relegating it to a footnote alone -- a genuinely different editorial choice from ESV/NIV/CSB's cleaner omission. **AMP is also confirmed directly** joining this same bracketed camp, checked against its own text: "[For the Son of Man has come to save that which was lost.]" with a footnote reading "Early mss do not contain this verse."
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse. **RSV2CE is now confirmed directly** in the omitting camp above, checked against its own published text at bible-history.com: its main text moves directly from verse 10 into verse 12 with no "11" shown at all, and its footnote at verse 10 reads "Other ancient authorities add verse 11, For the Son of man came to save the lost" -- the same fully-omitted style already confirmed for BSB and the NET Bible, though diverging from RSV's own bare-marker "[No text]" style at this same verse. **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: its main text likewise moves directly from verse 10 into verse 12 with no "11" shown, and its footnote reads "Other ancient authorities add verse 11, For the Son of Man came to save the lost." **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text moves directly from verse 10 into verse 12, and its footnote reads "Some manuscripts add, 'For the Son of Man has come to save what was lost'; cf. Mt 9:13. This is practically identical with Lk 19:10 and is probably a copyist's addition from that source." **The CJB is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com: its main text at verse 11 reads only the note "Some manuscripts include verse 11: For the Son of Man came to save the lost," moving directly from verse 10 into verse 12 without supplying the verse as actual text.

## Summary observations

- Matthew's most significant variant (6:13) follows a pattern that will recur throughout the New Testament portion of this project: KJV and NKJV, working from the Textus Receptus, retain longer readings that most modern critical-text translations flag as later liturgical or scribal additions. This expansion pass has now confirmed that the KJV-preservation translations (AKJV, Webster's Bible) and the Vulgate-tradition translations (Douay-Rheims, CPDV) fall predictably on opposite sides of this same divide, matching their respective textual traditions.
- The Barabbas case is a good example of the opposite intuition sometimes applying: here, the *shorter, more common* reading is generally considered the later one, because it's more plausible that scribes removed an uncomfortable reading than invented one. This entry did not turn up confirmed data for any of the 17 additional translations, and — unlike the Textus-Receptus-vs-critical-text entries above — there isn't a reliable textual-tradition predictor to reason from here, since even most critical-text translations don't adopt "Jesus Barabbas" into their main text.
