# Judges: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

## Judges 18:30 — was the idolatrous priest's grandfather "Moses," or "Manasseh"?

**The variant:** The Masoretic Text names the priest serving at the idol shrine in Dan as a grandson of "Manasseh" — but a small raised letter *nun* (ן) is inserted mid-word in the traditional Hebrew text, suspended above the line, which most textual scholars read as a scribal device signaling that the name was deliberately altered: remove that one letter, and the name reads "**Moses**" instead. The Septuagint, Vulgate, and some Hebrew manuscripts support the "Moses" reading directly. Most scholars conclude that later scribes found it uncomfortable to have a direct descendant of Moses serving at an idolatrous shrine, and inserted the extra letter specifically to change the name to the more distant, indirect "Manasseh" instead.

**How the translations render it:**
- **"Manasseh" (following the Masoretic Text as pointed):** KJV, NKJV, NASB (its predecessor ASV had read "Moses," but NASB's own translators switched back to "Manasseh"). **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("Jonathan, son of Gershom, son of Manasseh"). **The AKJV is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("Jonathan, the son of Gershom, the son of Manasseh"), matching KJV's wording exactly, as expected given its preservation method. **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("Jonathan, the son of Gershom, the son of Manasseh"), matching KJV's wording exactly. **Young's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("Jonathan son of Gershom, son of Manasseh"). **The CJB is also confirmed directly** in this same camp, checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com: "Y'honatan the son of Gershom, the son of M'nasheh," using its own transliterated spellings but matching the Masoretic "Manasseh" reading.
- **"Moses" (following the Septuagint, Vulgate, and the evident original consonantal reading):** ESV, NIV, CSB, NLT, RSV2CE — most with a footnote explaining the suspended-letter device in the Hebrew. **ASV, Douay-Rheims, and RSV** are all confirmed directly in this camp as well, each reading "Moses" in their main text. Also confirmed directly in this camp: **Darby, the WEB, the NET Bible** (whose own translator's note explicitly states "several ancient textual witnesses, including some LXX mss. and the Vulgate, support the reading 'Moses' here"), **the BSB**, and **the ERV** ("Gershom was the son of Moses," with its own footnote marker acknowledging the textual issue). **The CPDV is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("Jonathan, the son of Gershom, the son of Moses"). **AMP is also confirmed directly** in this camp, checked against its own text at blueletterbible.org: "Jonathan the son of Gershom, the son of [fn]Moses," with a footnote reading "Changed to Manasseh in the Hebrew text." **LSB is also confirmed directly** in this camp, checked against its own text at blueletterbible.org: "the son of Gershom, the son of [fn]Moses," with a footnote reading "As in some Heb mss, LXX, and Vg.; M.T. Manasseh." **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: "Jonathan son of Gershom, son of Moses," with a footnote reading "Another reading is son of Manasseh." **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: "Jonathan, son of Gershom, son of Moses," with a footnote explaining "In the traditional Hebrew text an additional letter has been suspended over the name 'Moses' to alter it to 'Manasseh,' thus protecting Moses from association with idol worship."
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse.

**The difference in meaning:** This is a genealogical claim with real weight — whether the man is a direct descendant of Moses himself (a striking and uncomfortable detail, given the idol-worship context) or of the more obscure "Manasseh." Nearly all modern textual scholars regard "Moses" as the original reading and "Manasseh" as a deliberate, theologically motivated alteration. Worth noting: the ASV's own reading ("Moses") did not survive into its direct descendant NASB, which reverted to "Manasseh" -- a useful reminder that even translations in the same family line can diverge on a specific verse when their editors reach different textual judgments.

## Judges 16:13–14 — a missing sentence in the Delilah loom scene

**The variant:** In the Masoretic Text, Delilah asks Samson how he could be bound, he tells her to weave his hair into a loom, and then — with no transition — the text jumps straight to Delilah doing exactly that, without ever narrating that she actually followed his instructions in between. This is a textbook case of **parablepsis** (a copyist's eye skipping from one occurrence of similar wording to a later one, dropping everything in between): the Septuagint preserves the missing sentence, in which Samson finishes his instructions ("...and fasten it with the pin, then I shall become weak like any other man") and Delilah is narrated as actually doing so before he falls asleep.

**How the translations render it:**
- **Restore the missing material in full, based on the Septuagint:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NLT. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this restoring camp: "If you use the loom to weave the seven braids of hair on my head and tighten it with a pin, I will become as weak as any other man," with verse 14 then narrating Delilah actually doing so, including its own additional detail that Samson pulled up "the tent peg, the loom, and the shuttle" when he awoke -- ERV's fullest and most detailed rendering of the restored Septuagint material among the translations checked in this entry. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this restoring camp: "If you weave the seven braids of my head into the web of a loom and tighten it with a pin, I will become as weak as any other man," with verse 14 narrating Delilah doing exactly that ("Delilah took the seven braids of his hair and wove them into the web... Then she tightened it with a pin"), and Samson awaking to pull "out the pin with the loom and the web." **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this restoring camp: "If you weave the seven braids of my hair into the fabric on the loom and secure it with the pin, I will become weak and be like any other man," with verse 14 narrating Delilah doing exactly that. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this restoring camp, checked against its own text at bible.com: "If you weave the seven locks of my head with the web and make it tight with the pin, then I shall become weak, and be like any other man." **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same restoring camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com, using near-identical wording to its RSV parent: "If you weave the seven locks of my head with the web and make it tight with the pin, then I shall become weak, and be like anyone else," with verse 14 narrating Delilah doing exactly that, and a footnote confirming the restoration: "Compare Gk: in verses 13–14, Heb lacks and make it tight . . . into the web."
- **Restore it but mark the added material as supplied (e.g., in brackets):** NASB.
- **Follow the shorter, elliptical Masoretic text as written, without supplying the missing sentence, using the Hebrew's own "web"/"pin of the beam" weaving imagery:** KJV, NKJV (already established) -- and confirmed directly against the full text of each translation, **the ASV** ("If thou weavest the seven locks of my head with the web... she fastened it with the pin... plucked away the pin of the beam, and with the web"), **the WEB** (World English Bible, using near-identical phrasing), and **Smith's Literal Translation** ("If thou shalt weave the seven braids of my head with the warp... she will fasten with a peg... he will tear away the peg of the braid and the warp" -- its own literal "warp"/"peg" vocabulary, but structurally the same short, unrestored reading as KJV/ASV/WEB). **The AKJV is also confirmed directly** in this camp, matching KJV's wording exactly ("If you weave the seven locks of my head with the web... And she fastened it with the pin... went away with the pin of the beam, and with the web"). **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("If thou weavest the seven locks of my head with the web... went away with the pin of the beam, and with the web"), matching KJV's wording exactly. **Darby is also confirmed directly** in this camp as well ("If thou shouldest weave the seven locks of my head with the web... tore out the pin of the beam, and the web"). **Young's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("If thou weavest the seven locks of my head with the web... journeyeth with the pin of the weaving machine, and with the web"). **AMP is also confirmed directly** in this camp, checked against multiple independent sources: "If you weave the seven braids of [the hair of] my head with the web... went away with the pin of the [weaver's] beam and with the web" -- the same short, unrestored structure, consistent across both the current AMP and its AMPC predecessor.
- **A third, genuinely distinct camp, confirmed directly against the full text of each translation:** **the Douay-Rheims Bible** and **the CPDV** both follow the same short, elliptical structure as the KJV-family camp above (neither restores the missing Septuagint sentence), but describe an entirely different fastening mechanism, reflecting the Vulgate's own distinct rendering of the underlying instrument rather than either the Hebrew's "web and pin of the beam" or the Septuagint's added material. Douay-Rheims reads: "If thou plattest the seven locks of my head with a lace, and tying them round about a nail fastenest it in the ground, I shall be weak... she said to him: The Philistines are upon thee, Samson. And awaking out of his sleep he drew out the nail with the hairs and the lace" -- no loom, web, or beam at all, but braiding ("plattest") into a lace tied to a nail driven into the ground. The CPDV renders this same instrument as "a loom" rather than Douay-Rheims' "a lace," while agreeing with Douay-Rheims' "spike"/"nail" and "the ground" rather than KJV's "pin of the beam": "If you weave the seven locks of my head with a loom, and if you tie these around a spike and fix it to the ground, I will be weak... And when Delilah had done this, she said to him, 'The Philistines are upon you, Samson.'" Despite their difference in the specific word for the weaving instrument itself ("a lace" vs. "a loom"), both translations agree on the ground-fastened spike/nail mechanism and the short, unrestored structure, distinguishing them clearly from the KJV-family "pin of the beam" camp above.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** LSB and RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with TRB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse. **NABRE is now confirmed directly** in the restoring camp above, checked against its own official text at bible.usccb.org: "If you weave the seven locks of my hair into the web and fasten them with the pin, I shall grow weaker and be like anyone else," with verse 14 narrating Delilah doing exactly that: "Delilah took the seven locks of his hair and wove them into the web, and fastened them with the pin." **The CJB is also now confirmed directly** in this same restoring camp, checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com: "If you weave the seven locks of my hair across thread on a loom," with verse 14 narrating Delilah doing exactly that: "she fastened her cloth work in the loom with a pin and wove his hair in."

**The difference in meaning:** Without the restoration, the narrative has an actual gap — Delilah's action is narrated as if Samson's instructions were never repeated back or acted on step-by-step, which reads as abrupt in English. With the restoration, the scene flows as a complete, connected sequence. This is a case where most scholars agree the shorter reading reflects real, identifiable scribal data loss rather than a legitimate stylistic ellipsis.
