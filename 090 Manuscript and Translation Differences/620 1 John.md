# 1 John: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

1 John contains what may be the most historically consequential single textual variant discussed anywhere in this entire project — a case where the manuscript evidence is unusually one-sided, and where the story of how the passage entered the English Bible is as significant as the textual question itself. It's also the one entry in this entire New Testament section where this book's usual Vulgate-tradition prediction runs in the opposite direction from every other case, for a specific, well-documented historical reason explained below.

---

## 1. 1 John 5:7–8 — the "Comma Johanneum" and the three heavenly witnesses

**The variant:** The KJV reads: "For there are three that bear record **in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth,** the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one." Strip out the bolded material (known as the "Comma Johanneum," Latin for roughly "the Johannine clause"), and the verse simply reads: "For there are three that testify: the Spirit and the water and the blood; and these three agree" — a statement about three earthly witnesses to Christ, with no direct mention of the Trinity at all.

This is, by a wide margin, one of the most decisively resolved textual questions in the New Testament. The heavenly-witnesses clause is **absent from every Greek manuscript** of 1 John prior to the 14th century, absent from every ancient version (Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopic, Arabic), and never quoted by any Greek-speaking church father — including theologians who were writing at length about the Trinity and who would have had every reason to cite such a direct proof-text had they known of it. The clause's earliest secure appearance is in Latin, not Greek: it seems to have originated as a fourth-century Latin marginal gloss or homiletical aside, allegorically connecting the Spirit/water/blood of the genuine text to the three persons of the Trinity, which then gradually worked its way from the margin into the actual text of some Old Latin and Vulgate copies. Only much later, apparently by back-translation from Latin into Greek, does it start appearing in a small handful of very late Greek manuscripts (the earliest by most accounts from the 14th–15th century).

The clause's path into English Bibles runs through a specific and well-documented episode: Erasmus, compiling the first published Greek New Testament in 1516, omitted the clause because he could find it in no Greek manuscript. Facing criticism for the omission, he reportedly indicated he would include it if a single Greek manuscript containing it could be produced (the precise nature of any such "promise" is itself disputed by historians of Erasmus, but the outcome is not: a manuscript surfaced — Codex Montfortianus, apparently produced specifically to meet the challenge, likely by back-translating the clause from Latin into Greek) — and Erasmus, true to his word, included the clause in his third edition (1522), reportedly with his own footnote expressing doubt about its authenticity. From there it entered the Textus Receptus and, via that route, the KJV.

**Why it matters:** If genuine, this would be the single most direct and explicit statement of Trinitarian doctrine anywhere in the Bible — naming Father, Word, and Holy Spirit together as one in a single sentence. Its near-total absence from the Greek manuscript tradition, and total absence from patristic citation during the very centuries when Trinitarian doctrine was most fiercely debated and defended, is considered very strong evidence against its originality by virtually all textual scholars today, including many who are otherwise strongly committed to traditional Trinitarian theology — the doctrine of the Trinity itself does not depend on this verse, since it's affirmed on other, textually secure grounds throughout the New Testament (including, some argue, in the surviving, undisputed portion of this very passage's logic).

**Translations — a real split, with the Vulgate tradition on the unexpected side:**
- **Include the full Comma Johanneum:** KJV and NKJV (NKJV includes a footnote acknowledging the clause's absence from most Greek manuscripts, while still printing it as the main text). **YLT and Webster's Bible are both confirmed directly in this camp as well** — a genuinely surprising pair to find alongside KJV/NKJV, since both generally track the Textus Receptus tradition rather than any specifically doctrinal consideration. **Darby is also confirmed directly** including the full clause ("And there are three who give testimony in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost. And these three are one"). **The Douay-Rheims Bible is also confirmed directly including the clause** -- and this is the one case in this entire NT section where the Vulgate-tradition prediction runs the *opposite* direction from every other entry: because the Comma originated in Latin and entered the Vulgate manuscript tradition directly, centuries before reaching Greek at all, Douay-Rheims' inclusion isn't an exception to its usual pattern of siding with the shorter reading -- it's a direct consequence of translating a Latin tradition that itself contains the clause as one of its original sources of transmission, not a late graft onto it the way the Comma is in Greek. **A genuine correction to this entry's prior expectation for the CPDV specifically:** a Catholic Answers Q&A response addressing this exact verse states directly that "in English the only versions to retain it are the King James, the Douay-Rheims, and the Knox. Modern translations all omit it, even those for liturgical use." Since the CPDV (2009) is a modern translation, despite following the same underlying Vulgate that Douay-Rheims translates, it would now be expected to *omit* the clause along with other modern translations rather than include it -- reversing this entry's prior assumption that it would simply follow Douay-Rheims' lineage into inclusion. This was not separately confirmed against the CPDV's own text, but the expectation itself is corrected. **The AKJV** would still be expected to include it, given its KJV-preservation method. **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly including the Comma**, though in a genuinely distinctive structural presentation: SLT merges verses 7 and 8 grammatically, setting the Comma itself off in parentheses in between: "For three are testifying (in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit: and these three are one. 8 And three are testifying in the earth,) the spirit, and the water, and the blood: and the three are for one" -- a unique way of presenting the material not matching any other translation's layout documented in this entry.
- **Omit the Comma Johanneum, following the shorter and near-universally preferred earlier reading:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT, and the rest of the modern critical-text tradition, consistent with the Catholic Answers source above's characterization of "modern translations all omit it." **The WEB is confirmed directly** in this omitting camp ("It is the Spirit who bears witness, because the Spirit is the truth"). **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp: "So there are three witnesses that tell us about Jesus: the Spirit, the water, and the blood," with no mention of the Father, the Word, or their unity in heaven. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp: "For there are three that testify: the Spirit, the water, and the blood—and these three are in agreement," with no mention of the Father, the Word, or their unity in heaven. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, with nearly identical wording to BSB: "For there are three that testify, the Spirit and the water and the blood, and these three are in agreement," with no mention of the Father, the Word, or their unity in heaven.
- **Omit the clause, following the overwhelming Greek manuscript evidence, with a footnote explaining its absence and Latin origin:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, and NLT. **The ASV and the RSV are both confirmed directly in this camp**, consistent with both predating and then explicitly reversing the KJV tradition's inclusion. **The NRSV-CE** would be expected to follow the same critical-text-based omission as its NRSV parent.
- **AMP** renders the shorter reading in the main text.
- **RSV2CE** follows the same pattern as the other modern critical-text translations, omitting the clause with an explanatory note.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** NABRE, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific passage.

## Summary observations

- 1 John 5:7–8 stands out even within this NT-wide project as the case with the most lopsided manuscript evidence of any variant covered — essentially the entire Greek tradition on one side, and a documented, traceable, centuries-later Latin-to-Greek insertion on the other.
- It's also a rare case in this whole project where we can trace, in real historical detail, exactly how and why a specific reading entered a specific English translation tradition (via Erasmus and the pressures on his 1522 edition) — most other variants in this project are questions of ancient scribal transmission, not 16th-century publishing history.
- This is the one entry in this book's entire New Testament section where the Vulgate-tradition translations (Douay-Rheims, and by extension CPDV) side *with* KJV/NKJV rather than against them -- and the reason why is itself instructive: the usual pattern holds only for variants that entered the Byzantine Greek tradition later than the Vulgate's own Old Latin sources. Here, the addition entered Latin first, making the Vulgate tradition the origin point of the variant rather than a witness against a later Greek accretion.
