# Luke: Significant Textual Variants Across 27 Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, Douay-Rheims, CPDV, Darby, Young's Literal, Smith's Literal, Webster's Bible, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

As explained in the companion Matthew document, New Testament translations divide mainly between those based on the later Textus Receptus (KJV, NKJV) and those based on a modern critical text weighted toward the earliest manuscripts (most others), with Vulgate-tradition translations (Douay-Rheims, CPDV) forming a third lineage. Luke contains two of the most emotionally significant variants in this category — both concerning the crucifixion scene.

---

## 1. Luke 22:43–44 — the angel and the sweat like drops of blood

**The variant:** In the account of Jesus praying in Gethsemane, many manuscripts include two verses describing "an angel from heaven, strengthening him," and Jesus in his anguish sweating "like great drops of blood falling down to the ground." These verses are absent from a number of important early manuscripts (including Papyrus 75 and, originally, both Vaticanus and Sinaiticus), and several early church fathers show clear awareness that the verses were contested even in antiquity — some (like Hilary and Jerome) note they were absent from some copies, while others discuss them as genuine. Interestingly, the verses are also stylistically somewhat unusual for Luke (some of the vocabulary and grammatical constructions are considered atypical for this author elsewhere), which some textual critics take as additional evidence they may not be original to this Gospel, whatever their ultimate origin and value.

**Why it matters:** This detail — Jesus visibly sweating blood under extreme anguish, comforted by an angel — has become one of the most vivid and frequently cited images of Christ's suffering in Christian art, devotion, and hymnody (it's the source of the medical term "hematohidrosis," a rare real condition sometimes invoked to argue the detail is physiologically plausible). Its textual status is worth knowing given how central it's become to popular depictions of the Passion.

**A clarification before the split:** the wording of these two verses is essentially identical across every translation that includes them — what differs is whether they're presented as ordinary text or flagged as textually uncertain.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include without qualification:** KJV and NKJV. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would be expected to include the verses unflagged as well, given their general Textus-Receptus-preservation methods discussed in the companion Matthew and Mark entries. **Confirmed directly, and consistent with this expectation, the Douay-Rheims Bible** includes the verses without qualification ("And there appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him. And being in an agony, he prayed the longer"). Also confirmed directly including the verses unflagged: **the ASV, Darby,** and **the WEB**, along with **Smith's Literal Translation** ("And a messenger was seen to him from heaven, strengthening him. And being in a violent struggle, he prayed more intently: and his sweat was as clots of blood coming down upon the earth" -- using "messenger" rather than "angel," consistent with SLT's characteristic word choice at Psalm 8:5 elsewhere in this project). **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including the verses, matching the expectation ("Then an Angel appeared to him from heaven, strengthening him. And being in agony, he prayed more intensely; and so his sweat became like drops of blood, running down to the ground"). **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** including the verses unflagged ("And there appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him. And being in an agony, he prayed more earnestly: and his sweat was as it were great drops of blood falling to the ground").
- **Include but bracketed or footnoted as textually uncertain:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, and LSB. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this bracketed camp, printing both verses in square brackets in its main text ("[Then an angel from heaven appeared to him and strengthened him. And in his anguish he prayed more earnestly, and his sweat was like drops of blood falling to the ground.]"). **AMP is also confirmed directly** joining this same bracketed camp, checked against an independent source quoting its full text: "[Now an angel from heaven appeared to Him, strengthening Him. And being in agony He was praying very fervently; and His sweat became like drops of blood, falling down upon the ground.]" **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same bracketed camp, checked against its own official text at bible.usccb.org: the passage is enclosed in a single asterisk-marked bracket, "*[43And to strengthen him an angel from heaven appeared to him. 44He was in such agony and he prayed so fervently that his sweat became like drops of blood falling on the ground.]"
- **NLT** includes the verses with an explanatory footnote. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this same general pattern: its main text reads the verses in full, ordinary prose ("Then an angel from heaven came to help him. Jesus was full of pain; he struggled hard in prayer. Sweat dripped from his face like drops of blood falling to the ground"), with a footnote marker at the end rather than any bracketing -- closer to NLT's explanatory-footnote approach than to the fully unflagged KJV/Douay-Rheims camp above.
- **RSV2CE** follows the general modern pattern of noting the verses' uncertain status. **The NRSV-CE** would be expected to follow the same pattern. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this same general pattern, reading the verses in full ordinary prose ("Then an angel from heaven appeared to Him and strengthened Him. And in His anguish, He prayed more earnestly, and His sweat became like drops of blood falling to the ground") with a footnote marker at the end.
- **A more extreme position than any of the above, confirmed directly and not previously documented for this entry:** **RSV** omits the verses from its main text entirely, showing only "43 * [No text]" and "44 * [No text]" -- rather than bracketing the verses in place (as ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NET, and AMP do) or including them with a footnote (as NLT, RSV2CE, and BSB do), the RSV's main text shows no verse content at all, relegating the material to a footnote entirely, the same minimal-marking approach already confirmed at Matthew 17:21, Matthew 18:11, Mark 11:26, and Mark 15:28.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific passage.

## 2. Luke 23:34 — "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do"

**The variant:** This is arguably Jesus' single most quoted line from the cross — and it is absent from several of the very earliest and most significant manuscripts, including Papyrus 75 (one of the oldest and most important Luke manuscripts we have) and the original hand of Codex Vaticanus. It is present in Codex Sinaiticus and a great many other manuscripts, both early and late. Because the saying is so widely attested overall and so deeply embedded in Christian tradition, most textual scholars who doubt its originality to Luke's Gospel still don't doubt that it's a genuine and very early saying of Jesus — the debate is specifically about whether Luke's Gospel originally contained it, not about whether Jesus said it. This is a case where serious textual scholarship has explored a specific historical explanation for the omission with unusual depth: some scholars (including Bart Ehrman) argue the verse's absence from certain early manuscripts, including the influential Codex Bezae, reflects deliberate removal amid growing 2nd-century anti-Jewish polemic within parts of the early church -- an uncomfortable prayer of forgiveness, on this theory, for those a scribe held responsible for the crucifixion.

**Why it matters:** Beyond the textual question itself, the removal theory above illustrates something important about textual criticism generally: manuscript variants sometimes preserve traces of the social and theological pressures of the communities that copied them, not just innocent copying errors.

**A clarification before the split:** the wording of this line is identical across every translation that includes it — what differs is only whether it's flagged as textually uncertain.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include without qualification:** KJV, NKJV, and AMP. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would be expected to include it unflagged as well, on the same grounds noted above. **Confirmed directly, and consistent with this expectation, the Douay-Rheims Bible** includes the verse without qualification ("And Jesus said: Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do"). Also confirmed directly including it unflagged: **the ASV, Darby,** and **the CJB** ("Yeshua said, 'Father, forgive them; they don't understand what they are doing'"), along with **Smith's Literal Translation** ("And Jesus said, Father, let them go; for they know not what they do"). **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this unflagged-inclusion camp ("Jesus said, 'Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing'"), matching the standard "forgive them" wording. SLT's wording is worth flagging as a small exception to this entry's earlier claim that the wording is identical across every translation that includes it -- SLT renders the verb "let them go" rather than "forgive them," even though it includes the verse itself in the same unflagged position as the rest of this camp. **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** in this unflagged-inclusion camp ("Then said Jesus, Father, forgive them: for they know not what they do"), matching the standard "forgive them" wording. **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including the verse, matching the expectation ("Then Jesus said, 'Father, forgive them. For they know not what they do'").
- **Include but flagged (bracketed, footnoted, or both) as absent from important early manuscripts:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, and LSB. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this bracketed camp ("[But Jesus said, 'Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing.']"). **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same bracketed camp, checked against its own official text at bible.usccb.org: "[Then Jesus said, 'Father, forgive them, they know not what they do.']" with an asterisk footnote marker.
- **NLT** includes the line with an explanatory footnote. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this same pattern: "Jesus said, 'Father, forgive them. They don't know what they are doing.'" with a footnote marker, ordinary prose in the main text rather than brackets. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this pattern: "Then Jesus said, 'Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing,'" with a footnote marker. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this same pattern, checked against multiple independent sources: its main text reads the line in full, ordinary prose, unbracketed ("And Jesus said, 'Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.'"), accompanied by a footnote noting "Other ancient authorities omit the sentence."
- **RSV2CE** includes the line, generally footnoted per its typical practice for contested passages of this kind. **The NRSV-CE** would be expected to follow the same pattern.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse.

## 3. Luke 9:55–56 — "the Son of man is not come to destroy men's lives"

**The variant:** After James and John ask Jesus to call down fire on a Samaritan village that rejected him, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) have Jesus rebuke them at greater length: "and said, Ye know not what manner of spirit ye are of. For the Son of man is not come to destroy men's lives, but to save them." The earliest manuscripts read much more briefly: "he turned, and rebuked them" — without specifying what he said. Most textual scholars regard the longer reading as an early expansion, filling in plausible content for a rebuke the shorter text leaves unstated.

**Why it matters:** The sentiment fits Jesus' character as presented throughout the Gospels, but the specific saying rests on a notably less secure textual foundation than most of his recorded words — worth knowing for anyone treating this exact wording as a direct quotation.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the longer rebuke in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible includes this material as well, in its own wording ("The Son of man came not to destroy souls, but to save"), with no bracketing -- the fourth confirmed instance in this project's set of disputed-verse entries (see also Matthew 18:11 and the two Mark entries above) of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, a passage absent from the earliest manuscripts. Also confirmed directly including the longer rebuke: **the WEB** ("For the Son of Man didn't come to destroy men's lives, but to save them"), **YLT** ("for the Son of Man did not come to destroy men's lives, but to save"), and **Smith's Literal Translation** ("For the Son of man came not to destroy men's souls, but to save them"). **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("For the Son of man is not come to destroy men's lives, but to save them"), matching KJV's wording exactly. **The AKJV** would be expected to include it, on the same Textus-Receptus-preservation grounds discussed throughout this NT section. **AMP is also confirmed directly** in this camp, checked against its own text: "But He turned and rebuked them and He said, 'You do not know what kind of spirit you are...'" -- included without brackets, unlike NASB and LSB's bracketed middle position documented below.
- **Read the shorter text, typically with a footnote noting that some manuscripts add the longer material:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB. **LSB** prints the longer material in brackets rather than omitting it outright ("for the Son of Man did not come to destroy men's lives, but to save them.]"), the same middle position documented at several companion entries in Matthew and Mark; **the CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this passage, matching the expectation ("The Son of man came, not to destroy lives, but to save them"). Confirmed directly reading the shorter text (no longer rebuke at all): **the ASV** ("And they went to another village," with no preceding rebuke content) and, notably, **Darby**, which uses the identical shorter phrasing -- a genuine departure from Darby's own pattern of including comparable disputed material elsewhere in this project's Mark and Matthew entries, underscoring again that even a single translation's editorial choices must be checked verse by verse rather than assumed to run uniformly. **NLT is now confirmed directly** in this same shorter-text camp, checked against multiple independent sources: "But Jesus turned and rebuked them. So they went on to another village," with the longer material relegated to a footnote. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp: "But Jesus turned and criticized them for saying this. Then he and his followers went to another town," with a footnote marker but no longer rebuke content. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp: "But Jesus turned and rebuked them. And He and His disciples went on to another village," with a footnote marker but no longer rebuke content. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp: "But Jesus turned and rebuked them, and they went on to another village," with no footnote marker at all shown at this point.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse. **RSV is now confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp, checked against multiple independent sources: "But he turned and rebuked them. And they went on to another village." **RSV2CE is also confirmed directly** in this same shorter-text camp, checked against its own published text at bible-history.com: "But he turned and rebuked them.[f] And they went on to another village" -- identical wording to its RSV parent, with no longer rebuke content in the main text. **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same shorter-text camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: "But he turned and rebuked them. Then they went on to another village," with a footnote noting that other ancient authorities add the longer rebuke. **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same shorter-text camp, checked against its own official text at bible.usccb.org: "Jesus turned and rebuked them, and they journeyed to another village."

## 4. Luke 17:36 — a missing verse in the account of the Second Coming

**The variant:** Describing the suddenness of his return, Jesus warns that two people will be side by side and only one taken — given for a bedroom (17:34) and a mill (17:35). A number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) add a third such pairing: "Two men shall be in the field; the one shall be taken, and the other left." The verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal insertion drawn from the parallel list at Matthew 24:40, where the "field" pairing is undisputed.

**Why it matters:** The teaching itself is undisputed as Matthew's own wording — the question here is whether Luke's Gospel also includes this third pairing, or whether it belongs only to Matthew's version of the same discourse.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **Confirmed directly, with a genuine nuance**, the Douay-Rheims Bible also includes this same "field" pairing content -- but folds it into the end of its own verse 35 rather than presenting it as a separately numbered verse 36, reflecting the Vulgate's own distinct versification at this point (Douay-Rheims' verse 35 reads, in full: "Two women shall be grinding together: the one shall be taken, and the other shall be left: two men shall be in the field; the one shall be taken, and the other shall be left," with the chapter then proceeding directly to verse 37). The underlying content is present either way; only the verse numbering differs. Also confirmed directly including it as their own separately numbered verse 36: **YLT** ("Two men shall be in the field; the one shall be taken, and the other left"), **the ASV** ("There shall be two men in the field; the one shall be taken, and the other shall be left"), and **Webster's Bible** ("Two men will be in the field; the one will be taken, and the other left"). **The AKJV** would be expected to include it, on the same grounds noted throughout this section.
- **A middle-ground approach, confirmed directly and not previously documented for this entry:** **Darby** includes the verse but marks it as textually uncertain with square brackets around the entire verse in its main text: "[Two men shall be in the field; the one shall be seized and the other let go.]" -- neither fully unflagged inclusion nor omission, but inclusion with an explicit typographic signal of doubt, similar to NASB's own bracketing approach at other disputed verses elsewhere in this project. **AMP is also confirmed directly** joining this same bracketed middle position, checked against its own text at bible.com: "[Two men will be in the field; one will be taken and the other will be left.]," with a footnote marker on the verse.
- **Omit it from the main text, typically with a footnote noting its absence from the earliest manuscripts and the parallel at Matthew 24:40:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT. **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly in this omitting camp, in a genuinely striking way**: rather than silently skipping the verse number or relegating a note to the margin, SLT prints the verse number with a bracketed editorial comment directly in the main text in place of any content: "36 [Verse 36 is wanting (Missing) in most of the Greek copies.]" -- an unusually transparent, self-documenting way of flagging the omission, distinct from every other omission-camp translation surveyed in this entry. **The CPDV**, given its own general reliance on the Vulgate (which does contain this content, as Douay-Rheims' text confirms, albeit differently numbered), would now be expected to include this material as well, in whatever verse-numbering convention it follows, reversing this entry's prior expectation that it would side with the shorter reading. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its own characteristic minimal marking: its main text shows only "36 §" with no verse content, moving directly from verse 35 into verse 37. **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, in fact more minimal still than ERV's own approach: its main text shows only the bare number "36" with absolutely nothing following it -- not even a footnote marker -- moving directly from verse 35 into verse 37. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, going even further still: it omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 35 into verse 37 with no "36" shown at all. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same fully-omitted style, moving directly from verse 35 into verse 37 with no "36" shown at all.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** RSV2CE (as the original entry already noted), along with RSV, TRB, and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse. **NRSV-CE is now confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: its main text moves directly from verse 35 into verse 37, with no "36" shown at all, and its footnote reads "Other ancient authorities add verse 36, 'Two will be in the field; one will be taken and the other left.'" **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text moves directly from verse 35 into a bracketed "[36]" with no content, and its footnote reads "The inclusion of Lk 17:36, 'There will be two men in the field; one will be taken, the other left behind,' in some Western manuscripts appears to be a scribal assimilation to Mt 24:40."

## 5. Luke 23:17 — "of necessity he must release one unto them"

**The variant:** In the account of Barabbas's release, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) include an explanatory verse: "For of necessity he must release one unto them at the feast." The verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal insertion drawn from the parallel explanation of the Passover custom found at Matthew 27:15 and Mark 15:6, where it is undisputed.

**Why it matters:** The custom of a Passover release is undisputed as recorded in Matthew and Mark — the question here is narrower, whether Luke's own account also states the custom explicitly at this point, or moves directly from the crowd's demand to Pilate's response without it.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **Confirmed directly**, the Douay-Rheims Bible includes this verse as well ("Now of necessity he was to release unto them one upon the feast day") -- the fifth confirmed instance in this project's set of disputed-verse entries (see also Matthew 18:11 and the entries above in Mark and Luke) of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, a verse absent from the earliest manuscripts. **Darby is also confirmed directly** using this same wording. **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp, though presented parenthetically ("(And he had necessity to loose one at the festival.)"). **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("Now he had to release one prisoner to them at the feast"). **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** in this camp, also presented parenthetically like SLT ("(For of necessity he must release one to them at the feast.)"). **The AKJV and YLT** would be expected to include it, on the same grounds noted throughout this section.
- **Omit it from the main text, typically with a footnote noting its absence from the earliest manuscripts and the parallels at Matthew 27:15 and Mark 15:6:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT. **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this verse, matching the expectation ("Now he was required to release one person for them on the feast day"). **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its characteristic minimal marking: its main text shows only "17 *" with no verse content. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its own more minimal style: it omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 16 into verse 18. **The ASV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using the same bare-number style: its main text shows only the number "17" with nothing following it. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same fully-omitted style, moving directly from verse 16 into verse 18 with no "17" shown at all. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against an independent source specifically surveying which translations contain this verse, which lists RSV alongside CSB, NIV, NLT, ESV, and CEV among the versions omitting it entirely.
- **A middle position, confirmed directly and not previously documented for this entry: print the verse in brackets rather than omitting it outright:** **AMP**, which reads "[Now he was obligated to release to them one prisoner at the Feast.]" -- a genuinely different editorial choice from ESV/NIV/CSB/NASB's cleaner omission, joining the bracketed-inclusion pattern documented at several companion entries elsewhere in this project.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific verse. **RSV2CE is also now confirmed directly** in the omitting camp above, checked against its own published text at biblia.com: its main text moves directly from verse 16 into verse 18 with no verse 17 shown at all, matching its RSV parent's own omission. **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: its main text likewise moves directly from verse 16 into verse 18 with no "17" shown, and its footnote reads "Here, or after verse 19, other ancient authorities add verse 17, Now he was obliged to release someone for them at the festival." **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its footnote states "This verse, 'He was obliged to release one prisoner for them at the festival,' is not part of the original text of Luke. It is an explanatory gloss from Mk 15:6 (also Mt 27:15) and is not found in many early and important Greek manuscripts."

## Summary observations

- Luke's two headline cases (22:43–44 and 23:34) both involve some of the most theologically and devotionally weighty material in the Passion narrative, and both show the same basic split pattern seen throughout this NT project: KJV/NKJV retain the material without flagging it, while most other modern translations retain it but mark its textual uncertainty.
- Worth noting for both: even textual scholars who doubt these specific verses' place in Luke's original text generally don't doubt the underlying historical plausibility or theological soundness of what they describe — the question is narrowly about manuscript transmission, not about core Christian teaching.
- The reasoned expectations for Douay-Rheims and the CPDV in this file are calibrated differently depending on the type of variant: for early, widely-attested readings like 22:43-44 and 23:34, the Vulgate-tradition prediction is less certain (both sides of the divide have serious ancient support); for later scribal-harmonization-type additions like entries 3-5, the Vulgate-tradition prediction (siding with the shorter, critical-text reading) follows the same confident pattern established in the Matthew and Mark entries.
