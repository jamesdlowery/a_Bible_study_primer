# Acts: Textual and Translation Differences

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE, ASV, WEB, DR, CPDV, Darby, YLT, SLT, WBT, AKJV, NRSV-CE, RSV, NABRE, ERV, NET, BSB, TRB, CJB

As explained in the Gospels documents in this collection, New Testament translations divide mainly between those based on the later Textus Receptus (KJV, NKJV) and those based on a modern critical text weighted toward the earliest manuscripts (most others), with Vulgate-tradition translations (Douay-Rheims, CPDV) forming a third lineage. Acts has an additional wrinkle beyond this: its "Western text" (represented especially by the 5th-century Codex Bezae) is considerably longer than the standard text in numerous places throughout the book — sometimes by as much as 10% overall — making Acts one of the most textually complex books in the New Testament, even though most of those Western expansions are minor narrative details rather than major single variants.

## 1. Acts 8:37 — the Ethiopian eunuch's confession of faith

**The variant:** In the well-known story of Philip and the Ethiopian eunuch, one reading has the eunuch, upon seeing water, ask to be baptized, prompting Philip to say "If you believe with all your heart, you may," and the eunuch replying, "I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God" — after which they go down into the water together. This exchange is entirely absent from the earliest and most significant manuscripts of Acts, including Papyrus 45 (3rd century), Papyrus 74, Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, and Codex Alexandrinus. The earliest Greek manuscript that does contain it is Codex Laudianus, from the 6th century. However, the substance of the confession is quoted by name as early as around 180 AD by Irenaeus, and referenced by Cyprian around 250 AD — meaning the tradition behind the verse is genuinely ancient, even though its insertion into the continuous Greek text of Acts appears to be later. Most textual scholars conclude the line originated as an early baptismal formula (something a new convert would recite as part of the baptism ceremony itself), which was then written into the margin of some copies of Acts as an explanatory note — since the plain narrative, without it, has the eunuch asking to be baptized without ever voicing an explicit statement of faith, which apparently struck at least one early scribe as needing to be supplied.

**Why it matters:** Beyond textual-critical interest, this verse has had outsized influence on later ideas of how baptism ought to be practiced (many groups have used its wording as a model baptismal confession), even though most scholars regard the sentence as a later addition to Acts rather than Luke's own writing.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include without qualification:** KJV and NKJV. **The AKJV and YLT** would be expected to include it unflagged as well, on the same Textus-Receptus-preservation grounds discussed throughout this NT section. Also confirmed directly including it unflagged: **the ASV** and **Darby** ("And Philip said: If thou believest with all thy heart, thou mayest. And he answering, said: I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God"), along with **Smith's Literal Translation** ("If thou believest from the whole heart, it is lawful... I believe the Son of God to be Jesus Christ" -- a notably reversed word order compared to most other translations, closer to the underlying Greek's own ordering) and **Webster's Bible**, confirmed directly using wording identical to Darby's ("And Philip said, If thou believest with all thy heart, thou mayest. And he answered and said, I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God").
- **Include but bracketed and footnoted as absent from early manuscripts:** NASB and AMP.
- **Omit from the main text entirely, moving straight from verse 36 to verse 38, generally with a footnote on the alternative:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NLT, and LSB. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its characteristic minimal marking: its main text shows only "37 ‡" with no verse content, moving directly from verse 36 into verse 38. **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using an even more minimal style than ERV's: its main text shows only the bare number "37" with absolutely nothing following it -- not even a footnote marker. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, going even further still: it omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 36 into verse 38. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same fully-omitted style, moving directly from verse 36 into verse 38 with no "37" shown at all. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against its own text: its main text shows only "37 * [No text]," matching the same bare-marker pattern already confirmed at several companion entries throughout this project.
- **Include without qualification, alongside the KJV-tradition translations:** confirmed directly, **the Douay-Rheims Bible** ("And Philip said: If thou believest with all thy heart, thou mayest. And he answering, said: I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God"), which includes this verse in full, unbracketed -- explicitly noted by at least one Catholic commentator addressing this exact question ("the Catholic Douay-Rheims New Testament includes Acts 8:37"). This is the seventh confirmed instance in this project's set of disputed-verse entries of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, material absent from the earliest manuscripts (see the companion Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John entries elsewhere in this document set). **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this verse, matching the expectation ("Then Philip said, 'If you believe from your whole heart, it is permitted.' And he responded by saying, 'I believe the Son of God to be Jesus the Christ'") -- interestingly using the same reversed word order as Darby and Smith's Literal Translation ("the Son of God" named before "Jesus").
- **RSV2CE** follows the same pattern as most modern critical-text translations, omitting the verse from the main text with a footnote.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse. **RSV is now confirmed directly** in the omitting camp above, checked against its own text at biblestudytools.com: its main text shows only "* [No text]," the same bare-marker pattern already confirmed at Matthew 17:21 and other companion entries throughout this project. **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: its main text moves directly from verse 36 into verse 38 with no "37" shown, and its footnote reads "Other ancient authorities add all or most of verse 37, 'And Philip said, "If you believe with all your heart, you may." And he replied, "I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God."'" **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text shows only a bare "37*" marker with no content before moving to verse 38, and its footnote reads "The oldest and best manuscripts of Acts omit this verse, which is a Western text reading: 'And Philip said, "If you believe with all your heart, you may." And he said in reply, "I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God."'" **The CJB is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com: its main text moves directly from verse 36 into verse 38 with no "37" appearing at all -- not even as a bare marker or manuscript note, a notably cleaner omission than CJB's usual "Some manuscripts have verse X" convention seen at other disputed verses in this project.

## 2. Acts 15:34 — "it pleased Silas to abide there still"

**The variant:** After Paul and Barnabas, along with Judas and Silas, deliver the Jerusalem council's letter to Antioch, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) add a note: "Notwithstanding it pleased Silas to abide there still." The verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal addition, supplied to explain how Silas came to be back in Antioch a few verses later (15:40) when Paul chooses him as a travel companion, after 15:33 had just described Judas and Silas being sent back to Jerusalem.

**Why it matters:** Without this verse, the text moves from Judas and Silas returning to Jerusalem (15:33) directly to Paul selecting Silas for the next journey (15:40) without explaining how Silas came to be available in Antioch again — a minor narrative gap the added verse exists specifically to fill.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **The AKJV** would be expected to include it, on the same grounds noted throughout this section. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible also includes this verse ("But it seemed good unto Silas to remain there; and Judas alone departed to Jerusalem") -- the eleventh confirmed instance in this project's set of disputed-verse entries of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, material absent from the earliest manuscripts. **YLT is also confirmed directly** including the verse ("and it seemed good to Silas to remain there still"). **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** including it ("And it seemed fitting to Silas to wait for him"). **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** including it, matching KJV's wording exactly ("Notwithstanding, it pleased Silas to abide there still"). **AMP is also confirmed directly** in this unqualified-inclusion camp, checked against multiple independent sources: "However, Silas decided to stay there."
- **Omit it from the main text, typically with a footnote explaining the apparent gap it was added to smooth over:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its characteristic minimal marking: its main text shows only "34 ‡" with no verse content. **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, again using its own even more minimal style: its main text shows only the bare number "34" with nothing following it. **The ASV is also confirmed directly** in this same bare-number omitting style. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, going even further: it omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 33 into verse 35. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same fully-omitted style, moving directly from verse 33 into verse 35 with no "34" shown at all. **Darby is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using the same bare-number style as ASV/WEB -- a genuinely notable departure from Darby's own pattern of including disputed Textus-Receptus material documented at multiple other verses elsewhere in this project (e.g. Matthew 17:21, Mark 9:29, Mark 11:26), showing Darby's own textual choices are made verse by verse rather than following one fixed rule. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against its own text: its main text shows only "34 * [No text]," matching the same bare-marker pattern already confirmed at several companion entries throughout this project.
- **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this verse, matching the expectation ("But it seemed good to Silas to remain there. So Judas alone departed to Jerusalem").
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse. **RSV2CE is now confirmed directly** in the omitting camp above, checked against its own published text at bible-history.com: its main text moves directly from verse 33 into verse 35, with no verse 34 shown at all, and its footnote at verse 33 reads "Other ancient authorities insert verse 34, But it seemed good to Silas to remain there" -- confirming its omission, using the fully-omitted style rather than RSV's own bare-marker convention at other companion entries. **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: its main text likewise moves directly from verse 33 into verse 35 with no "34" shown, and its footnote reads "Other ancient authorities add verse 34, But it seemed good to Silas to remain there." **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text moves directly from verse 33 into verse 35 with no "34" shown at all, and its footnote reads "Some manuscripts add, in various wordings, 'But Silas decided to remain there.'" **The CJB is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com: its verse 34 appears as a bare number with no text at all, moving straight from verse 33 into verse 35 -- a bare-marker omission style like RSV's, rather than CJB's more usual "Some manuscripts have verse X" note seen elsewhere.

## 3. Acts 24:6–8 — how Paul came to be brought before Felix

**The variant:** In the prosecuting attorney Tertullus's account before Felix of Paul's arrest, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) include additional material describing the Roman tribune's intervention: "and would have judged according to our law. But the chief captain Lysias came upon us, and with great violence took him away out of our hands, commanding his accusers to come unto thee." This material is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal expansion, filling in narrative detail consistent with (though not directly copied from) the tribune Lysias's own letter quoted earlier at 23:27–30.

**Why it matters:** The added material doesn't introduce new facts in dispute elsewhere in Acts — it recaps Lysias's role largely consistent with the earlier chapter — but its absence from the earliest manuscripts affects how much of Tertullus's specific recorded speech is treated as original to this passage versus a later, harmonizing expansion.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the fuller material in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **The AKJV and YLT** would be expected to include it, on the same grounds noted throughout this section. **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly**, matching KJV's wording exactly ("the chief captain Lysias came and with great violence took him out of our hands, Commanding his accusers to come to thee"). **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, both the Douay-Rheims Bible ("But Lysias the tribune coming upon us, with great violence took him away out of our hands; commanding his accusers to come to thee") and, notably, **the CPDV itself** ("But Lysias, the tribune, overwhelming us with great violence, snatched him away from our hands, ordering his accusers to come to you") include this material -- a rare case in this project where the CPDV's own text was directly confirmed rather than merely inferred from Douay-Rheims' pattern. This is the twelfth confirmed instance of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, material absent from the earliest manuscripts. **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("And Lysias the captain of a thousand, having come with much force, carried away out of our hands, Having commanded his accusers to come to thee"). **Darby is also confirmed directly** including the material, though again marked with square brackets around the disputed span in its main text: "[and would have judged according to our law; but Lysias, the chiliarch, coming up, took [him] away with great force out of our hands, having commanded his accusers to come to thee;]" -- the same bracketing convention Darby uses at Luke 17:36 elsewhere in this project. **AMP is also confirmed directly** joining Darby's same bracketed-inclusion approach, checked against an independent source: "He even tried to desecrate the temple, but we took him into custody and we intended to judge him by our Law, [But Lysias the commander came along, and with much violence took him out of our hands...]"
- **Read the shorter text, typically with a footnote noting the longer reading in some manuscripts:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp, combining verses 6-8 into its own condensed paraphrase with a footnote marker: "Also, he was trying to make the Temple unclean, but we stopped him. You can decide if all this is true. Ask him some questions yourself" -- with no "Lysias" material at all. **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp, using a distinctive mixed presentation: verse 6 is present but shorter ("He even tried to profane the temple, and we arrested him"), verse 7 shows only the bare number "7" with nothing following it (WEB's characteristic minimal-omission style documented elsewhere in this project), and verse 8 is present but shorter as well ("By examining him yourself you may ascertain all these things of which we accuse him") -- with no "Lysias" material anywhere in the passage, consistent in substance with the other shorter-text translations even though its verse-by-verse presentation is unique. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp, going even further than WEB's presentation: verse 6 is present but shorter ("he even tried to desecrate the temple; so we seized him"), and the text then skips directly to verse 8 with no verse "7" shown at all, verse 8 itself also shorter ("By examining him yourself, you will be able to learn the truth about all our charges against him") -- again with no "Lysias" material anywhere in the passage. **The ASV is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp, with the same mixed presentation as WEB: verse 6 present but shorter ("who moreover assayed to profane the temple: on whom also we laid hold"), verse 7 shown as the bare number "7" with nothing following it, and verse 8 present but shorter ("from whom thou wilt be able, by examining him thyself, to take knowledge of all these things whereof we accuse him") -- again with no "Lysias" material anywhere in the passage. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp, matching BSB's fully-omitted style exactly: verse 6 is present but shorter ("He even tried to desecrate the temple, so we arrested him"), and the text then skips directly to verse 8 with no "24:7" shown at all, verse 8 itself also shorter ("When you examine him yourself, you will be able to learn from him about all these things we are accusing him of doing") -- again with no "Lysias" material anywhere in the passage. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this shorter-text camp, checked against its own text: "He even tried to profane the temple, but we seized him," with no "Lysias" material following.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB and the CJB were not checked against their own text for this specific passage. **RSV2CE is now confirmed directly** in the shorter-text camp above, checked against its own published text at bible-history.com: "He even tried to profane the temple, but we seized him.[a]... 8 By examining him yourself you will be able to learn from him about everything of which we accuse him," with no "Lysias" material anywhere in the passage, and a footnote reading "Other ancient authorities add and we would have judged him according to our law. 7 But the chief captain Lysias came and with great violence took him out of our hands, 8 commanding his accusers to come before you" -- matching its RSV parent's own shorter reading exactly. **NRSV-CE is also now confirmed directly** in this same shorter-text camp, checked against its own published text at biblehub.com: "He even tried to profane the temple, and so we seized him.[d]... 8By examining him yourself you will be able to learn from him concerning everything of which we accuse him," moving directly from verse 6 to verse 8 with no Lysias material. **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same shorter-text camp, checked against its own official text at bible.usccb.org: "6He even tried to desecrate our temple, but we arrested him.b 7* 8If you examine him you will be able to learn from him for yourself about everything of which we are accusing him," moving directly from verse 6 through a bare "7*" marker to verse 8 with no Lysias material.

## 4. Acts 28:29 — a closing verse after Paul's appeal to the Jewish leaders in Rome

**The variant:** After Paul's extended appeal to the Jewish leaders in Rome ends with a citation of Isaiah 6:9–10 and the statement that God's salvation has been sent to the Gentiles, a number of later Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus) add a closing narrative note: "And when he had said these words, the Jews departed, and had great reasoning among themselves." The verse is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and most textual scholars regard it as a scribal addition, supplying a natural-sounding transition out of the scene before the book's actual final two verses (28:30–31), which are undisputed.

**Why it matters:** The added verse doesn't change anything substantive about Paul's message or the book's ending — Acts closes either way with Paul preaching under house arrest in Rome — but affects how the transition out of this specific scene is worded.

**Translations — a real split:**
- **Include the verse in the main text, without qualification:** KJV, NKJV. **The AKJV, Webster's Bible, and YLT** would be expected to include it, on the same grounds noted throughout this section. **Confirmed directly, and correcting this entry's prior assumption**, the Douay-Rheims Bible includes this verse as well -- the thirteenth confirmed instance of Douay-Rheims including, rather than omitting, material absent from the earliest manuscripts. **A correction to this entry's own prior quotation of Douay-Rheims' wording:** a direct check against the full Douay-Rheims text shows it actually reads "And when he had said these things, the Jews went out from him, having much reasoning among themselves" -- not the "departed... a great dispute" wording this entry had previously (and incorrectly) attributed to it. **Darby is also confirmed directly** including it, using this same exact wording as Douay-Rheims' verified text. **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** including it ("And he having said these things, the Jews departed, having much discussion in themselves"). **The WEB is also confirmed directly** including it as well, in fact using the wording this entry had previously mislabeled as Douay-Rheims': "When he had said these words, the Jews departed, having a great dispute among themselves." **Webster's Bible is also confirmed directly** including it, closely matching KJV's own wording ("And when he had said these words, the Jews departed, and had great reasoning among themselves"). **AMP is also confirmed directly** in this unqualified-inclusion camp, checked against an independent source: "And when he had said these things, the Jews went away, arguing and disputing among themselves."
- **Omit it from the main text, moving directly from Paul's Isaiah citation to the book's closing summary (28:30–31), typically with a footnote noting the addition in some manuscripts:** ESV, NIV, CSB, NASB, LSB, NLT. **The ERV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using its characteristic minimal marking: its main text shows only "29 *" with no verse content, moving directly into verse 30. **The BSB is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, going even further: it omits the verse number entirely, moving directly from verse 28 into verse 30. **The ASV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, using the same bare-number style: its main text shows only the number "29" with nothing following it. **The NET Bible is also confirmed directly** in this same fully-omitted style, moving directly from verse 28 into verse 30 with no "29" shown at all. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this omitting camp, checked against its own text: its main text moves directly from verse 28 to verse 31, with no verse 29 content shown at all.
- **The CPDV is now confirmed directly** including this verse, matching the expectation ("And when he had said these things, the Jews went away from him, though they still had many questions among themselves").
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse. **RSV2CE is now confirmed directly** in the omitting camp above, checked against its own published text at bible-history.com: its main text moves directly from verse 28 into verse 30, with no verse 29 shown at all, and its footnote at verse 28 reads "Other ancient authorities add verse 29, And when he had said these words, the Jews departed, holding much dispute among themselves" -- matching its RSV parent's own omission of this verse exactly. **The NRSV, and by direct extension NRSV-CE, which follows the NRSV's New Testament wording at this kind of disputed verse throughout this project's other entries, is also confirmed in this same omitting camp**, checked against the NRSV's own published text: its main text likewise moves directly from verse 28 into verse 30, with a footnote reading "Other ancient authorities add verse 29, And when he had said these words, the Jews departed, arguing vigorously among themselves." **NABRE is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own official text and footnote at bible.usccb.org: its main text shows only a bare "29*" marker with no content, moving directly to verse 30, and its footnote reads "The Western text has added here a verse that is not found in the best Greek manuscripts: 'And when he had said this, the Jews left, seriously arguing among themselves.'" **The CJB is also now confirmed directly** in this same omitting camp, checked against its own published text at biblestudytools.com: its verse 29 appears as a bare number with no text at all, moving straight from verse 28 into verse 30 -- the same bare-marker omission style already confirmed at Acts 15:34.

## Summary observations

- Acts 8:37 fits the now-familiar pattern from the Gospels: KJV/NKJV retain a passage absent from the earliest manuscripts, while most modern translations flag or omit it — though NASB's practice of retaining it in brackets, rather than dropping it outright, is a useful middle case to be aware of.
- Acts as a whole is worth knowing as an unusually textually complex book, given the Western text's extensive (though mostly minor) expansions throughout — a feature not paralleled to the same degree in most other New Testament books.
