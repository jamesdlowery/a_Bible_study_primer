# Lamentations: Significant Textual Variants Across Ten Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE

Lamentations is textually one of the calmer books in the Old Testament — two fragmentary copies exist among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and they generally confirm the Masoretic Text rather than diverging from it in the dramatic ways seen in Samuel or Jeremiah. Its most distinctive textual feature isn't a wording variant at all, but a structural one built into the book's acrostic design.

---

## 1. The pe-before-ayin puzzle — a scrambled alphabet, confirmed by an ancient inscription

**The variant:** Four of Lamentations' five chapters are acrostics — each verse (or, in chapter 3, each group of three verses) begins with a successive letter of the 22-letter Hebrew alphabet, in order. But in chapters 2, 3, and 4, the letter **pe (פ)** appears *before* the letter **ayin (ע)** — the reverse of the standard alphabetical order used everywhere else in the Hebrew Bible (including Lamentations' own chapter 1, and the acrostic Psalm 119). This isn't a copying accident isolated to a single manuscript — it's consistent across the entire Masoretic tradition for these three chapters, meaning it reflects something real about the text's original composition or a very early, thoroughgoing standardization, not a later scribal slip.

For a long time this looked like an unexplained oddity unique to Lamentations. That changed with the discovery of abecedaries (practice inscriptions listing the alphabet in order) from Ugarit and other sites in the ancient Near East, some of which show a **pe-before-ayin** letter order in use during the same general era — evidence that this wasn't an error at all, but a genuine, if less common, ordering of the Hebrew/Canaanite alphabet that existed alongside the more familiar one, and that the poet(s) of Lamentations 2–4 were simply working from. Fragments of Lamentations found among the Dead Sea Scrolls are consistent with the Masoretic Text's chapter-by-chapter ordering, including this pe/ayin reversal, showing the feature is both ancient and stable rather than a late medieval Masoretic quirk.

**Why it matters:** This is a case where a feature that looks at first like a mistake turns out to be independently corroborated by outside archaeological evidence entirely unconnected to the Bible's own transmission — a nice example of how textual criticism sometimes vindicates an unusual reading rather than correcting it.

**Translations:** This is a structural feature of the underlying Hebrew, not a translation choice -- English word order can't reproduce a Hebrew acrostic anyway, so none of the ten tracked translations attempts to preserve or flag the pe/ayin ordering in the English text itself. A few study editions (some printings of the NASB and ESV among them) include a translator's note explaining the acrostic structure and this specific reversal, but it doesn't affect the wording of the English text in any of the ten.

---

## Summary observations

- Lamentations is a good reminder that not every noteworthy textual feature is a "variant" in the wording sense — sometimes the interesting question is about a book's underlying structure, confirmed not by comparing Hebrew manuscripts against each other but by outside archaeological evidence like Ugaritic abecedaries.
- Because the book is comparatively stable in the manuscript record (no dramatic MT-vs-LXX split on the scale of Jeremiah or Samuel), there isn't a translation-splitting variant to report here at the tier covered elsewhere in this project — this entry is included for completeness and because the pe/ayin feature is a genuinely interesting piece of the book's textual history.
