# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Colossians

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Colossians,
either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Ephesians, Romans,
Exodus, and Leviticus documents. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses "the firstborn of all
creation" -- the single most frequently cited proof-text in a real, currently active
denominational dispute (used by Jehovah's Witnesses and other non-Trinitarian traditions to
argue Christ was a created being), presented with the genuine linguistic arguments on both
sides. Entry 2 addresses one of the most startling individual phrases in the Pauline corpus --
"filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions" -- including its historical connection to
the pre-Reformation "treasury of merits" doctrine, a reading contemporary Catholic scholarship
itself no longer generally advocates.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 2, and 4 are presented with genuine theological and
denominational diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine theological or
denominational diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Interpretation

### 1. 'The firstborn of all creation' -- the central proof-text in a real, ongoing modern denominational dispute

**References:** Colossians 1:15-17

**The claim:** Colossians 1:15 describes Christ as 'the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation' (Greek prototokos pases ktiseos). This is the single most frequently cited proof-text used by Jehovah's Witnesses (and, in different specific forms, by other non-Trinitarian and Arian-type theological positions) to argue that Christ was himself the first being God created, rather than eternally existing, uncreated God -- a live, actively pressed theological argument in real, ongoing denominational disagreement, rather than a purely historical or academic dispute.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Colossians 1:15 as some form of 'firstborn of all creation' or 'firstborn over all creation,' without resolving the underlying interpretive question in the translation itself; this remains a matter of genuine, currently active theological and denominational disagreement, not resolved through translation choice among the ten translations surveyed here.

**Reconciliation:** This entry presents the genuine argument on both sides directly, since both are actively defended in real contemporary religious discourse. Jehovah's Witness sources argue the Greek term prototokos most literally and commonly means 'born first.' If Paul had intended a title of rank or preeminence rather than a claim about Christ's own origin, they argue, other available Greek terms -- such as protoktistos, 'first created,' or protogonos, 'first generated' -- would have more directly and unambiguously conveyed a meaning of simple pre-eminence without any connotation of coming into existence, making the choice of prototokos itself significant. Trinitarian scholars respond that prototokos is a well-attested Hebraic and biblical title of rank, priority, and privileged status rather than a strict claim about birth order or chronological origin. They point to its use elsewhere in Scripture where a 'firstborn' status is explicitly assigned to someone who was not literally born first: Israel is called God's 'firstborn' among the nations in Exodus 4:22, without this making Israel the first nation to exist, and David, the youngest of Jesse's sons, is called God's 'firstborn' in Psalm 89:27, a title there explicitly defined as being 'the highest of the kings of the earth' -- a matter of rank and covenant status rather than actual birth order. They also note that the same term is applied to Christ again three verses later regarding his resurrection (1:18, 'the firstborn from the dead'), where it self-evidently cannot mean Christ was the first person, chronologically, ever to die and rise, but clearly denotes supremacy and priority of rank among those raised. Trinitarian scholars further point to the immediately following verses (1:16-17) as decisive context: 'for by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible... all things were created through him and for him. And he is before all things, and in him all things hold together.' This language explicitly positions Christ as the active agent and sustaining cause of the entirety of creation, rather than as one item, however privileged, within the class of created things. This project does not adjudicate the underlying theological question of Christ's precise nature and relationship to the Father. This remains a matter of genuine, currently active disagreement between different religious traditions, and this entry presents the specific linguistic and contextual arguments on each side as accurately as possible.

### 2. 'Filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions' -- does this suggest Christ's atonement was insufficient?

**References:** Colossians 1:24

**The claim:** Colossians 1:24 has Paul state, 'Now I rejoice in my sufferings for your sake, and in my flesh I am filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the church' -- a startling phrase that, read at face value, seems to suggest something was genuinely 'lacking' or deficient in Christ's own sufferings, requiring Paul's own additional suffering to supplement or complete it, raising a serious question about the sufficiency of Christ's atoning work, which this same letter elsewhere describes in the most complete and triumphant terms (2:13-15, Christ having 'canceled the record of debt... nailing it to the cross').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Colossians 1:24 plainly and consistently as given, preserving its startling wording; this remains a genuinely debated interpretive question with real historical doctrinal significance, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated interpretive question with real historical doctrinal significance -- this verse was historically cited (prior to the Reformation, and by some Catholic theologians afterward) as scriptural support for the doctrine of a 'treasury of merits,' the idea that the accumulated sufferings of Christ and the saints together form a reservoir of merit the church can draw upon and apply to others, though contemporary Catholic scholarship no longer generally advocates this specific reading of the verse. Almost all contemporary commentators, across denominational lines, agree the verse cannot mean Christ's own atoning sufferings were deficient in their inherent worth or effectiveness for salvation, since this would contradict this very letter's own repeated, explicit affirmations of the completeness and sufficiency of Christ's finished work. Instead, several closely related interpretations are offered: some (following the influential reformer John Calvin) read the verse through the lens of the 'mystical union' between Christ and his body, the church, understanding Paul's own sufferings as, in some sense, Christ's own sufferings continuing to be experienced through his body -- what is 'lacking' being simply the full measure of suffering the whole body of Christ (head and members together) is appointed to experience, not any deficiency in the value of Christ's own atoning suffering specifically. Others (including the preacher John Piper, and reflected in more recent scholarship identified as an emerging consensus) read 'what is lacking' in a missional sense -- not a lack of atoning value, but a lack of visible presentation and proclamation to the nations, since Christ, now physically absent from the earth, accomplishes the ongoing, visible demonstration of his own sacrificial love specifically through the continued suffering of his messengers and representatives, who make that suffering known and felt among those who have not yet encountered it. Every major interpretation offered by serious commentators explicitly denies that this verse questions or diminishes the completeness of Christ's own atoning work, differing only in how they understand the specific sense in which something remains to be 'filled up' through the ongoing suffering of Christ's people.

### 3. 'Worship of angels' -- a puzzling, brief reference to a specific false teaching

**References:** Colossians 2:18

**The claim:** Colossians 2:18 warns against being disqualified 'by insisting on asceticism and worship of angels, going on in detail about visions, puffed up without reason by his sensuous mind' -- a brief, somewhat cryptic reference to a specific false teaching evidently circulating in the Colossian church involving some form of angel veneration or worship, without further explanation of exactly what this practice involved.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Colossians 2:18 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a brief, only partially reconstructable historical reference, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a brief reference to a specific, locally circulating false teaching whose exact nature remains only partially reconstructable from Paul's own brief description. Most commentators connect this to a broader pattern of syncretistic teaching evidently troubling the Colossian church, addressed throughout this letter, particularly 2:8's warning against 'philosophy and empty deceit, according to human tradition... and not according to Christ.' It likely involved some combination of Jewish mystical or ascetic practice and broader Greco-Roman religious speculation regarding angelic or cosmic intermediary powers, in which angels were accorded a degree of religious veneration Paul regarded as displacing proper, exclusive devotion to Christ. Christ alone, as this letter's own preceding argument establishes at length (1:15-20, 2:9-10), holds supremacy over all such powers, whether angelic or otherwise, and in him 'the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily.' The precise ritual or devotional form this angel-veneration took -- whether direct worship, invocation, or a more ambiguous mystical practice of communing with angels as part of visionary experience, itself connected to the passage's mention of visions -- is not fully specified in Paul's own brief description, and remains a matter of scholarly reconstruction rather than certain historical knowledge.

## Theological Tensions

### 4. 'A shadow of things to come' -- the continuing validity of Sabbath and food laws

**References:** Colossians 2:16-17; Leviticus 23:1-44

**The claim:** Colossians 2:16-17 instructs, 'let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ' -- language many read as declaring the Old Testament's ceremonial food laws, festival calendar, and Sabbath observance no longer binding on believers, raising a question about how this relates to the perpetual, ongoing significance the Sabbath and other ceremonial observances are given within the Torah itself (addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding the Sabbath commandment and 'perpetual statutes' in the companion Exodus and Leviticus documents), and connecting to genuine, ongoing denominational differences (most notably with Seventh-day Adventist and other Sabbatarian traditions) regarding the continuing obligation of Sabbath observance specifically.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Colossians 2:16-17 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most actively and sincerely debated questions in contemporary Christian denominational practice, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the more actively and sincerely debated questions in contemporary Christian theology and practice regarding the continuing applicability of Old Testament ceremonial law, and this project does not adjudicate the underlying question, consistent with its treatment of comparably contested denominational questions elsewhere in this series. Most mainstream Christian traditions read this passage as declaring that the entire ceremonial system -- food laws, festivals, new moon observances, and Sabbath-keeping specifically named together in this verse as a single category -- functioned as a preparatory 'shadow' pointing forward to Christ, whose own coming and completed work now constitutes the 'substance' those shadows anticipated, meaning believers are no longer bound to observe these specific ceremonial practices as a matter of religious obligation, though they remain free to observe or not observe such practices as a matter of personal conscience (a principle Paul develops at greater length in Romans 14, addressed in the companion Romans document). Sabbatarian traditions, including Seventh-day Adventism, generally respond that the Sabbath command, rooted in creation itself (Genesis 2:2-3) and included within the Ten Commandments specifically (unlike the food laws and festival calendar, which these traditions treat as a separate category of ceremonial law), occupies a distinct and more foundational status than the other items grouped alongside it in Colossians 2:16, and that Paul's language here addresses specific, additional ceremonial festivals and new-moon observances rather than the weekly Sabbath commandment as such -- a reading most mainstream commentators reject, noting the passage's own wording groups 'a Sabbath' together with festivals and new moons as a single category without distinguishing the weekly Sabbath as a separate, still-binding exception. This project takes no position on which reading is correct.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 5. The household code -- addressed at length in the companion Ephesians document

**References:** Colossians 3:18-4:1; Ephesians 5:21-33

**The claim:** Colossians 3:18-4:1 contains a household code closely paralleling Ephesians 5:21-6:9 (addressed at length in the companion Ephesians document), instructing wives to submit to husbands, husbands to love their wives, children to obey parents, and slaves to obey masters.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Colossians 3:18-4:1 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Ephesians document for the fuller discussion of the household code's interpretation.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Ephesians document, which addresses the same egalitarian/complementarian interpretive debate regarding the household code's gender instructions, without adjudicating between the positions. Colossians' own version is somewhat more compact than Ephesians' fuller treatment, but addresses the same basic relationships and instructions, and is generally read according to the same interpretive principles discussed at length in that companion document.

## Ethical Tensions

### 6. Should believers engage in conversation with non-believers, or keep separate from them?

**References:** Colossians 4:5-6; 1 Corinthians 5:9-11; 2 Corinthians 6:14

**The claim:** Colossians 4:5-6 instructs believers to 'walk in wisdom toward them that are without... let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man' -- actively commending gracious engagement and conversation with outsiders. This is set by some against other Pauline instructions urging separation from unbelievers: 'not to keep company' with certain sinners (1 Corinthians 5:9-11) and 'be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers' (2 Corinthians 6:14).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These address different, non-competing concerns, and 1 Corinthians 5 itself explicitly supplies the needed clarification within the same passage: Paul immediately qualifies his instruction not to company with sinners by stating, 'not altogether with the fornicators of this world... for then must ye needs go out of the world' (5:10) -- explicitly ruling out any general avoidance of ordinary social contact with unbelievers, and clarifying that his actual concern in that passage is specifically a professing believer who persists unrepentant in serious sin while claiming church membership (5:11), a matter of internal church discipline, not a rule about contact with the world generally. 2 Corinthians 6:14's warning against being 'unequally yoked' is likewise read as addressing binding partnerships or compromising alliances that would draw believers into shared complicity with idolatry or persistent sin, not casual social or conversational contact. Colossians 4:5-6's instruction to engage outsiders with grace and readiness to answer questions about the faith addresses exactly the kind of ordinary, evangelistic social contact both of the other passages explicitly leave open -- all three texts agree that believers should engage with the surrounding world while avoiding complicit partnership in sin, rather than offering competing instructions about the same activity.
