# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Romans

This document catalogs 9 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the Epistle to
the Romans, either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with James, the companion
Malachi, Exodus, Daniel, Acts, and Job documents. For each item, this document states the claim
as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim
appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's theological density:** Romans addresses several of the most
substantively debated questions in the history of Christian theology, and this document
reflects that directly. Entries 2, 4, and 6 in particular are presented with genuine,
sincere, and long-running scholarly and denominational diversity of opinion rather than a
single forced resolution -- the nature of divine election in Romans 9, the identity of the
"wretched man" in Romans 7, and the scope of "all Israel" in Romans 11 are each questions
this project does not adjudicate.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine and sincere theological
diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult
the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'Justified by faith apart from works of the law' -- vs. James's 'justified by works and not by faith alone'

**References:** Romans 3:28; Romans 4:1-5; James 2:14-26

**The claim:** Romans 3:28 states plainly, 'we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law,' a statement Paul develops at length using Abraham as his central example (4:1-5, 'to the one who does not work but believes... his faith is counted as righteousness'). James 2:24, however, states just as plainly, 'you see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone' -- using the same example, Abraham, and even citing the identical Old Testament proof-text (Genesis 15:6) that Paul cites, yet apparently reaching the opposite conclusion. This is among the most historically significant and consequential apparent contradictions in the entire New Testament, central to the doctrinal disputes of the Protestant Reformation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Romans 3:28, 4:1-5, and James 2:14-26 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the specific questions and senses of key terms each author addresses, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, across a wide range of theological traditions, resolve this by distinguishing the different specific questions each author is answering, and the different specific senses in which each author uses key terms like 'faith,' 'works,' and 'justified.' Paul's argument in Romans addresses how a sinner is initially declared righteous before God -- his consistent target throughout Romans is the claim that a person can earn righteous standing before God through law-keeping or human merit, which he denies absolutely, insisting this initial justification comes only through faith, understood as genuine trust and reliance on Christ rather than a mere intellectual assent (Paul's own letters elsewhere describe this kind of faith as inherently active and transformative, e.g., Galatians 5:6, 'faith working through love'). James, writing to an audience apparently tempted to claim an inert, merely intellectual 'faith' produces no observable change in conduct (James 2:14, 19's pointed example that 'even the demons believe -- and shudder'), addresses a different specific question: how genuine, saving faith can be recognized and demonstrated as authentic, arguing that true faith of the kind Paul describes will necessarily and observably produce good works as its natural fruit -- 'faith apart from works is dead' (2:26) -- meaning James's 'works' function as evidence confirming genuine faith's presence, not as a separate, additional basis for justification alongside or instead of faith. On this widely-held reading, Paul denies that works can be the ground or basis of justification before God, while James denies that faith lacking any accompanying works is the kind of genuine faith that actually justifies -- both authors, on this reading, agree that authentic faith and its resulting good works are inseparably connected, addressing this connection from two different angles and against two different specific errors (self-righteous legalism, in Paul's case; a dead, merely notional faith, in James's).

### 2. Romans 9's election language -- Jacob and Esau, Pharaoh's hardened heart, and the potter and the clay

**References:** Romans 9:10-24; Malachi 1:2-3; Exodus 9:12

**The claim:** Romans 9 develops an extended argument for God's sovereign freedom in election, citing Malachi 1:2-3's 'Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated' (addressed at length in the companion Malachi document) regarding the two patriarchs chosen 'though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad' (9:11), Pharaoh's hardened heart as an instance of God's sovereign purpose 'that I might show my power in you' (9:17, citing Exodus 9:12, itself addressed in this project's general document set regarding who hardens Pharaoh's heart), and the extended image of a potter's absolute right over the clay he shapes (9:19-21) -- language read by many as teaching a strong doctrine of unconditional individual election to salvation or damnation, raising substantial questions about human free will, moral responsibility, and the fairness of such a system that Paul himself explicitly anticipates and responds to within the same passage (9:14, 19-20).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Romans 9:10-24 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most substantively and sincerely debated theological questions across major Christian traditions, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Malachi document regarding Malachi 1:2-3's own original context, which addresses the genuine, long-running theological division between different Christian traditions on precisely this question -- broadly, Reformed/Calvinist traditions reading Romans 9 as teaching unconditional individual election to salvation consistent with their broader theological system, and Arminian and other traditions reading the same chapter as primarily addressing corporate, national election (Israel's own place in salvation history, and God's sovereign right to include Gentiles alongside a remnant of Israel, the specific concern the following two chapters, Romans 10-11, develop at length) rather than a direct statement about each individual's own eternal, unconditional destiny apart from any response of faith. This project does not adjudicate between these two major theological traditions' reading of Romans 9, consistent with its treatment of the same underlying question in the companion Malachi document, noting only that Paul's own argument culminates, within the same extended discussion (Romans 9-11 read as a single unit), in an affirmation of God's mercy extended to 'all' who call on him in faith (10:12-13) and an extended meditation on Israel's own future restoration (11:1-32), suggesting the passage's full argument is considerably more complex than any single verse in isolation, however that complexity is ultimately resolved by different theological traditions.

### 3. Universal condemnation ('all have sinned') -- and the specific, biblical descriptions of certain figures as 'blameless'

**References:** Romans 3:23; Job 1:1; Luke 1:6

**The claim:** Romans 3:23 states, 'all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God' -- an apparently universal, exceptionless claim. Yet Scripture elsewhere describes specific individuals in terms that read as claiming a kind of blamelessness: Job is introduced as 'blameless and upright' (Job 1:1, addressed at length in the companion Job document), and Zechariah and Elizabeth, John the Baptist's parents, are described as 'righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the commandments and statutes of the Lord' (Luke 1:6).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Romans 3:23, Job 1:1, and Luke 1:6 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same distinction addressed at greater length in the companion Job document between absolute sinless perfection and relative, sincere moral integrity, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Job document, which addresses the same general distinction applicable here: 'blameless' in these specific descriptions is generally read by commentators as describing a person's general, sincere moral integrity and genuine faithfulness relative to their contemporaries and their own conduct's general pattern, rather than a claim of sinless moral perfection in every individual instance throughout their entire life -- a reading directly supported in Job's own case by his subsequent need for repentance (Job 42:6, addressed in the companion Job document) despite the same book's own earlier commendation of his blamelessness, showing 'blameless' in that context did not mean sinless. Romans 3:23's universal claim addresses the more absolute, comprehensive question of whether any human being (Job, Zechariah, and Elizabeth included) has ever lived a genuinely sinless life meeting God's own perfect standard, which Paul answers negatively for every person without exception -- a claim fully compatible with these same individuals also being genuinely, comparatively 'blameless' in the sense of consistent, sincere covenant faithfulness the other passages describe, since the two claims operate at different standards of comparison (absolute divine perfection, versus consistent faithfulness relative to ordinary human conduct and one's own community).

### 4. Who is God's firstborn son -- Israel, or Christ?

**References:** Romans 8:29; Exodus 4:22; Colossians 1:15

**The claim:** Romans 8:29 describes Christ as 'the firstborn among many brethren.' Exodus 4:22, however, has God call the nation of Israel 'my son, even my firstborn.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These use 'firstborn' in different senses applied to different kinds of subjects, not competing claims to a single title: Exodus 4:22 designates Israel as God's firstborn in a corporate, national sense -- a covenantal statement of Israel's privileged place and priority of calling among the nations of the earth (comparable to how a firstborn son held a position of special honor and inheritance rights within a family), not a claim about individual or personal sonship. Romans 8:29's description of Christ as 'firstborn among many brethren' instead describes Christ's supreme preeminence as the source, pattern, and head of a new spiritual family -- believers who become adopted sons of God through union with him (the immediate context, 8:29's 'predestinated to be conformed to the image of his Son'), with Christ himself as first and preeminent within this new family of adopted sons, a title of rank and priority (compare Colossians 1:15, 18's similar use of 'firstborn' for Christ's supremacy over creation and the church) rather than a claim about birth order. Israel's corporate, national 'firstborn' status among earthly nations and Christ's preeminent, personal 'firstborn' status within the new spiritual family of believers are different applications of the same honorific term to entirely different kinds of subjects.

## Theological Interpretation

### 5. The 'wretched man' of Romans 7 -- Paul's present Christian struggle, or his pre-conversion experience under the law?

**References:** Romans 7:14-25; Romans 6:1-14; Romans 8:1-17

**The claim:** Romans 7:14-25 describes an intense, first-person struggle with sin: 'I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate... Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?' This passage's precise referent -- whether Paul describes his own present, ongoing experience as a mature Christian, his own experience before conversion, or uses 'I' as a rhetorical device describing the general human condition under the law apart from Christ -- has been debated since at least Augustine (who himself changed his own view on the question over his career) and remains genuinely unresolved among serious biblical scholars, with real pastoral stakes for how contemporary Christians understand their own ongoing struggle with sin.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Romans 7:14-25 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most extensively debated interpretive questions in the Pauline corpus, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most extensively and sincerely debated interpretive questions in the entire Pauline corpus, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. Proponents of the 'present, post-conversion' reading (including the scholar John Piper, and reflected in a detailed grammatical analysis published in an academic paper on this exact question) point to the passage's grammatical shift from past tense (describing Paul's own past experience, 7:7-13) to sustained present tense throughout 7:14-25, and note that Paul elsewhere gives no indication of the kind of anguished, internally conflicted struggle over sin this passage describes as characterizing his own life before conversion (compare his own more confident self-description as 'blameless' regarding the law's external requirements in Philippians 3:6) -- suggesting the passage more naturally describes an ongoing, present tension between a genuinely regenerate will (7:22, 'I delight in the law of God, in my inner being') and indwelling sin that continues even after conversion, prior to final glorification. Proponents of the 'pre-conversion' or 'rhetorical device' reading (including the influential twentieth-century German scholar W.G. Kummel, whose 1929 study substantially shifted critical scholarly opinion toward this position, and the commentator Douglas Moo) argue this passage's description of being 'sold under sin' and 'captive to the law of sin' (7:14, 23) is difficult to reconcile with Paul's own confident description, only one chapter earlier, of the Christian as having already died to sin and been freed from its enslaving power (Romans 6:6-14, 18, 22) -- reading 'I' in 7:14-25 not as Paul's own personal testimony but as a rhetorical, dramatized voicing of the universal human condition under the law's demands, viewed in retrospect by someone who now, in Christ, sees that condition clearly for what it was. A minority mediating position (associated with the preacher Martyn Lloyd-Jones) holds the passage describes a genuine but spiritually immature Christian who has not yet grasped the fuller freedom Romans 8 describes. This project does not adjudicate between these positions, consistent with its treatment of comparably difficult, long-debated interpretive questions elsewhere in this series.

### 6. 'As one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification... for all men' -- does this teach universal salvation?

**References:** Romans 5:12-19

**The claim:** Romans 5:18 states, 'as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men' -- a parallel construction some read as implying that if Adam's sin resulted in condemnation for literally every human being without exception, Christ's righteous act must, by the same symmetrical logic, result in justification for literally every human being without exception, apparently teaching universal salvation regardless of individual faith, in tension with the rest of the New Testament's consistent emphasis on the necessity of personal faith for salvation (including elsewhere within Romans itself, e.g., 10:9-13).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Romans 5:12-19 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading the passage's own immediate qualifying context (5:17's explicit 'those who receive') and Paul's consistent emphasis on faith elsewhere in Romans into 5:18's more compact parallel construction, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, across nearly all major Christian traditions, with the notable exception of those holding to universalism as a considered theological position, read the parallel between Adam and Christ in this passage as intentionally asymmetrical in its actual mode of application, despite the verse's own symmetrical grammatical construction. The passage's own immediate surrounding context repeatedly and explicitly qualifies the benefit of Christ's righteous act as received specifically 'through the one man Jesus Christ' by 'those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness' (5:17) -- explicit language of reception this same passage supplies just one verse before 5:18's more absolute-sounding formulation. The 'all' in the second half of 5:18's parallel is therefore generally read as 'all who are in Christ' or 'all without distinction, Jew and Gentile alike,' rather than 'all without exception regardless of faith.' This reading is consistent with Paul's own repeated emphasis throughout Romans that justification is specifically received 'by faith' (3:28, 5:1, and elsewhere). On this reading, Paul's rhetorical point is about the parallel structure and comparable scope of Adam's and Christ's respective representative acts, each affecting all who belong to their respective 'family' or 'headship' -- not a claim that every single individual descended from Adam will, without exception and without any need for personal faith, automatically receive Christ's righteousness as well. Adam's condemnation is inherited automatically by all his natural descendants, while Christ's righteousness is received specifically by those united to him through faith. This is a difference in the mode of transmission that the passage's own broader argument, and Paul's consistent emphasis throughout the rest of the letter, requires reading into the verse's more compact, rhetorically balanced wording.

### 7. 'And in this way all Israel will be saved' -- a significant, actively debated eschatological statement

**References:** Romans 11:25-27

**The claim:** Romans 11:25-27 states, 'Lest you be wise in your own sight, I want you to understand this mystery, brothers: a partial hardening has come upon Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in. And in this way all Israel will be saved' -- a statement whose precise scope and meaning ('all Israel') remains one of the most significant and actively debated questions in New Testament eschatology, with substantial implications for how different Christian traditions understand the relationship between ethnic, national Israel and the Christian Church.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Romans 11:25-27 plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here is one of the most substantively debated in New Testament eschatology, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most substantively debated eschatological questions addressed anywhere in this series, and this project does not adjudicate it, consistent with its general practice regarding actively and sincerely contested eschatological questions (addressed similarly regarding Daniel's seventy weeks and Ezekiel's Gog and Magog in their own companion documents). Several major interpretive positions are seriously held: some read 'all Israel' as referring to a future, large-scale conversion of ethnic, national Israel to faith in Christ at the end of the age, consistent with much of Romans 9-11's own sustained argument regarding God's continuing purposes for ethnic Israel specifically; others read 'all Israel' as referring to the complete number of elect individuals from ethnic Israel throughout history (not necessarily a future mass conversion at any single point), consistent with a more gradual, ongoing pattern of individual Jewish conversion already underway throughout the church age; still others (a position sometimes associated with 'replacement' or 'supersessionist' theology, though itself internally varied) read 'Israel' here as referring to the true, spiritual Israel comprising all believers, Jew and Gentile alike, united in Christ, rather than to ethnic Israel as a specific national or genetic category at all. This project takes no position on which reading is correct, noting that this question remains genuinely and sincerely contested among careful, serious biblical scholars and theologians across a range of denominational and theological traditions.

## Ethical Tensions

### 8. 'Let every person be subject to the governing authorities' -- vs. examples of righteous civil disobedience

**References:** Romans 13:1-7; Acts 5:27-29; Exodus 1:15-21; Daniel 3:1-18

**The claim:** Romans 13:1-2 instructs, 'Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God. Therefore whoever resists the authorities resists what God has appointed' -- language read by some as an unqualified, absolute command of political submission. This raises a question in light of several biblical examples of deliberate civil disobedience treated approvingly elsewhere (the Hebrew midwives defying Pharaoh's order to kill Israelite infants, Exodus 1:15-21, addressed at length in this project's companion Exodus document; Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refusing to worship Nebuchadnezzar's image, Daniel 3:1-18; and the apostles themselves, when ordered by the Jewish council to stop preaching, replying, 'We must obey God rather than men,' Acts 5:29).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Romans 13:1-7 and the cited examples of civil disobedience plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing Romans 13's own general scope (ordinary civic submission to a legitimately functioning government) as distinct from the narrower question of direct conflict between a specific governmental command and a clear, higher divine command, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Romans 13:1-7 as addressing the general, ordinary case -- civil government as a genuinely God-ordained institution for maintaining social order, punishing wrongdoing, and promoting the general welfare (13:3-4 explicitly describes this positive function as the passage's own stated basis for the submission it commands) -- rather than as an absolute, unqualified command that would require obedience even when a specific government directly commands what God himself forbids, or forbids what God himself commands. This reading is generally held to be fully compatible with, rather than contradicting, the specific instances of righteous civil disobedience found elsewhere in Scripture, since each of those cases involves a direct conflict between a specific governmental command and a clear, higher divine command (killing innocent infants, worshiping an idol, or ceasing to proclaim the gospel), a category of conflict Romans 13 itself does not address or resolve one way or the other -- the passage's own scope is general civic submission and respect for legitimate governmental authority and function, not a specific ruling on the narrower question of what a believer should do when a specific governmental command directly requires disobedience to God's own clear, higher command, which the New Testament's own explicit example in Acts 5:29 addresses directly and resolves in favor of obedience to God over a specific, conflicting human command.

### 9. Should we try to please others, or does seeking to please others disqualify someone as Christ's servant?

**References:** Romans 15:1-2; Galatians 1:10

**The claim:** Romans 15:2 instructs, 'Let every one of us please his neighbour for his good to edification.' Galatians 1:10, however, has Paul ask rhetorically, 'do I now persuade men, or God? or do I seek to please men? for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ' -- seemingly rejecting the effort to please people altogether.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two passages address different kinds of 'pleasing' aimed at different goals, a distinction Romans 15:2 itself makes explicit in its own wording: pleasing one's neighbor specifically 'for his good, to edification' -- a selfless, sacrificial consideration for another person's genuine spiritual benefit and growth, in the immediate context of the 'strong' bearing with the 'weak' for the sake of the community's unity and mutual building up (15:1-7). Galatians 1:10 instead addresses a different and opposite kind of people-pleasing -- compromising or softening the truth of the gospel message in order to win personal approval or acceptance, the specific temptation Paul is defending himself against in that letter's opening argument for the integrity and divine origin of his gospel. Selflessly serving another's genuine good, even at cost to oneself (Romans 15:2), and compromising truth for the sake of personal approval (which Galatians 1:10 rejects), are not the same activity -- one is commended throughout Scripture as a form of love, the other condemned as a betrayal of gospel integrity.
