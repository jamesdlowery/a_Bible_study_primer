# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Judges

This document catalogs 15 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Judges, either entirely internal to Judges or between Judges and a directly related passage
elsewhere (Genesis, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, 1 Kings, 2 Samuel, 1 Corinthians, 1
Timothy, Hebrews). For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised,
notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the
translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in
English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entry 5 (Jephthah's vow) is presented without a single, confident
resolution, because whether Jephthah actually sacrificed his daughter or dedicated her to
lifelong service is a genuinely disputed question with a long history on both sides, going
back to ancient Jewish and early Christian interpreters. This is flagged as a live question
rather than given a manufactured harmonization, consistent with this project's practice
elsewhere of acknowledging where the evidence genuinely does not settle the matter. Entry 12
(the Sisera death accounts) is, by contrast, included specifically to show that not every
claimed discrepancy survives a close reading -- the prose and poetic accounts agree.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where none commands real consensus,
an honest account of the live disagreement. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary
texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Jerusalem captured and burned -- yet the Jebusites were not driven out

**References:** Judges 1:8; Judges 1:21

**The claim:** Judges 1:8 states that the men of Judah 'fought against Jerusalem and captured it and struck it with the edge of the sword and set the city on fire.' Thirteen verses later, Judges 1:21 states that the tribe of Benjamin 'did not drive out the Jebusites who lived in Jerusalem,' who 'have lived with the people of Benjamin in Jerusalem to this day.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing a raid from securing a fortified stronghold, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry is treated at greater length in the companion Joshua document (which addresses the parallel statement at Joshua 15:63), since the same underlying question appears in both books. In brief, the standard harmonization holds that Judah's forces inflicted serious damage on the lower or more exposed parts of the city in a raid (Judges 1:8), without securing permanent control of the city's fortified citadel, which the Jebusites retained -- consistent with 2 Samuel 5:6-9 later describing David's capture of the city's stronghold as a first, decisive seizure rather than a re-conquest of ground Israel already fully held.

### 2. 'The LORD was with Judah' -- yet Judah 'could not' drive out the inhabitants who had iron chariots

**References:** Judges 1:19

**The claim:** Judges 1:19 states, 'And the LORD was with Judah, and he took possession of the hill country, but he could not drive out the inhabitants of the plain, because they had chariots of iron' -- read by some as implying that iron chariots represented a military technology capable of limiting or overcoming God's own power, in tension with God's demonstrated total power elsewhere in the conquest narratives (parting the sea, collapsing Jericho's walls, and so on).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'he could not drive out' with the same ambiguous 'he' (referring most naturally, in context, to Judah rather than the LORD) that the Hebrew itself uses; the resolution rests on reading the sentence's grammar and the book's broader rebuke of Israel's own failure (2:1-3), not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Nearly all commentators read this verse's grammatical subject for 'could not' as Judah (the human tribal army), not the LORD -- the sentence structure distinguishes divine presence and blessing ('the LORD was with Judah') from the human army's own tactical and military failure to complete the conquest of the more heavily fortified and better-equipped valley regions, a failure of nerve, faith, or follow-through on Israel's part rather than a stated limitation on divine capability. This reading is reinforced by the broader pattern established across Judges 1 and reinforced explicitly in Judges 2:1-3, where an angel of the LORD directly rebukes Israel for failing to drive out the remaining inhabitants and for compromising with them -- a rebuke that presupposes the failure was Israel's own, not God's, since one does not rebuke God for a limitation in his own power.

### 3. Divine direction to attack Benjamin, yet Israel suffers two initial defeats

**References:** Judges 20:18; Judges 20:21; Judges 20:23; Judges 20:25; Judges 20:26-28; Judges 20:35

**The claim:** Before the civil war against the tribe of Benjamin (in response to the Gibeah atrocity of chapter 19), Israel inquires of God, who tells them Judah should attack first (20:18). Israel is nonetheless routed twice in succession, losing 22,000 men on the first day (20:21) and 18,000 more on the second (20:25), despite inquiring of and weeping before the LORD again between each defeat (20:23) -- before finally receiving an explicit promise of victory (20:28) and prevailing on the third attempt (20:35).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each stage of the inquiry and battle sequence plainly and consistently as given, including the escalating specificity of God's responses; the harmonization rests on reading the three inquiries as progressively different in content, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that God's answer to 'who shall go up first' (20:18) is a response to a narrower tactical question about battle order, not a blanket promise of immediate, effortless victory -- a distinction the text itself appears to mark, since only the third inquiry (20:28) receives the more complete assurance 'tomorrow I will give them into your hand,' language absent from the earlier exchanges. The narrative is widely read as depicting the assembled Israelite tribes -- who had, only chapters earlier, tolerated and shared complicity in various forms of covenant unfaithfulness across the book -- being humbled and disciplined through initial defeat before being granted victory, a pattern with some resemblance to Israel's earlier initial defeat at Ai (Joshua 7) following unaddressed sin in the camp, and to the broader biblical theme that inquiring of God and receiving general guidance does not by itself guarantee an immediate, cost-free outcome divorced from a nation's own spiritual condition.

### 4. Samson's death: a commended act of self-sacrifice, or a prohibited act of self-destruction?

**References:** Judges 16:28-30; Hebrews 11:32

**The claim:** Samson, blinded and bound between the pillars of the Philistine temple, prays for strength one final time and then pulls the pillars down, killing himself along with thousands of Philistines gathered there (16:28-30) -- an act some read as morally equivalent to suicide, raising the question of whether Scripture is commending an act of self-destruction, particularly given his inclusion among the commended in Hebrews 11:32.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Judges 16:28-30 and Hebrews 11:32 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the stated purpose and framing of Samson's final act from suicide undertaken for its own sake, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish Samson's action from suicide in the ordinary sense (an act aimed at ending one's own life for its own sake, out of despair or to escape suffering) by its stated purpose and framing: Samson's prayer explicitly asks God for strength 'that I may be avenged on the Philistines' (16:28) as a final act of deliverance and judgment against Israel's oppressors, in keeping with the specific commissioning given before his birth that he would 'begin to save Israel from the hand of the Philistines' (13:5) -- his death is the means by which he completes this God-given task in a single decisive act, more comparable, in the categories most commentators use, to a soldier's self-sacrificial action in the course of achieving a military objective than to suicide undertaken for its own sake or out of despair unconnected to any larger purpose. On this reading, Hebrews 11:32's commendation (alongside the broader commendation of faith 'who through faith... became mighty in war,' 11:33-34, language that plausibly includes Samson's culminating act) is consistent with treating this final act, whatever else may be said about Samson's deeply flawed life leading up to it, as an act of faith rather than an act of despair.

### 5. Can iron chariots stop God's deliverance, given Judah's earlier defeat by them?

**References:** Judges 1:19; Judges 4:3, 13-16

**The claim:** Judges 1:19 states that Judah 'could not drive out the inhabitants of the valley, because they had chariots of iron' -- addressed elsewhere in this document as describing a failure of Judah's own nerve and follow-through rather than a limit on God's power. Judges 4 then presents Sisera's Canaanite army, equipped with 900 iron chariots (4:3, 13), as an intimidating and by every human measure superior force -- yet God, through Deborah and Barak, gives Israel a complete and decisive victory over this same iron-chariot army (4:15-16), raising the question of whether iron chariots represent a real obstacle to God's power or not, given the two seemingly different outcomes.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Read together, Judges 1:19 and Judges 4 are consistent rather than conflicting, and in fact directly illustrate the very point made in this document's entry on Judges 1:19: iron chariots were never a limitation on God's own power, only on unfaithful or hesitant human execution of a task God had already enabled. Judges 4 supplies the positive demonstration of exactly this principle -- against the same kind of formidable iron-chariot force that intimidated Judah into an incomplete conquest a chapter earlier, God, through judges willing to trust and act (Deborah's confidence and Barak's obedience once given clear direction, 4:14), delivers total victory, with the chariots themselves proving no obstacle once Israel actually engaged in faith. The two passages are best read as showing cause and effect: unbelief and hesitancy produced Judah's earlier partial failure; faith and obedience produced Israel's later complete victory over the identical kind of technological threat.

## Law and Ethics

### 6. Jephthah's vow and his daughter: did he actually offer a human sacrifice?

**References:** Judges 11:30-31; Judges 11:34-40; Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31; Deuteronomy 18:10

**The claim:** Jephthah vows that whatever comes out of his house first to meet him upon his victorious return will belong to the LORD and be offered as a burnt offering (11:31). His only daughter is the first to come out (11:34). The text goes on to say 'he did with her according to his vow that he had made' (11:39) -- read by many as describing an actual human sacrifice, which would place Jephthah's own action in direct conflict with the Law's explicit and repeated prohibition of human sacrifice (Leviticus 18:21, Deuteronomy 12:31, 18:10), and would raise the further question of why Scripture appears to narrate this without explicit condemnation, and even lists Jephthah among the honored 'heroes of faith' in Hebrews 11:32.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE:** Nine of the ten translations in this survey render Judges 11:31 with the ambiguous pronoun 'it' rather than specifying 'him,' 'her,' or 'that person,' preserving the same interpretive openness present in the Hebrew text; only CSB's 'that person' commits more explicitly to the reading that a human being was in view. None of the ten translations renders the verse with the alternative 'or' construction ('it shall be the LORD's, or I will offer it as a burnt offering') proposed by a minority of commentators (including Adam Clarke) as a way of resolving the tension via translation -- all ten follow the majority Hebrew reading with 'and.'
- **CSB:** CSB renders Judges 11:31 explicitly with 'that person,' committing more clearly to the human-sacrifice reading in its wording than most other translations: 'I will offer that person as a burnt offering' (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV, along with ESV, NASB, NIV, NKJV, and NLT, renders the verse with the more ambiguous 'it,' preserving the same ambiguity present in the Hebrew rather than resolving it toward either reading (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Two main readings have been held throughout the history of interpretation, and this entry does not resolve between them, since serious commentators remain divided: (1) the traditional and, for most of Christian history, majority reading holds that Jephthah did offer his daughter as a literal burnt offering -- a rash, ill-considered vow (compare 11:35's own anguished 'you have brought me very low... I have opened my mouth to the LORD, and I cannot take back my vow') carried out despite, not because of, the Law's prohibition, with the narrative presenting it critically or tragically rather than approvingly (the annual four-day commemoration by the young women of Israel, 11:39-40, is generally read as mourning the tragedy, not celebrating a righteous act), and Hebrews 11:32's commendation understood to honor Jephthah's faith and military deliverance of Israel specifically, not every detail of his personal conduct -- a pattern of qualified commendation applied to other flawed figures named in the same passage (such as Samson). (2) A minority but historically significant reading (held by various rabbinic and some Christian interpreters, and reflected in the Jewish Antiquities-era debate over the passage) holds that Jephthah instead dedicated his daughter to lifelong virginity and exclusive service to the LORD rather than sacrificing her -- pointing to the text's specific emphasis on her mourning 'my virginity' (11:37-38) rather than her impending death, to the fact that Israelite law provided a mechanism for redeeming a vowed person with a monetary payment rather than requiring death (Leviticus 27:1-8), and to the difficulty of reconciling an actual human sacrifice with the Law's absolute prohibition, which this reading holds Jephthah -- despite the narrative's otherwise unflattering portrait of his background and character -- would not have so flagrantly violated with the story's approval.

### 7. Gideon's ephod: idolatry from a judge commended for faith

**References:** Judges 8:22-27; Judges 6:25-27; Hebrews 11:32

**The claim:** Gideon, having just torn down his own father's altar to Baal at God's command (6:25-27) and led Israel to a great deliverance, later collects gold from the plunder and makes it into an ephod, which he sets up in his hometown of Ophrah -- and 'all Israel whored after it there, and it became a snare to Gideon and to his family' (8:27). Gideon is nonetheless listed by name among the commended 'heroes of faith' in Hebrews 11:32.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Judges 8:22-27 and Hebrews 11:32 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing Judges' own explicitly negative verdict on the ephod and Hebrews' narrower point of commendation, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The text of Judges itself does not present Gideon's ephod-making as a praiseworthy act -- it is narrated immediately after Gideon's own religiously and politically ambiguous response to Israel's request that he rule as king (8:22-23, in which Gideon verbally declines a hereditary monarchy while apparently proceeding to accumulate the kind of trappings, including many wives and, per 9:2, at least seventy sons, associated with one), and the passage's own concluding verdict ('it became a snare to Gideon and to his family,' 8:27) explicitly marks the ephod as a spiritual failure rather than a commended act. Most commentators read Hebrews 11:32's commendation, alongside Barak, Samson, and Jephthah in the same verse, as honoring the specific quality of faith each figure displayed in a particular decisive moment (in Gideon's case, most naturally his faith in stepping out against the Midianites with a greatly reduced force at God's direction, 7:2-7) without thereby endorsing every subsequent choice each figure made -- a pattern of qualified commendation the book of Judges applies to nearly every judge it names, several of whom (Gideon, Jephthah, and especially Samson) are portrayed with substantial, narratively unresolved moral failures alongside their genuine faith and God-given military success.

### 8. Deborah's public leadership and judgeship (Judges 4-5) and later New Testament restrictions on women's public teaching authority

**References:** Judges 4:4-5; Judges 4:14; 1 Corinthians 14:34-35; 1 Timothy 2:11-12

**The claim:** Deborah is introduced as 'a prophetess... judging Israel at that time' (4:4), issues military direction to Barak on God's explicit authority (4:6-7, 14), and is celebrated in the victory song of chapter 5 without any narrative qualification or criticism of her public, authoritative role. Paul's later instructions restrict women from certain forms of public teaching and authoritative speech in the gathered church (1 Corinthians 14:34-35, 1 Timothy 2:11-12), which some read as in tension with Deborah's approved example.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Deborah's introduction and role, and Paul's later instructions, plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns distinguishing categories of leadership and authority across differing historical and institutional contexts, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators across the theological spectrum generally note that Deborah's role as judge in Israel's pre-monarchic tribal confederacy, and as a prophetess directly relaying God's specific military instructions, belongs to a different category and context than the specific instructions Paul addresses regarding order in the gathered New Testament church assembly -- the Old Testament elsewhere approvingly records other prophetesses exercising a recognized, authoritative prophetic function (Miriam, Exodus 15:20; Huldah, consulted directly by the king's officials and the high priest, 2 Kings 22:14-20) without this being treated as a violation of any stated principle, since prophetic authority (understood as directly relaying God's own message) and the specific teaching-authority structures Paul addresses within a settled congregational assembly are generally treated by most interpreters as distinct categories rather than the same kind of role addressed in two contradictory ways.

### 9. Is it acceptable to test God, given Gideon's fleece?

**References:** Judges 6:36-40; Deuteronomy 6:16; Matthew 4:7

**The claim:** Judges 6:36-40 has Gideon twice asking God for a sign involving a fleece of wool (first wet with dew while the ground stays dry, then the reverse) to confirm God's promise of deliverance, and God accommodates both requests without rebuke. Deuteronomy 6:16 commands, 'Ye shall not tempt the LORD your God,' a command Jesus himself later quotes against Satan's temptation to test God (Matthew 4:7).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators generally distinguish two different postures toward God that can both be called 'testing' in English but are treated differently in Scripture: a demanding, presumptuous testing that treats God's proven faithfulness as insufficient and insists he perform on command as a substitute for trust (the kind of testing Israel is condemned for at Massah, Exodus 17:1-7, and the kind Satan tempts Jesus toward in Matthew 4, asking him to force God's hand for no reason but to prove a point already settled) -- versus a struggling, still-obedient believer humbly seeking confirmation of a genuinely uncertain, specific calling before acting on it, without either doubting God's character or refusing to move forward regardless of the answer. Gideon's fleece requests are widely read in this second category: Gideon has already committed to obey (he has just torn down his father's altar to Baal at God's command, 6:25-27, at real personal risk) and explicitly frames his requests with humility, asking God not to be angry with him (6:39) -- a posture of a weak but genuinely willing servant seeking reassurance for an unprecedented and daunting task, which God patiently accommodates, rather than a demanding refusal to act without a sign, of the kind Deuteronomy 6:16 and Matthew 4:7 address.

## Law and Covenant Sign

### 10. Samson's lifelong Nazirite vow, and his repeated contact with the dead

**References:** Judges 13:5, 7; Judges 14:8-9; Judges 14:19; Judges 15:15; Numbers 6:6-8

**The claim:** Samson is set apart 'a Nazirite to God from the womb' (13:5, 7), a lifelong vow. Numbers 6:6-8 requires a Nazirite to avoid all contact with a dead body, even a close relative's, for the duration of the vow. Yet Samson repeatedly comes into contact with the dead: he scoops honey out of a lion's carcass and eats it (14:8-9), kills thirty men at Ashkelon (14:19), and later kills a thousand Philistines with the fresh jawbone of a dead donkey (15:15) -- picking up and wielding a dead animal's bone as a weapon.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the relevant passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing the specific scope of the Nazirite corpse-contact rule and Samson's narrated pattern of increasing compromise, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that the specific Nazirite regulations in Numbers 6 most directly concern voluntary, ordinary contact with a corpse (such as attending a funeral or being present at a death) rather than every conceivable incidental brush with something dead in the course of a divinely empowered act of deliverance, and that Samson's story as a whole -- unusually, for a judge described as set apart by God from before birth -- narrates a life marked by repeated, serious lapses (his choice of foreign wives against his parents' explicit objection, 14:3; his visits to a prostitute, 16:1; his relationship with Delilah, 16:4-20) that the text does not present as a model of consistent faithfulness to his calling. Most conservative readings hold that Samson's Nazirite status was progressively and increasingly compromised throughout the narrative (most decisively at his haircut, 16:17-20, after which 'the LORD had left him,' 16:20) rather than perfectly maintained, with the honey and jawbone incidents understood either as earlier, less severe lapses in an already imperfect observance, or (in the case of the honey, which he did not touch the carcass itself to eat, per 14:9's detail that he scraped honey from it into his hands) as not constituting the kind of direct corpse-contact Numbers 6 has most directly in view.

## Numbers and Chronology

### 11. Do the judges' individually stated years add up consistently with other biblical chronological markers?

**References:** Judges 3:11, 30; Judges 4:3; Judges 5:31; Judges 8:28; Judges 9:22; Judges 10:2-3, 8; Judges 12:7, 9, 11, 14; Judges 13:1; Judges 16:31; 1 Kings 6:1

**The claim:** Judges gives specific figures for periods of oppression and 'rest' under each judge (for example: 8 years of oppression, 40 years of rest under Othniel; 18 years of oppression, 80 years of rest under Ehud; 20 years of oppression, 40 years of rest under Deborah/Barak; and so on through the book). Simply adding these figures together (without allowing for any overlap between judges whose activity may have been regional rather than nationwide) produces a total exceeding 300 years for the judges period alone -- which, combined with the 40 years of wilderness wandering, roughly 7 years of conquest under Joshua, and 40-year reigns each for Saul, David, and part of Solomon's reign before the Temple, would substantially exceed the 480-year figure 1 Kings 6:1 gives from the Exodus to the start of Temple construction in Solomon's fourth year.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each individual figure in Judges and 1 Kings 6:1's 480 years exactly as given, without adjusting any of them; the resolution is a matter of historical-chronological interpretation (regional versus national judgeships, and the stylized nature of the 480-year figure), not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the related question addressed for 1 Kings 6:1 in the general document set, the most widely offered resolution holds that a number of the judges' periods of activity were regional rather than nationwide, and therefore overlapped in time rather than following one another in a single unbroken national sequence. The book of Judges itself gives no indication that all Israel was uniformly under a single judge's authority at every point -- compare, for example, how Jephthah's activity in Gilead, east of the Jordan (11:1-12:7), and Samson's activity centered on the Philistine-adjacent territory of Dan and Judah (13:2, 25), read naturally as regionally focused rather than nationwide. That would make a straightforward addition of every judge's tenure an overstatement of the actual elapsed time, even on the text's own terms. 1 Kings 6:1's 480 years is also widely read, as addressed at greater length elsewhere in this project, as a stylized, generational figure (12 x 40) rather than a strict, independently-arrived-at sum, further reducing the pressure to force an exact match between the two separate chronological approaches.

### 12. Were all seventy of Gideon's sons killed by Abimelech, or did one survive?

**References:** Judges 8:30-31; Judges 9:5

**The claim:** Judges 8:30 states that Gideon had 'seventy sons of his body begotten, for he had many wives.' Judges 9:5 then states that Abimelech 'slew his brethren the sons of Jerubbaal [Gideon], being threescore and ten persons [seventy], upon one stone,' but the same verse immediately adds, 'notwithstanding yet Jotham the youngest son of Jerubbaal was left; for he hid himself' -- raising a counting question of whether the text means all seventy were killed (in tension with Jotham's stated survival) or sixty-nine were killed with Jotham as the surviving seventieth.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The verse is naturally read as using 'seventy' as the round, total number of Gideon's sons as a group (matching 8:30's stated figure exactly), immediately followed by an explicit exception clause identifying Jotham, the youngest, as the one member of that group of seventy who escaped -- a common and unremarkable narrative pattern in which a round total is given for a group before immediately noting a specific exception to it, rather than a claim that literally all seventy, without any survivor, were killed. The Hebrew and every English translation preserve exactly this structure (a total figure followed immediately by 'notwithstanding... was left'), which most naturally functions as the narrator's own clarifying qualification rather than an internally contradictory count -- comparable to saying 'he killed the whole family, except the youngest, who escaped,' where 'the whole family' is understood to describe the group as a whole rather than asserting the survivor's death alongside everyone else's.

## Literary Composition

### 13. The Gibeah atrocity and the Sodom narrative: two accounts of one story, or two tragically similar events?

**References:** Judges 19:1-30; Genesis 19:1-11

**The claim:** Judges 19's account of the Levite and his concubine at Gibeah closely parallels Genesis 19's account of the two angels at Sodom in structure and specific detail: a traveler is taken in by a host in a city square; the men of the city surround the house demanding to 'know' the male guest; the host offers his own daughter (and, in Judges, also the concubine) to the mob instead; the guest(s) narrowly escape the immediate threat. The close correspondence raises the question of whether one narrative has been modeled on, or duplicated from, the other, rather than describing two independent historical episodes.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both narratives plainly and consistently as given, preserving their distinct settings, participants, and outcomes; the literary relationship between them is a matter of composition and rhetorical strategy, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, across a range of critical and conservative approaches, read the correspondence as a deliberate literary strategy on the part of Judges' author/compiler rather than evidence that one event did not really happen or that the accounts have been confused with one another: by narrating the men of Gibeah -- an Israelite town, in the tribal territory of Benjamin -- replicating the behavior of Sodom, one of the Old Testament's most notorious examples of a city given over to wickedness and divine judgment, the author underscores just how far Israel's own moral condition had degenerated during the era 'when there was no king in Israel' (19:1, 21:25), a central editorial theme of the book's closing chapters. The correspondence is read as intentional, pointed literary echo -- inviting the reader to measure Gibeah against Sodom and find Israel's own conduct comparably damning -- rather than as evidence that the two underlying historical events are actually one and the same.

### 14. 'Everyone did what was right in his own eyes' -- a critique of the whole era, or specifically of its final chapters?

**References:** Judges 17:6; Judges 21:25; Judges 2:11-19

**The claim:** Judges twice states, at the close of the book (17:6, 21:25), 'In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes' -- a strongly negative assessment. Yet the bulk of the book (chapters 3-16) narrates a repeating cycle in which God himself directly raises up and empowers judges to deliver Israel, seemingly describing divinely sanctioned leadership rather than mere anarchic self-rule, which some read as in tension with the closing refrain's negative framing of 'those days' as a whole.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the refrain and the deliverer-cycle summary (2:11-19) plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is a matter of recognizing the book's own two-part literary structure, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most literary analyses of Judges' structure read the book as intentionally organized in two distinct parts with two different rhetorical purposes: chapters 3-16 present the recurring deliverer cycle (sin, oppression, crying out, deliverance, and relapse, explicitly summarized in 2:11-19) as a period of real, if repeatedly interrupted, divine faithfulness toward an unfaithful people, while chapters 17-21 -- where the 'no king... right in his own eyes' refrain actually appears, bracketing this section specifically -- narrate a set of episodes (the Micah/Levite idolatry story, the Levite's concubine and the Benjaminite civil war) with no judge or deliverer figure present at all, illustrating just how thoroughly Israel's social and religious order had collapsed once even the imperfect leadership of the judges era gave way to pure individual autonomy. On this reading, the refrain functions as the book's closing editorial commentary specifically on its final section's total leadership vacuum, inviting the reader (writing, most scholars agree, from a vantage point after the monarchy had been established) to see the preceding chaos as an argument for why Israel needed the kind of centralized, godly leadership a king could in principle provide -- not as a blanket condemnation retroactively applied to every judge and event across the whole book.

## Miracles and Literary Genre

### 15. Do the prose and poetic accounts of Sisera's death actually conflict?

**References:** Judges 4:17-22; Judges 5:24-27

**The claim:** Judges 4's prose narrative and Judges 5's poetic 'Song of Deborah,' describing the same event, are sometimes cited as an example of two conflicting accounts, given the differences in style and detail between a prose battle report and a victory poem.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both chapters plainly and consistently as given; a careful comparison shows agreement rather than conflict, and no translation differences are relevant to this entry.

**Reconciliation:** A close comparison of the two chapters shows them to be fundamentally consistent rather than conflicting: both agree that Jael, wife of Heber the Kenite, killed the fleeing Canaanite general Sisera by driving a tent peg through his head while he lay in her tent (4:21; 5:26-27), both agree he had sought refuge and been offered milk or curds to drink and covered for rest (4:18-19; 5:25), and both credit Jael alone with the decisive act. The poetic account in chapter 5 uses more vivid, compressed, and emotionally heightened language appropriate to victory poetry (including an imagined scene of Sisera's mother anxiously awaiting his return, 5:28-30, which has no prose counterpart but does not conflict with anything the prose account states), but does not narrate a different sequence of events or a different killer. This entry is included as a caution that not every claimed instance of two accounts 'disagreeing' survives a close side-by-side reading -- in this case, the two chapters substantively agree.
