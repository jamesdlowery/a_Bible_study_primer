# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Sirach (Ecclesiasticus)

**A note on this document's place in the series:** As with Tobit, Judith, and the Wisdom of
Solomon, Sirach is a deuterocanonical book included in only one of the ten translations
surveyed in this project: RSV2CE, a Catholic edition. The other nine (AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV,
NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT) follow the Protestant and Jewish canon, which excludes it. This
document notes, for each entry, that nine of the ten translations do not contain the book at
all.

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable theological
questions involving Sirach, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with
Genesis, Romans, 1 Timothy, Proverbs, 2 Chronicles, Daniel, and Ecclesiastes. For each item,
this document states the claim as it is typically raised and gives the standard scholarly
reconciliation.

**A note on this book's most distinctive feature:** Entry 1 highlights something unique among
every book addressed in this entire series: Sirach names its actual historical author within
its own text and is preceded by a translator's preface (written by the author's own grandson)
supplying an unusually precise date. This stands in direct contrast to the pseudonymous Wisdom
of Solomon and the genuinely disputed authorship of Ecclesiastes and Song of Solomon, addressed
in their own companion documents.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 2 were both checked in detail against multiple sources,
including academic and translators'-handbook-level scholarship on Sirach 25:24's genuinely
disputed relationship to Eve and universal human death. The remaining entries are theological
questions addressed from standard scholarly positions.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by relevant scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly disagreement remains, an honest account
of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary text directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. The only book in the entire Bible or deuterocanon with a named author and a securely datable translator's preface

**References:** Sirach 50:27; Sirach (Prologue)

**The claim:** Unlike every other book addressed in this project -- including the pseudonymous Wisdom of Solomon and the disputed Ecclesiastes and Song of Solomon, both addressed at length in their own companion documents -- Sirach names its actual historical author directly within the text itself: 'Instruction in understanding and knowledge I have written in this book, Jesus [Yeshua] the son of Sirach, son of Eleazar, of Jerusalem' (50:27). The book is further preceded by a prologue, written by the author's own grandson, explaining that he translated his grandfather's original Hebrew work into Greek after arriving in Egypt 'in the thirty-eighth year of the reign of Euergetes' -- a specific regnal reference allowing modern scholars to date the grandson's arrival with unusual precision to 132 BC.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus). It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Sirach is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the full text of Sirach as canonical Scripture. The book concludes (50:27) naming its author directly: 'Instruction in understanding and knowledge I have written in this book, Jesus [Yeshua] the son of Sirach, son of Eleazar, of Jerusalem,' and is preceded by the grandson's translator's prologue referencing his arrival in Egypt in the 38th year of Euergetes' reign (verified against the published text and independently corroborated by the ~132 BC dating this yields).

**Reconciliation:** This is not a point of contradiction but a notable point of contrast this project highlights explicitly: Sirach is widely recognized, including by secular and Jewish scholarship with no particular stake in its canonical status, as one of the most securely and precisely dated books in the entire biblical and deuterocanonical corpus. The regnal reference is unambiguous (only two Ptolemaic kings bore the epithet 'Euergetes,' and the reign lengths of each allow scholars to identify Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II, whose reign is dated with confidence from 170 BC, placing the grandson's arrival at 132 BC); working backward two generations places the grandfather's original Hebrew composition in the 180s BC, a dating further corroborated by internal evidence -- the book eulogizes the high priest Simon II (chapter 50), who died around 196 BC, while showing no awareness whatsoever of the Maccabean crisis beginning in 167 BC, bracketing the likely composition date narrowly between these two points. This unusual precision is further reinforced by external manuscript evidence unavailable for most other books discussed in this series: a twelfth-century Hebrew manuscript of Sirach was rediscovered in Cairo in 1896, and additional Hebrew fragments, independently dated to the first century BC, were later found among the Dead Sea Scrolls and at Masada, allowing scholars to compare portions of the grandson's own Greek translation directly against fragments of his grandfather's original Hebrew wording -- a level of textual verification not available for most other ancient works of any kind.

## Theological Tensions

### 2. 'From a woman sin had its beginning' -- Eve, or a bad wife specifically? And how this relates to Romans 5 and 1 Timothy 2

**References:** Sirach 25:13-26; Genesis 3:1-19; Romans 5:12; 1 Timothy 2:13-14

**The claim:** Sirach 25:24 states, 'From a woman sin had its beginning, and because of her we all die' -- language widely read as attributing the origin of universal human sin and death specifically to Eve, in some tension with Paul's own statement in Romans 5:12 that 'sin came into the world through one man' (Adam, not Eve), even though 1 Timothy 2:13-14 separately emphasizes that 'Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived and became a transgressor' -- meaning the New Testament's own two most relevant passages do not fully agree with each other on how responsibility for the Fall should be apportioned between Adam and Eve, and Sirach adds a third, seemingly still different emphasis.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus). It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Sirach is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (25:24): "From a woman sin had its beginning, and because of her we all die" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated question among biblical scholars on two separate levels, and this entry presents both rather than resolving either definitively. First, on whether Sirach 25:24 is even about Eve and universal human death at all: a substantial body of more recent scholarship, reflected in a published translators' handbook on Sirach citing the scholar John Levison's specific argument, holds that this verse, read within its immediate surrounding context, most likely uses 'death' hyperbolically. 25:13-26 is entirely devoted to the miseries a bad wife specifically brings upon her own husband, not a general statement about womankind or all humanity, so the verse most likely describes the emotionally and practically ruinous effect an evil wife has on her own husband's life and happiness -- not a doctrinal claim about the origin of universal human mortality traceable to Eve at all. This reading is supported by noting that Sirach elsewhere never otherwise connects Adam and Eve's sin to the origin of death (17:1-2 presents death simply as an ordinary part of God's created intention for human life), and, only two verses after 25:24, explicitly states that a good wife lengthens her husband's life (26:1-2). Other scholars read 25:24 as intending exactly what its traditional interpretation holds: an early witness within Second Temple Jewish thought to interpreting Genesis 3 as placing primary blame on Eve specifically, consistent with, and likely directly informing, 1 Timothy 2:14's own related emphasis on Eve's deception. On the second question -- Paul's own apparent difference in emphasis between Romans 5's Adam-centered account and 1 Timothy 2's Eve-centered account -- most commentators hold these address different aspects of the same event rather than contradicting each other. Romans 5 addresses federal, representative headship: Adam, not Eve, is treated throughout Scripture as humanity's covenantal representative, whose disobedience is therefore what formally 'brought' sin's reigning power into the world. 1 Timothy 2:14, by contrast, addresses the specific manner and sequence of the couple's individual deception -- Eve was directly deceived by the serpent, while Adam, present but not himself deceived, sinned with full knowledge. This is a distinction between representative legal responsibility and the specific psychological and moral character of each individual's own transgression, and these two passages are generally read as addressing separately rather than competing to assign blame.

### 3. Kindness to a father 'will be credited to you against your sins' -- good deeds atoning for sin?

**References:** Sirach 3:1-16

**The claim:** Sirach 3:14-15, part of an extended teaching on honoring one's parents, states, 'kindness to a father will not be forgotten... it will be credited to you against your sins' -- language suggesting that a specific good deed (caring for an aging parent) can offset or atone for one's own sins, raising a question about how this relates to the rest of Scripture's more typical emphasis on sacrifice, repentance, and (in New Testament theology) faith in Christ's own atoning work as the proper means of addressing sin, rather than compensatory good deeds.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus). It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Sirach is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (3:14-15): "For kindness to a father will not be forgotten, and will be credited to you against your sins; in the day of your distress it will be remembered in your favor" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this passage as reflecting a broader and well-attested strand of Second Temple Jewish theology (also found in other Jewish wisdom and ethical literature of the period) that held acts of charity, mercy, and honor toward parents could have an atoning or mitigating effect on a person's accumulated record of sin -- a theology in some tension with, but not entirely foreign to, ideas found elsewhere in the canonical wisdom literature (compare Proverbs 10:12's 'love covers all offenses,' and Daniel 4:27's counsel to Nebuchadnezzar to 'atone for your sins by righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed'), rather than as a fully developed, systematic doctrine of salvation by works displacing the need for genuine repentance or, in later Christian theological terms, Christ's own atoning sacrifice. Most theologians who affirm Sirach's canonical and inspired status generally read this as a genuine but partial insight into the moral and relational significance of concrete acts of love and honor (which Scripture consistently commends as evidence of, rather than a substitute for, a right relationship with God), while holding that fuller New Testament revelation clarifies and does not contradict this insight by locating ultimate atonement specifically and exclusively in Christ's own work, into which such genuine acts of charity are received as fruit rather than independent payment.

### 4. 'Honor the physician... for the Lord created him' -- vs. King Asa's condemnation for seeking physicians rather than the LORD

**References:** Sirach 38:1-15; 2 Chronicles 16:12

**The claim:** Sirach 38:1-15 offers an extended, remarkably positive theology of medicine: 'Honor the physician with the honor due him, according to your need of him, for the Lord created him... the Lord created medicines from the earth, and a sensible man will not despise them... There is a time when success lies in the hands of physicians, for they too will pray to the Lord that he grant them success in diagnosis and in healing' -- explicitly affirming both medicine and physicians as part of God's own good provision, to be honored and used without any suggestion this competes with or displaces trust in God. This stands in some contrast to 2 Chronicles 16:12's brief, critical account of King Asa's final illness: 'even in his disease he did not seek the LORD, but sought help from physicians' -- language read by some as implying that seeking medical treatment reflects a lack of trust in God.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus). It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Sirach is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (38:1-4): "Honor the physician with the honor due him, according to your need of him, for the Lord created him... The Lord created medicines from the earth, and a sensible man will not despise them," and (38:14) that physicians "pray to the Lord that he should grant them success in diagnosis and in healing" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not read 2 Chronicles 16:12 as a general condemnation of seeking medical treatment as such, but as addressing Asa's own specific, complete pattern of abandoning reliance on God evident throughout the entire surrounding narrative (2 Chronicles 16 as a whole recounts Asa relying on a foreign alliance rather than God for military security, imprisoning the prophet who rebuked him for it, and oppressing his own people, 16:7-10) -- the criticism in context concerns Asa's total, entrenched pattern of self-reliance and refusal to seek God at any point, of which the physicians detail is presented as the final illustration, not a standalone claim that consulting physicians is itself sinful or contrary to genuine faith. Sirach 38's fuller theological reflection on medicine, composed centuries later, is generally read as making explicit and developing at length a principle already compatible with, rather than contradicting, the Asa narrative's own actual point -- physicians and medicine are themselves gifts of God's own providence (Sirach's own explicit point) to be received and used gratefully alongside, not instead of, ongoing trust in and prayer to God (which Sirach 38:9 itself explicitly commends: 'my child, when you are ill, do not delay, but pray to the Lord, and he will heal you'), meaning both texts are compatible once Asa's own specific, exclusive self-reliance -- not medical treatment generally -- is identified as the actual target of the earlier text's criticism.

## Ethical Tensions

### 5. Sirach's severe statements about women -- an outlier within the wisdom tradition's more balanced treatment elsewhere

**References:** Sirach 25:13-26; Sirach 42:14; Proverbs 31:10-31

**The claim:** Beyond 25:24 (addressed in entry 2), Sirach contains several further severe statements about women in general: 'there is no venom worse than a snake's venom, and no anger worse than a woman's wrath' (25:15), and 'better is the wickedness of a man than a woman who does good' (42:14) -- language considerably harsher in its general characterization of women than the wisdom tradition's treatment elsewhere, most notably Proverbs 31:10-31's extended, warmly admiring acrostic poem praising an excellent wife's industriousness, wisdom, and business acumen.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus). It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Sirach is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (25:15): "There is no venom worse than a snake's venom, and no anger worse than a woman's wrath," and includes the further severe statement at 42:14 (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that Sirach's harshest statements occur within extended passages specifically and explicitly addressing a bad wife or shameless daughter (25:13-26's entire context concerns marital misery caused specifically by an evil wife, and much of the surrounding material in chapters 9, 25-26, and 42 addresses the specific anxieties an ancient Mediterranean patriarchal household head might have held regarding daughters' sexual conduct and wives' fidelity, reflecting concerns and social anxieties characteristic of the author's own particular historical and cultural setting), rather than universal claims about women's inherent moral nature as a class -- the same chapters that contain Sirach's harshest statements about bad wives also contain warm praise for a good wife (26:1-4, 13-18, closely paralleling Proverbs 31's own praise), indicating the author's own intended contrast is between good and bad wives specifically, not a blanket claim about women generally. This project does not minimize that Sirach's specific rhetoric, particularly at 42:14, is considerably harsher and more one-sidedly attributed to women's failings than comparable material addressing men's own failings elsewhere in wisdom literature, and treats this as a genuine feature of the text reflecting its author's own particular time, place, and social assumptions, which later interpreters and readers are not obligated to adopt uncritically simply because the book is regarded as canonical by traditions that recognize it -- a document's canonical status, in traditions that recognize Sirach, has not historically been understood to require treating every specific cultural assumption or rhetorical excess within it as itself a timeless moral prescription.

## Theological Interpretation

### 6. A caution against investigating 'what is too difficult for you' -- vs. the wisdom tradition's call to actively seek understanding

**References:** Sirach 3:21-23; Proverbs 2:1-5; Ecclesiastes 7:16

**The claim:** Sirach 3:21-23 counsels restraint in intellectual inquiry: 'Neither seek what is too difficult for you, nor investigate what is beyond your power... Reflect upon what has been assigned to you, for you do not need what is hidden' -- language of epistemic humility and restraint that some read as standing in tension with the wisdom tradition's more typical, energetic encouragement to actively and diligently pursue wisdom and understanding (Proverbs 2:1-5's call to 'seek it like silver' and 'search for it as for hidden treasures').

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus). It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Sirach is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (3:21-22): "Neither seek what is too difficult for you, nor investigate what is beyond your power... Reflect upon what you have been commanded, for what is hidden is not your concern" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This question parallels, and is generally resolved the same way as, the comparable tension addressed in this project's companion Ecclesiastes document regarding Ecclesiastes 7:16's caution against being 'too wise.' Most commentators read Sirach 3:21-23 not as discouraging the vigorous pursuit of genuine, accessible wisdom (which Sirach itself, as an extensive wisdom book, obviously commends and models throughout its own fifty-one chapters), but as cautioning specifically against a prideful, presumptuous curiosity toward matters God has not chosen to reveal -- speculative inquiry into hidden, esoteric, or forbidden mysteries beyond what has actually been disclosed, rather than the diligent study and application of wisdom genuinely available and 'assigned' to ordinary human life and conduct, which the immediately surrounding verses (3:17-20, 24-29) continue to commend in the same breath as this specific caution.
