# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Numbers

This document catalogs 23 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Numbers, either entirely internal to Numbers or between Numbers and a directly related passage
elsewhere (Deuteronomy, Exodus, 1 Chronicles, Psalms, 1 Corinthians, 2 Peter, Jude,
Revelation, 2 Kings). For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised,
notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the
translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in
English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, including candid acknowledgment (entry 6)
of the one case in this set where no fully satisfying explanation commands scholarly
consensus. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references
directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Did the sons of Korah die with him, or survive?

**References:** Numbers 16:31-33; Numbers 26:9-11

**The claim:** Numbers 16 describes the earth swallowing Korah, his fellow rebels, and 'all that belonged to them,' including their households, in judgment. Numbers 26:11 explicitly states, 'the sons of Korah, however, did not die.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses plainly as given, preserving the same explicit exception in Numbers 26:11; the harmonization is drawn directly from the text's own wording, not from translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is generally read as the text itself supplying its own clarification rather than a genuine conflict: 'all that belonged to Korah' in chapter 16 is understood to mean his direct household present with him at the moment of judgment, while his sons (evidently not standing with him in the rebellion at that specific moment, or providentially spared) are explicitly noted in chapter 26 as a distinct exception -- a distinction the text itself draws attention to, rather than an unnoticed inconsistency. The sons of Korah are, in fact, credited as authors or dedicatees of several psalms (e.g., Psalms 42, 84), consistent with their line continuing.

### 2. God tells Balaam to go -- then is angry that he went?

**References:** Numbers 22:12; Numbers 22:20; Numbers 22:22

**The claim:** God initially tells Balaam not to go with Balak's messengers (22:12). When a second, more distinguished delegation arrives, God tells Balaam, 'if the men have come to call you, rise, go with them' (22:20). Yet the very next verse states, 'God's anger was kindled because he went' (22:22), and sends the angel with the drawn sword to oppose him.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render all three verses plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is exegetical, concerning the discernment of Balaam's underlying motive, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization reads God's permission in 22:20 as conditional and probing rather than a straightforward endorsement: God discerns that Balaam's heart is already set on going for the sake of Balak's promised reward (a motive 22:20's own permission is given in light of, and which 2 Peter 2:15 and Jude 11 later explicitly condemn as 'the way of Balaam,' loving 'the wages of unrighteousness'), and grants Balaam's own persistent request while still disapproving of the underlying motive -- a pattern some compare to how God 'gave Israel what they asked for' regarding a king or quail, while the request itself reflected an improper heart (compare Psalm 106:15, 1 Samuel 8). The anger in 22:22, on this reading, addresses Balaam's manner of going (with mercenary intent, and evidently without the restraint God's permission implied) rather than an outright reversal of the permission granted two verses earlier.

### 3. Did God command the spies to be sent into Canaan, or did the people request it?

**References:** Numbers 13:1-2; Deuteronomy 1:19-23

**The claim:** Numbers 13:1-2 opens, 'The LORD said to Moses, Send men to spy out the land of Canaan.' Deuteronomy 1:22-23, Moses' own later retelling of the same episode, has Moses say, 'all of you came near me and said, Let us send men before us,' with the initiative credited to the people rather than to a divine command, and Moses states 'the thing seemed good to me' before acting on it.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the sequence in the text; the resolution rests on reading Numbers as recording the divine command and Deuteronomy as filling in its human background, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that both are true of the same event at different stages: the people first proposed and requested the reconnaissance mission (as Moses recounts in Deuteronomy), Moses found the request reasonable and brought it before the LORD, and the LORD then formally authorized and commanded it (as Numbers 13:1-2 records) -- with Numbers narrating the divine authorization stage and Deuteronomy, in Moses' later first-person sermon, filling in the human origin of the proposal that preceded it. Both texts are compatible with a request originating with the people, being approved by Moses, and then formally commanded by God, rather than describing two competing, mutually exclusive accounts of whose idea it was.

### 4. Why, exactly, was Moses barred from entering the Promised Land?

**References:** Numbers 20:2-13; Numbers 20:24; Numbers 27:12-14; Deuteronomy 1:37; Deuteronomy 3:26; Deuteronomy 4:21; Psalm 106:32-33

**The claim:** Numbers 20 attributes Moses' exclusion from the Promised Land directly to his own conduct at the rock at Meribah (striking the rock in anger rather than speaking to it as commanded, and failing to 'believe' or 'uphold [God] as holy' before the people). Deuteronomy, in Moses' own later retelling, several times states that the LORD was angry with Moses 'because of you' (i.e., because of the people, 1:37, 3:26, 4:21) -- appearing, in isolated reading, to shift responsibility onto the Israelites rather than Moses' own specific failure at the rock.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render all the relevant passages plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the language explicitly in the text; the resolution is exegetical, drawn from reading Numbers and Deuteronomy's accounts together, not from translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as complementary rather than competing explanations rather than a strict either/or: the Meribah incident (Numbers 20) was itself precipitated by the people's complaining and rebellion, so Deuteronomy's 'because of you' language can describe the people as the proximate occasion or provocation for Moses' own failure of temper and faith, without denying that Moses' particular response at the rock -- rather than the people's provocation itself -- was the specific act for which he personally was held accountable, exactly as Numbers 20:12 and 27:14 state directly. Both threads (the people's provocation, and Moses' own personal failure in response to it) are treated in Scripture's own theology of individual responsibility as compatible parts of a single causal chain, not mutually exclusive explanations competing for the single 'real' reason.

### 5. The bronze serpent: a God-commanded means of healing, later destroyed as idolatry

**References:** Numbers 21:8-9; 2 Kings 18:4

**The claim:** Numbers 21:8-9 has God directly command Moses to make a bronze serpent and mount it on a pole, so that anyone bitten by the fiery serpents sent as judgment could look at it and live -- an explicitly God-ordained, effective means of healing. Centuries later, 2 Kings 18:4 records that King Hezekiah, praised in the text for doing 'what was right in the eyes of the LORD,' 'broke in pieces the bronze serpent that Moses had made,' because by that time 'the people of Israel had made offerings to it' and it had become an object of idolatrous worship (given the name Nehushtan).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given, without softening either the original healing purpose or Hezekiah's later destruction of the object; the harmonization is a straightforward reading of a single historical arc, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not generally treated as a contradiction between two competing claims but as a single, coherent narrative arc most commentators read straightforwardly: an object that was legitimately used, by God's own command, for a specific healing purpose in Moses' generation was later, centuries afterward, misused by the people as an idol in its own right -- a corruption of its original, limited purpose that Hezekiah's destruction of it corrects, restoring right worship rather than reversing or contradicting the original Numbers narrative. The text nowhere claims the bronze serpent itself possessed ongoing, independent divine power meant to be venerated after the wilderness generation's immediate crisis had passed; Hezekiah's action is presented approvingly, as reform rather than as a rejection of Numbers 21's account.

### 6. Were the Levites to be numbered, or specifically excluded from being numbered?

**References:** Numbers 1:47-49; Numbers 3:14-16

**The claim:** Numbers 1:47-49 has God explicitly instructing Moses, 'the tribe of Levi thou shalt not number, neither take the sum of them among the children of Israel,' excluding the Levites from the general military census. Numbers 3:14-16, only two chapters later, has God telling Moses, 'Number the children of Levi after the house of their fathers, by their families,' directly commanding what the earlier passage seemed to forbid.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The text itself supplies the distinguishing purpose of each numbering, immediately in context: Numbers 1's census is explicitly a military muster of men 'able to go forth to war' (1:3), from which the Levites are excluded because they are set apart for tabernacle service rather than combat duty (1:47-53 explains this exemption at length, assigning the Levites responsibility for the tabernacle instead). Numbers 3's numbering is a separate, specifically Levitical census -- counting 'every male from a month old and upward' (3:15) for the distinct purpose of assigning tabernacle duties and calculating the redemption of Israel's firstborn (3:40-51) -- a different census, for a different purpose, of a different age range, not a reversal of the first exclusion.

### 7. Was Moses genuinely meek, given his anger at the officers who spared the Midianite women?

**References:** Numbers 12:3; Numbers 31:14-18

**The claim:** Numbers 12:3 describes Moses as 'very meek, above all the men which were upon the face of the earth.' Numbers 31:14 records Moses becoming angry ('wroth') with the military officers after the Midianite campaign, sharply rebuking them for sparing the women and commanding further, severe action against them.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Biblical meekness, as the term is used throughout Scripture, describes humility and self-restraint in the face of personal insult or provocation (the immediate context of Numbers 12:3 being Miriam and Aaron's personal challenge to Moses' authority, which Moses does not retaliate against himself) rather than an absence of righteous indignation at wrongdoing or disobedience to God's command -- the same pattern is seen in Jesus, described in Scripture as 'meek and lowly in heart' (Matthew 11:29) while also displaying righteous anger at the temple's desecration (John 2:13-17) and strong rebuke of religious hypocrisy (Matthew 23). Moses' anger in Numbers 31:14 is directed at the officers' specific failure to carry out God's commanded judgment on the nation responsible for seducing Israel into the Baal of Peor apostasy (Numbers 25), a matter of covenant obedience, not a personal grievance -- entirely compatible with the personal humility 12:3 describes.

### 8. Did Caleb alone argue for entering Canaan, or did Joshua join him from the start?

**References:** Numbers 13:30; Numbers 14:6-9

**The claim:** Numbers 13:30 has Caleb alone speaking up to still the people's fear: 'Caleb stilled the people before Moses, and said, Let us go up at once, and possess it.' Only in the next chapter, Numbers 14:6-9, does the text have both 'Joshua the son of Nun, and Caleb the son of Jephunneh' together tearing their clothes and jointly urging the people not to rebel.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two passages are read as sequential rather than conflicting: Numbers 13:30 records the first, immediate response as the spies' negative report was just beginning to spread panic through the camp, with Caleb the first to speak up in that specific moment; Numbers 14:6-9 records a second, more developed appeal the following day (14:1 marks a new night having passed) after the people's rebellion had fully escalated to talk of stoning Moses and returning to Egypt (14:1-4), at which point Joshua joins Caleb in the more urgent, dramatic protest of torn clothing. Nothing in the text states Joshua was silent or opposed in chapter 13; his absence from that specific verse's brief quotation of Caleb is consistent with an author naming only the most prominent early speaker at that moment, not asserting Joshua's non-involvement.

### 9. Did Israel kill every male in Midian, given that Midianites reappear generations later in Judges?

**References:** Numbers 31:7-11; Judges 6:1-6

**The claim:** Numbers 31:7 states that Israel 'warred against Midian, as the LORD commanded Moses; and they slew all the males.' Yet Midian reappears as a substantial, thriving nation only a few generations later in Judges 6-8, oppressing Israel with camel-mounted raiders 'like grasshoppers for multitude' (Judges 6:5) under Gideon's time.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The campaign narrated in Numbers 31 is explicitly a punitive strike specifically against the Midianite clans directly implicated in the Baal of Peor seduction narrated in Numbers 25 -- 31:16 explicitly ties the campaign to 'the counsel of Balaam' in that specific incident -- not a claim to have exterminated the entire, widespread Midianite people across all their territories. Midian was a large confederation of tribes spread across a wide area of the Arabian peninsula and Transjordan region, and the biblical text elsewhere assumes Midian's continued existence shortly afterward (compare Midianite kings and elders still active in Numbers 31 itself, and the Midianites' continued presence in Genesis and later texts). 'All the males' is best read, consistent with the ordinary hyperbolic and locally-scoped conventions of ancient Near Eastern military narrative (a pattern addressed at greater length elsewhere in this project regarding similarly totalizing conquest language), as describing a complete, decisive victory over the specific Midianite forces and settlements targeted in this particular campaign -- not the annihilation of every Midianite male across the entire region for all time.

## Numbers and Statistics

### 10. How many died in the plague after the Baal of Peor incident: 24,000 or 23,000?

**References:** Numbers 25:9; 1 Corinthians 10:8

**The claim:** Numbers 25:9 states 24,000 died in the plague; Paul, referencing the same event in 1 Corinthians 10:8, gives 23,000.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both numbers exactly as given; the harmonization is a matter of recognizing different scopes, not translation.

**Reconciliation:** A common and textually reasonable harmonization is that Numbers 25:9 gives the total death toll from the whole episode (idolatry, immorality, and the subsequent plague combined, including those executed by the judges per 25:4-5), while Paul in 1 Corinthians 10:8 specifies only those who died 'in a single day' from the plague itself -- a subset of the total, consistent with both figures being accurate descriptions of different scopes within the same event.

### 11. Do the Levite clan totals in Numbers 3 add up to the stated grand total?

**References:** Numbers 3:21-22; Numbers 3:27-28; Numbers 3:33-34; Numbers 3:39

**The claim:** Numbers 3 gives individual totals for the three Levite clans: the Gershonites at 7,500 (3:21-22), the Kohathites at 8,600 (3:27-28), and the Merarites at 6,200 (3:33-34). Added together, these three figures total 22,300. Yet Numbers 3:39 states the grand total of all Levites numbered was 22,000 -- a discrepancy of 300.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render all four figures (7,500; 8,600; 6,200; and the 22,000 total) exactly as given in the Masoretic Hebrew text, without adjusting any of them to make the arithmetic resolve; no translation differences are involved in this discrepancy.

**Reconciliation:** This is a long-recognized numerical puzzle within the Masoretic Hebrew text, and unlike some of the Kings/Chronicles numeral splits addressed elsewhere in this project, it is not resolved by an alternative reading in the Septuagint or another ancient version, which generally preserve the same figures. The most commonly offered explanations include: (1) a scribal transmission error in one or more of the individual clan subtotals (most often suspected in the Kohathite figure of 8,600, which several commentators have proposed may originally have read a smaller number); (2) the 300 may represent firstborn Levites already otherwise accounted for or excluded from the 'total' figure of 3:39 for a specific administrative reason the text does not spell out; no explanation commands full scholarly consensus, and this is generally treated as one of the more difficult unresolved numerical puzzles in the Pentateuch, honestly acknowledged as such rather than confidently explained away.

### 12. At what age did Levites begin their tabernacle service -- 30, or 25?

**References:** Numbers 4:3; Numbers 4:23; Numbers 4:30; Numbers 8:24

**The claim:** Numbers 4 (verses 3, 23, and 30) repeatedly specifies that Levites begin their assigned service at age 30 (through age 50). Numbers 8:24, addressing Levite service more generally, states the age of entry as 25.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '30' at Numbers 4:3, 23, 30 and '25' at Numbers 8:24 exactly as given, without adjusting either figure to match the other; the harmonization concerns distinguishing stages or types of Levitical service, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The most widely held harmonization treats these as describing two different stages or types of service: age 25 (Numbers 8:24) marking the beginning of an apprenticeship or preparatory period of training and assisting under the supervision of older, fully active Levites, with age 30 (Numbers 4) marking the point of full, independent responsibility for the specific heavy labor of transporting the tabernacle's furnishings described in that chapter -- a distinction supported by 1 Chronicles 23:24-27, which records a later adjustment of the entry age to 20, explicitly justified by David on the basis that the Levites' load-bearing transport duties (the very duties Numbers 4 describes) were no longer needed once the tabernacle had a permanent, non-portable location, implying the age requirements in Numbers were tied to the specific physical demands of that particular era of service.

### 13. Two censuses of fighting men -- do the totals make sense given the intervening losses?

**References:** Numbers 1:45-46; Numbers 26:51

**The claim:** The first census, taken near the start of the wilderness period, counts 603,550 fighting men (Numbers 1:46). The second census, taken near the end of the forty years (after the deaths of the entire first generation, repeated plagues, the Korah rebellion, and the Baal of Peor judgment), counts 601,730 (Numbers 26:51) -- a decline of only about 1,820, which some ask whether it plausibly reflects the scale of losses narrated across Numbers.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both totals exactly as given, without adjusting either number; the resolution concerns recognizing that the two censuses count two different generations, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The relatively small net change between the two totals is not, on close reading, surprising or contradictory: the first generation's forty years of decline (through natural mortality alone, given an already-adult population, plus the specific judgments recorded) occurred alongside forty years of new births into the second generation reaching military age, so the two censuses reflect two entirely different sets of individuals (per the LORD's explicit statement at Numbers 26:64-65 that none of the men counted in the first census, save Caleb and Joshua, remained alive for the second) with a similar overall national scale, not a claim that the same 603,550 men merely shrank by 1,820 through attrition.

### 14. Did only Caleb and Joshua survive of the whole first generation to see the promised land?

**References:** Numbers 14:29-30; Numbers 26:63-65; Deuteronomy 2:14-16

**The claim:** Numbers 14:29-30 and 26:63-65 both state that of those numbered in the wilderness census, none would enter the promised land except Caleb and Joshua -- language some read as claiming every single adult of the first generation, without exception, died in the wilderness. Deuteronomy 2:14-16 similarly emphasizes that 'the men of war' were consumed from the camp before Israel could cross into the land.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both Numbers passages specifically and consistently scope the judgment to those included in the original military census -- 'all that were numbered of you, according to your whole number, from twenty years old and upward, which have murmured against me' (Numbers 14:29) -- meaning adult men of military age at the time of the rebellion, a scope Deuteronomy 2:14's 'men of war' independently confirms. Women, Levites (who were separately numbered and exempted from the general military census, per this document's own entry on that subject), and anyone under twenty years old at the time of the rebellion are nowhere included in this specific judgment and are not stated to have died in the wilderness -- indeed, it is precisely this untouched younger generation, grown to adulthood by the end of the forty years, that Numbers 26's second census counts entering the land. 'Only Caleb and Joshua' therefore refers specifically to survivors from among that one particular counted group (military-age men at the time of the rebellion), not a claim that literally only two individuals of any age or status survived the entire forty years.

### 15. Does the new moon offering's prescribed recipe in Numbers match its description in Ezekiel?

**References:** Numbers 28:11-15; Ezekiel 46:6-7

**The claim:** Numbers 28:11-15 prescribes the monthly new moon offering in specific detail: two young bulls, one ram, and seven lambs as a burnt offering, with a goat for a sin offering, along with specified measures of flour and oil. Ezekiel's temple vision, centuries later, describes the same new moon offering with different figures: one young bull, six lambs, and one ram (46:6), with its own distinct grain and oil measures (46:7) that do not match the Numbers 28 figures.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the parallel question concerning the tabernacle's daily offering (addressed in this project's Exodus companion document), Ezekiel 40-48's temple vision is widely read by conservative interpreters as an idealized, symbolic, and in important respects future or eschatological picture rather than a literal blueprint meant to correct or replace the Mosaic sacrificial regulations already given through Moses -- the book explicitly signals its visionary character throughout ('in the visions of God,' 40:2), and several of its details differ in scale and specificity from any temple Israel ever actually built. On this reading, the differing figures for the new moon offering reflect the distinct purpose and literary genre of Ezekiel's prophetic vision, not a claimed correction of Numbers 28, which Ezekiel elsewhere continues to treat as authoritative background for Israel's sacrificial system generally.

## Chronology and Geography

### 16. Where -- and when -- did Aaron die?

**References:** Numbers 20:22-29; Numbers 33:38-39; Deuteronomy 10:6

**The claim:** Numbers 20:22-29 and Numbers 33:38-39 both state that Aaron died atop Mount Hor, in the fortieth year after the Exodus, at age 123. Deuteronomy 10:6, in a summary travel itinerary, states that Aaron died at Moserah (also spelled Moseroth) and was buried there.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'Mount Hor' and 'Moserah'/'Moseroth' as given in their respective passages, without harmonizing the place names in the text; the resolution rests on reading the itinerary of Numbers 33 alongside Deuteronomy 10:6, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The most common harmonization holds that Moserah/Moseroth was the name of the wider encampment or district at the base of Mount Hor, with Mount Hor itself being the specific peak within or adjacent to that district where Aaron actually died and was buried -- a relationship of a specific site to its surrounding region, comparable to describing an event as happening 'on a mountain near a particular town' in one account and simply 'at the town' in a summary account. The itinerary in Numbers 33 itself lists 'Moseroth' and 'Mount Hor' as separate, adjacent stops in the same travel sequence (33:30-31, 33:37-38), supporting the reading that these refer to a specific peak within a broader named locality rather than two entirely distinct, unrelated places.

## Names and Identity

### 17. Who was Moses' 'Cushite' wife?

**References:** Numbers 12:1; Exodus 2:16-21

**The claim:** Numbers 12:1 has Miriam and Aaron criticize Moses over 'the Cushite woman whom he had married,' identified in most translations as a Cushite (associated with the region south of Egypt, roughly modern Sudan/Ethiopia) -- yet Moses' only wife named elsewhere in the text, Zipporah, is explicitly identified as a Midianite, daughter of the Midianite priest (Exodus 2:16-21), a different and distinct people and region from Cush.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'the Cushite woman,' consistent with the modern-translation pattern.
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'the Cushite woman' (consistent with the pattern confirmed across other modern translations checked directly).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'the Cushite woman' (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'the Ethiopian woman' at Numbers 12:1 (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders 'the Ethiopian woman' (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'the Cushite woman' (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'the Cushite woman whom he had taken as a wife (for he had taken a Cushite woman)' (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'his Cushite wife... a Cushite' (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'a Cushite woman' (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'the Cushite woman whom he had married, for he had married a Cushite woman' (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** Two main harmonizations are offered: (1) 'Cushite' is understood by many scholars not to require strict geographic identity with the African region of Cush, since a related Cushan is mentioned in poetic parallel with Midian at Habakkuk 3:7 ('the tents of Cushan... the curtains of the land of Midian'), suggesting an Arabian branch or association of the Cush name that could describe Zipporah's Midianite background as well; on this view, 'the Cushite woman' simply is Zipporah, described by a broader or alternate ethnic designation. (2) Some Jewish and early Christian traditions instead identify this as a second wife (a tradition partly reflected in the ancient historian Josephus's account of an earlier Ethiopian marriage from Moses' youth in Egypt, prior to his flight to Midian), which would make Numbers 12:1 refer to someone other than Zipporah entirely. Scholars are divided between these two positions, and the text itself does not explicitly resolve which is correct.

## Theological Tensions

### 18. Was Balaam a genuine mouthpiece of the LORD, or a condemned false prophet?

**References:** Numbers 22:9-20; Numbers 23:5-12; Numbers 24:2-9; 2 Peter 2:15-16; Jude 1:11; Revelation 2:14

**The claim:** Numbers 22-24 repeatedly has the LORD speak directly to Balaam, put words in his mouth, and empower him to deliver genuine, accurate prophetic blessings over Israel that he explicitly says he cannot alter even for a large reward. Yet 2 Peter 2:15-16 and Jude 11 both condemn 'the way of Balaam' as a byword for a prophet motivated by love of money, and Revelation 2:14 associates Balaam with actively teaching Israel's enemies how to entrap Israel into sin -- casting him in a thoroughly negative light.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the Numbers oracles and the New Testament condemnations plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization draws on Numbers 31:16's own later disclosure about Balaam's advice to Balak, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These are generally read as addressing two different aspects of the same historical figure rather than contradicting each other: Numbers itself already portrays Balaam ambivalently, as someone whose prophetic gift was real and whose specific oracles were true (God can and does speak accurate words even through a compromised or mixed-motive vessel, a category with some parallel in Scripture's treatment of Caiaphas's unwitting true prophecy in John 11:49-52), while simultaneously depicting him as testing the limits of what God would allow him to do for payment (his repeated attempts to find a loophole across chapters 22-24) and, per Numbers 31:16, later advising Balak on how to draw Israel into sin at Baal-Peor through a scheme not narrated in the direct oracle chapters themselves but referenced afterward. The New Testament references draw specifically on this darker, money-and-scheme-driven side of Balaam's role (confirmed within Numbers itself at 31:16) as a moral example, without denying that his formal oracles, when he spoke them, were true words from God.

### 19. Is long hair on a man shameful, or a mark of consecration to God?

**References:** Numbers 6:5; 1 Corinthians 11:14

**The claim:** Numbers 6:5, describing the Nazirite vow, commands that 'no razor shall come upon his head... he shall let the locks of the hair of his head grow.' Paul, writing to the Corinthians, asks rhetorically, 'doth not even nature itself teach you, that if a man have long hair, it is a shame unto him?' (1 Corinthians 11:14).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization notes that the Nazirite vow (Numbers 6) was a specific, voluntary, and temporary consecration undertaken by particular individuals for a defined period as a visible sign of separation to God -- uncut hair functioning precisely as an unusual, countercultural marker of this special vow, which is what made it a recognizable sign in the first place. Paul's comment in 1 Corinthians 11:14 addresses the ordinary social convention of his own time and culture regarding customary, everyday grooming and gender presentation, not a claim that long hair is inherently or universally sinful in every circumstance -- indeed, the very unusualness Paul assumes as a general social norm is what made the deliberately long-haired Nazirite a recognizable exception to ordinary practice in the first place, rather than the two texts describing the same kind of hair-growing for the same purpose.

### 20. Should we follow our own hearts, or does the heart lead us astray?

**References:** Numbers 15:39; Ecclesiastes 11:9

**The claim:** Numbers 15:38-39 institutes the fringes on garments specifically 'that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them; and that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes, after which ye use to go a whoring' -- explicitly warning against following one's own heart. Ecclesiastes 11:9, by contrast, tells the young to 'rejoice... and walk in the ways of thine heart, and in the sight of thine eyes.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Ecclesiastes 11:9 itself supplies the qualification that resolves the apparent tension in its very next clause: 'but know thou, that for all these things God will bring thee into judgment' -- far from issuing unqualified license to follow every impulse, the verse is widely read (consistent with Ecclesiastes' recurring rhetorical pattern of voicing a worldly perspective before subjecting it to a higher, God-centered corrective) as permitting youthful joy and vigor while immediately reminding the reader that this freedom operates under, and is bounded by, ultimate divine accountability -- essentially the same caution Numbers 15:39 issues directly. Both texts agree that an unrestrained, self-directed heart uncorrected by the fear of God (Numbers 15:39's 'go a whoring,' or in Ecclesiastes' terms, forgetting future judgment) leads astray, while neither denies that a heart rightly submitted to God's commandments and coming judgment can be followed with joy.

### 21. Did Balaam actually curse Israel, or only bless them?

**References:** Numbers 23:7-12; Numbers 24:10; Deuteronomy 23:4-5; Nehemiah 13:2

**The claim:** Numbers 23-24 records Balaam repeatedly blessing Israel and explicitly refusing to curse them, to Balak's mounting frustration -- the narrative never shows Balaam actually pronouncing a curse. Yet Deuteronomy 23:4-5 and Nehemiah 13:2, looking back on the same episode, both state that Balaam was hired 'to curse' Israel and that God 'turned the curse into a blessing,' language that could be read as implying a curse was actually spoken.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Deuteronomy 23:4-5 and Nehemiah 13:2 are most naturally read as describing Balak's intent and Balaam's commissioned purpose in hiring him ('to curse thee'), which both texts explicitly state God overturned before it could be carried out -- 'the LORD thy God turned the curse into a blessing,' meaning God intervened at the level of Balaam's speech itself, so that the intended curse was never actually pronounced, exactly as Numbers 22-24 narrates it: Balaam himself explains he can only speak the words God puts in his mouth (Numbers 22:38; 23:12) and that God has not permitted him to curse. Read this way, the retrospective texts and the narrative fully agree: a curse was sought and intended, but what was actually spoken -- by God's direct intervention -- was blessing throughout, consistent with entry 12's broader discussion of Balaam's mixed and complicated role in this book.

### 22. How long does God's anger last -- a moment, or forty years?

**References:** Numbers 32:13; Psalm 30:5; Psalm 103:9

**The claim:** Numbers 32:13 states, 'the LORD's anger was kindled against Israel, and he made them wander in the wilderness forty years, until all the generation... was consumed.' Psalm 30:5 states God's 'anger endureth but a moment,' and Psalm 103:9 adds that 'he will not always chide: neither will he keep his anger for ever.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Psalms' language about the brevity of God's anger is generally read as describing God's settled disposition and his mercy's ultimate triumph over wrath in his ongoing relationship with the repentant -- anger is not his enduring, default posture toward his people, and it lifts as soon as its corrective purpose (rather than an arbitrarily fixed term) is served, consistent with the immediate context of both Psalms, which pair the statement with God's favor, forgiveness, and restoration for those who turn back to him. Numbers 32:13's forty years describes a specific historical judgment whose length was determined not by an inherent limit on divine anger's duration but by the scope of what needed to run its course -- one year for each of the forty days the spies spent in the land (Numbers 14:34 explicitly states this calculation), a period during which an entire disqualified generation would pass from the scene naturally. The two texts describe different things: God's general disposition and readiness to restore the repentant (the Psalms) versus the specific, purposeful length of one particular corrective judgment tied to a stated cause (Numbers) -- not two competing claims about a single, fixed universal duration for divine anger.

## Authorship and Composition

### 23. Could Moses have written a verse calling himself 'very meek, above all the men which were upon the face of the earth'?

**References:** Numbers 12:3

**The claim:** Numbers 12:3 states, in the third person, 'Now the man Moses was very meek, above all the men which were upon the face of the earth' -- a superlative self-description some find implausible for a genuinely meek person to have written about himself, raising a question about the verse's authorship if Moses wrote the Pentateuch.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Conservative scholars holding to Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch generally treat this verse (along with the account of Moses' own death in Deuteronomy 34) as one of a small number of passages most naturally read as a later, inspired editorial notation -- possibly added by Joshua or another prophetic successor shortly after Moses' death -- rather than evidence against Mosaic authorship of the great bulk of the material. Others note that an inspired writer recording an objective assessment of his own character under divine guidance is not inherently the same act as boastful self-praise (compare Paul's own frank, third-person-adjacent self-assessments of his apostolic credentials elsewhere, done for a stated didactic purpose rather than personal vanity, e.g. 2 Corinthians 11:5, 12:11); the immediate narrative context (Miriam and Aaron's jealous challenge to Moses' unique authority) explains why the point needed stating at all -- establishing precisely why Moses, despite provocation, does not retaliate or defend himself, which the following verses show God himself stepping in to do on Moses' behalf. This document does not attempt to resolve the broader question of the Pentateuch's composition and editorial history in general, which involves a wide range of additional evidence beyond this single verse.
