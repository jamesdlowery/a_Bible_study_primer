# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 3 John

This document catalogs 3 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 3 John, the
shortest book in the entire New Testament (14 verses), either entirely internal to the letter
or in comparison with the companion 1 John and 2 John documents, and Colossians. For each
item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised and gives the standard
scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entry:** Entry 1 addresses Diotrephes, a named
individual within the New Testament itself who openly resisted apostolic authority -- a rare
and historically valuable glimpse into genuine conflict and power struggles within the
earliest churches, including a notable linguistic connection between the Greek term for his
"love of preeminence" and the identical root word used only once elsewhere in the New
Testament, describing Christ's own rightful preeminence in Colossians 1:18.

**Verification status:** Entry 1 was checked in detail against multiple sources confirming
both the historical and linguistic details involved. Entries 2 and 3 are addressed by
cross-reference to treatments already established in the companion 1 John and 2 John
documents.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary text and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Diotrephes -- a named individual within the New Testament itself who rejected apostolic authority

**References:** 3 John 1:9-10

**The claim:** 3 John 1:9-10 describes a specific, named individual, Diotrephes, 'who likes to put himself first, [and] does not acknowledge our authority' -- refusing to welcome traveling teachers John had sent, spreading 'wicked nonsense' against John, and even expelling from the church anyone who did welcome them. This is a rare and striking case within the New Testament itself of a named church leader openly and successfully resisting apostolic authority, raising a question about how this episode relates to the broader New Testament portrait of apostolic authority as normally decisive and widely respected within the earliest churches.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 3 John 1:9-10 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a genuine, historically significant episode of resistance to apostolic authority, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a genuinely significant and historically informative episode this project addresses directly rather than downplaying. Diotrephes' specific offense, as the text itself describes it, was not a doctrinal dispute over the content of the gospel (unlike the false teachers addressed in 1 and 2 John, who denied Christ's coming in the flesh), but a matter of personal ambition and a refusal of proper deference to apostolic authority -- the Greek term John uses for Diotrephes 'loving to be first' (philoproteuon) is built from the same root word used only one other place in the New Testament, Colossians 1:18, to describe Christ's own rightful preeminence 'in everything,' a connection several commentators read as a deliberate, pointed irony: Diotrephes was, in effect, arrogating to himself the very kind of first-place status that properly belongs to Christ alone. This episode is generally read as valuable, candid historical evidence that apostolic authority, even within the lifetime of an apostle personally known to and trusted by the wider church, was not automatically or universally accepted by every individual or local congregation without resistance -- a realistic acknowledgment of actual conflict and power struggles within the earliest church's own leadership structures, rather than the New Testament's own idealized or triumphalist self-presentation glossing over such tensions. The letter's own resolution (John's stated intention to personally address the matter upon his next visit, 1:10, and his closing commendation of a contrasting, well-regarded figure, Demetrius, 1:12) is generally read as modeling a patient, direct, relationally-grounded response to this kind of internal church conflict rather than an immediate, forceful assertion of raw authority.

## Theological Tensions

### 2. 'Whoever does evil has not seen God' -- addressed alongside the companion 1 John document's treatment of sin

**References:** 3 John 1:11; 1 John 1:8, 10; 1 John 3:6, 9

**The claim:** 3 John 1:11 states, 'Beloved, do not imitate evil but imitate good. Whoever does good is from God; whoever does evil has not seen God' -- an absolute-sounding statement that, read strictly, could seem to say that anyone who has ever committed any evil act has therefore never truly known God, connecting to the closely related internal tension addressed at length in the companion 1 John document regarding that letter's own statements about sin and sinlessness.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 3 John 1:11 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion 1 John document for the fuller discussion of this same underlying question regarding sin, sinlessness, and settled moral character.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 1 John document, which addresses the standard resolution to comparable statements in that letter (1 John 3:6, 9's claim that one born of God 'cannot sin'): most commentators read this verse the same way, as addressing a settled, defining pattern or direction of moral character and conduct ('imitating' evil or good as an ongoing practice and orientation of life) rather than any single, isolated act -- consistent with 1 John's own explicit acknowledgment elsewhere (1:8, 10, addressed in its own companion document) that even genuine believers continue to commit individual acts of sin. On this reading, 'whoever does evil has not seen God' describes someone whose fundamental life-pattern is characterized by evil as its consistent direction and practice, not a claim that a single lapse or sin disqualifies someone who is otherwise walking in the light from having genuinely known God.

## Literary Composition

### 3. A closing formula nearly identical to 2 John's -- evidence for common authorship of both short letters

**References:** 3 John 1:13-14; 2 John 1:12

**The claim:** 3 John closes with language strikingly similar to 2 John's own closing: 'I had much to write to you, but I would rather not write with pen and ink. I hope to see you soon, and we will talk face to face' (1:13-14) -- closely paralleling 2 John 1:12's 'I have much to write to you, but I would rather not use paper and ink. Instead, I hope to come to you and talk face to face.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 3 John 1:13-14 and 2 John 1:12 plainly and consistently as given, preserving the close verbal parallel between the two closings; this entry addresses a matter of literary and compositional evidence, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable point of stylistic and structural evidence supporting common authorship of 2 and 3 John specifically -- both letters share not only this nearly identical closing formula, but also the same distinctive self-identification ('the elder,' addressed at length in the companion 2 John document regarding the broader 'two Johns' authorship question), comparable length, and similar overall themes (hospitality toward traveling teachers, and concern for truth and love within the Christian community). While the broader question of whether this 'elder' was the apostle John himself or a distinct historical figure remains genuinely debated (addressed in the companion 2 John document), the close, specific verbal and structural similarity between these two particular letters' closings is widely read as strong internal evidence that both were written by the same individual, whoever he was, working from a similar personal epistolary style and set of concerns.
