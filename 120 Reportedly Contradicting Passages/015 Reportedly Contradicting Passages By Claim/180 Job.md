# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Job

This document catalogs 15 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual and
theological questions involving the book of Job, either entirely internal to the book or in
comparison with 1 Peter, Revelation, Numbers, and James. For each item, this document states
the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Three entries here (3, 5, and 9) are presented without a single,
confident resolution -- whether Elihu's speeches and the "hymn to wisdom" in chapter 28 are
original to the book or later additions, and whether Job's resurrection hope in chapter 19 is
compatible with his apparent denial of an afterlife elsewhere, are all genuinely live
questions among serious scholars. Entry 11 documents one of the clearest cases in this entire
project of a single Hebrew textual variant producing translations with literally opposite
meanings.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine uncertainty remains
among scholars, an honest account of what is and is not established. Readers are encouraged
to consult the primary texts directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. 'Blameless and upright' -- yet Job repents in dust and ashes

**References:** Job 1:1, 8; Job 2:3; Job 42:6

**The claim:** Job is introduced, and twice affirmed by God himself, as 'blameless and upright, one who fears God and turns away from evil' (1:1, 8; 2:3) -- among the strongest character endorsements given to any figure in the Old Testament. Yet at the book's climax, after God's speeches from the whirlwind, Job says, 'I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes' (42:6), language that reads as a confession of sin or wrongdoing, seemingly in tension with the earlier verdict of blamelessness.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the prologue's commendation and Job's closing words plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing general moral blamelessness from a narrower repentance over how Job spoke during the dialogue, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish two different senses in play: 'blameless' (tam) in the prologue describes Job's general moral integrity and genuine piety relative to other people -- he was not a hypocrite, and his suffering was not a specific punishment for specific sin, a point God's own repeated affirmation and the entire structure of the book's opening wager establish firmly. Job's closing words in 42:6 are generally read not as a retraction of that blamelessness, but as repentance for a different and narrower fault that emerged over the course of the dialogue itself: Job's own increasingly bold accusations against God's justice and his demand to cross-examine the Almighty as though Job himself were qualified to judge divine governance (a charge God's whirlwind speeches directly and extensively address, e.g., 38:2, 40:2, 8) -- repentance, that is, for how Job spoke about God under the pressure of unexplained suffering, not a confession that his original, God-affirmed integrity was false all along.

### 2. 'The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away' -- yet Satan is the direct agent of Job's affliction

**References:** Job 1:21; Job 1:12; Job 2:6-7

**The claim:** After losing his children and possessions in a single day, Job says, 'the LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD' (1:21), attributing the calamity directly to God. Yet the narrative itself, in the immediately preceding verses, has shown Satan as the one who directly strikes Job's possessions, servants, and children (1:12, 'the LORD said to Satan, Behold, all that he has is in your hand') and later Job's own body (2:6-7, 'the LORD said to Satan... he is in your hand... So Satan went out... and struck Job').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1:21 and both permission-scenes (1:12, 2:6) plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing layered divine and creaturely causation, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction but as the same pattern of layered causation addressed elsewhere in this project regarding Satan's role in inciting David's census (2 Samuel 24:1/1 Chronicles 21:1): the book's own narrative structure explicitly distinguishes Satan as the proximate, active agent (permitted, and in both cases explicitly limited by God's own stated boundaries, 1:12, 2:6) from God as the ultimate sovereign who grants that permission and remains the final, morally responsible governor of everything that occurs within his creation. Job's own theology in 1:21 reflects a robust Hebrew doctrine of divine sovereignty operating even through, and setting the limits of, a hostile secondary agent's actions -- the book never presents Job as mistaken or uninformed in attributing the outcome ultimately to God, even though the reader has been shown, and Job has not, the specific mechanism (Satan's role) by which it came about.

### 3. 'The patience of Job' -- reconciling James's commendation with Job's own raw complaints

**References:** James 5:11; Job 3:1-26; Job 6:2-3; Job 10:1

**The claim:** James 5:11 famously commends 'the steadfastness of Job' (rendered 'patience' in some translations) as a model for believers facing trial. Yet Job's own speeches are frequently anything but patient in the popular sense: Job 3 opens with Job cursing the day of his birth in some of the darkest language in the Old Testament ('why did I not die at birth... let that day be darkness,' 3:11, 4), and elsewhere Job describes his own anguish as heavier than the sand of the sea (6:2-3) and states plainly, 'I loathe my life' (10:1) -- a portrait considerably more raw, despairing, and complaining than the popular image of uncomplaining patience the phrase 'the patience of Job' has come to represent in common usage.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders James 5:11 as 'the patience of Job,' the wording most responsible for the popular idiom and the sense of tension some readers feel with Job's own complaints.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that the Greek term James uses (hypomone) is better and more precisely translated 'steadfastness,' 'endurance,' or 'perseverance' than the older English 'patience,' which in modern usage has come to suggest calm, uncomplaining forbearance -- a connotation the Greek term does not require and Job's own conduct does not display. What James commends is Job's ultimate refusal to abandon his faith and integrity despite unrelenting pressure to curse God (his wife's explicit counsel, 2:9) or to falsely confess sins he had not committed (the friends' relentless pressure) -- Job endured and persisted in fundamental faithfulness to God across the entire ordeal, even while giving voice, honestly and at length, to his genuine anguish, confusion, and even anger along the way. On this reading, there is no real tension: James commends the outcome and underlying posture (continued faith and integrity through to the end, vindicated in the epilogue), not a claim that Job's emotional experience throughout was calm or uncomplaining -- the popular idiom 'the patience of Job' has simply drifted from the more accurate sense of the word James actually used.

### 4. Who brought the evil upon Job -- the LORD, or Satan?

**References:** Job 42:11

**The claim:** Job's family and friends comfort him, after his restoration, 'over all the evil that the LORD had brought upon him' (42:11) -- attributing the affliction directly to God, even though the narrative itself showed Satan as the direct, active agent striking Job's possessions, family, and body.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is the same layered-causation question already addressed in full elsewhere in this document (see the entry on Job 1:21's 'the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away'), simply restated by the surrounding community at the story's end rather than by Job himself at its beginning. The same resolution applies without modification: God remains the ultimate, sovereign permitting cause even where Satan is the proximate, active agent, and the book consistently allows both Job and, here, his community to speak of the affliction as coming from the LORD without this being corrected or treated as a misunderstanding anywhere in the text.

## Literary Composition

### 5. Elihu's speeches: a later addition to the book?

**References:** Job 32:1-37:24; Job 2:11; Job 42:7-9

**The claim:** A fourth speaker, Elihu, appears abruptly in chapter 32, delivering an extended series of speeches (chapters 32-37) that interrupt the flow between Job's final defense (chapters 29-31) and God's response from the whirlwind (chapter 38). Elihu is never mentioned in the book's prologue alongside Job's three named friends (2:11 names only Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar), and -- more strikingly -- he is entirely absent from the epilogue, where God specifically addresses and rebukes 'Eliphaz the Temanite' and instructs the three named friends to offer sacrifice and have Job pray for them (42:7-9), with no parallel mention of Elihu needing similar correction or inclusion, despite his lengthy speeches.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Elihu's speeches, the prologue's list of named friends, and the epilogue's rebuke exactly as given; the question addressed here concerns composition history, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and openly debated question among biblical scholars, including many who hold a high view of the book's inspiration and unity, and is presented here without a single forced resolution: a substantial body of scholarship (both critical and conservative) reads Elihu's speeches as a later addition to an originally shorter book, based on the observations above along with stylistic differences in the Hebrew of these chapters compared to the rest of the book; on this view, Elihu's material was inserted, whether by the original author at a later stage of composition or by a subsequent editor, to supply a fresh, younger, and in some ways more restrained voice bridging Job's defense and God's own response. Other scholars read Elihu's absence from the epilogue not as evidence of a separate compositional origin, but as reflecting the narrator's own assessment that Elihu, unlike the three friends, said nothing requiring correction or atonement (his speeches notably avoid the specific charge that Job's suffering must indicate hidden sin, instead emphasizing God's use of suffering for instruction and discipline) -- meaning his omission from the rebuke is not an oversight but a deliberate signal of relative approval, with the prologue's silence about him explained simply by his being a bystander who had not yet spoken at the point in the story chapter 2 describes. This project takes no position on which reading is correct.

### 6. Does God's whirlwind speech actually answer Job's questions?

**References:** Job 38:1-41:34; Job 3:1-26; Job 1:6-12; Job 2:1-6

**The claim:** Job spends the great majority of the book demanding to know why he suffers, insisting on the right to bring his specific case before God directly (13:3, 13:22-23, 23:1-7). When God finally responds from the whirlwind (chapters 38-41), the speech consists almost entirely of an extended series of rhetorical questions about creation, the natural world, and untamable creatures (Behemoth, Leviathan) -- and never once mentions the heavenly wager between God and Satan that the reader alone has been shown in chapters 1-2, nor does it directly explain why Job specifically was singled out to suffer.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both Job's demands and God's whirlwind speeches plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is a matter of literary and theological interpretation regarding what the book considers an adequate response, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as God evading or failing to answer Job's question, but as the book's own deliberate and considered theological point: God's response reframes the entire premise of Job's demand, implicitly teaching that a full, discursive explanation of the precise mechanism behind any individual's suffering (which the reader alone knows, and which Job is never told) is not what Job actually needs or what would actually satisfy him -- rather, an encounter with God's own overwhelming wisdom, power, and care even for creatures and processes entirely outside human utility or control is presented as the substantive, sufficient response, reorienting Job from demanding an explanation to trusting the character of the one who governs a world too vast and wise for Job (or any human) to fully audit or judge. Job's own response (40:3-5, 42:1-6) confirms this reading -- he does not complain that his question went unanswered, but instead expresses that the encounter itself has changed his posture from demanding an accounting to submission and repentance (addressed further in entry 1 above), suggesting the book itself regards this as a genuine, adequate resolution rather than a dodge.

### 7. Job 28's 'hymn to wisdom': a later insertion?

**References:** Job 28:1-28

**The claim:** Job 28, a self-contained poem reflecting on where wisdom can be found (concluding that 'the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom,' 28:28), sits between the end of the third and final cycle of dialogue with the friends (chapters 26-27) and Job's own extended closing defense (chapters 29-31). Its calm, reflective, and resolved tone -- reaching a settled theological conclusion -- differs noticeably from the surrounding chapters' raw, unresolved argument, and it is not clearly attributed to any specific speaker in the text as it stands, leading some scholars to treat it as a later, independently composed insertion rather than part of the original dialogue.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Job 28 exactly as given, without marking it as a separate insertion in the text; the question addressed here concerns composition history, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the Elihu speeches (entry 4 above), this is a genuine question of composition history on which scholars, including many with a high view of the book's inspiration, are divided rather than unanimous, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Those who read chapter 28 as an original part of the book generally understand it as the narrator's own editorial interlude -- a deliberate pause in the dialogue's escalating, unresolved human argument to offer the reader (though not necessarily the characters within the story) a preview of the book's ultimate resolution, anticipating and framing God's own whirlwind speeches later in the book. Those who read it as a later insertion generally do so on stylistic and structural grounds similar to those offered for Elihu's speeches, without this necessarily implying anything negative about the chapter's content or its canonical authority, only about the stage at which it became part of the book's final form.

## Theological Tensions

### 8. God commends Job's speech as 'right' and condemns the friends' -- despite Job's own provocative statements about divine justice

**References:** Job 42:7-8; Job 9:22-24; Job 10:2-3

**The claim:** In the epilogue, God tells Eliphaz, 'you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has' (42:7), a verdict repeated in 42:8 -- yet over the course of the dialogue, Job says things that read as sharply accusatory toward God's justice, including 'it is all one; therefore I say, he destroys both the blameless and the wicked' (9:22) and a direct accusation that God oppresses him without cause (10:2-3), while the friends, by contrast, mostly defend conventional, seemingly more reverent claims about God's justice and the reliability of retribution for sin. This raises the question of how Job's apparently harsher statements about God could be commended as 'right' over the friends' more traditionally pious-sounding speeches.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render God's commendation, Job's provocative statements, and the friends' central thesis plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing overall theological correctness from individual statements made under duress, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators resolve this by distinguishing the specific content being evaluated from the overall tone or individual provocative statements within a much longer speech: God's commendation is generally read as addressing the fundamental theological framework each party operated from, not a verse-by-verse endorsement of every individual statement Job made in his anguish. The friends' central, repeated thesis -- that suffering reliably and directly indicates specific hidden sin, and that Job's calamity therefore proves his secret guilt -- is a false and, per the book's own prologue, factually incorrect account of why Job suffered, applied by the friends with increasing cruelty and certainty despite having no actual evidence; Job, whatever his individual overstatements in anguish, correctly and persistently maintained that his suffering was not a direct, legible punishment for specific sin, refusing the friends' false diagnosis even under great pressure to accept it and thereby find easy resolution. On this reading, God vindicates Job's fundamental stance (that not all suffering is direct retribution, and that Job's own case specifically was not) over the friends' fundamentally mistaken theological system, without this requiring approval of every rhetorical excess Job's own grief and confusion produced along the way -- some of which, as addressed in entry 1 above, appears to be precisely what Job repents of in 42:6.

### 9. 'I know that my Redeemer lives' -- resurrection hope, or denial of any afterlife?

**References:** Job 19:25-27; Job 7:9-10; Job 14:7-12

**The claim:** Job's famous declaration, 'I know that my Redeemer lives, and at the last he will stand upon the earth... and after my skin has been thus destroyed, yet in my flesh I shall see God' (19:25-26), is widely read as an expression of hope in bodily resurrection or at least a post-mortem vindication before God. Yet elsewhere in his own speeches, Job appears to deny any such hope: 'he who goes down to Sheol does not come up; he returns no more to his house' (7:9-10), and 14:7-12 explicitly contrasts a felled tree (which can sprout again from its stump) with a man, who 'lies down and rises not again... till the heavens are no more, he will not awake or be roused out of his sleep' -- a stark denial of any return from death.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 19:25-27, 7:9-10, and 14:7-12 plainly and consistently as given, reflecting the same underlying, genuinely difficult Hebrew text in each case; this is a live and long-running exegetical debate, not resolved by any translation's specific word choices.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most actively debated exegetical questions in the entire book, and this entry does not claim a single settled answer, since serious, careful interpreters land in several different places even after close study of the Hebrew, which is unusually difficult and disputed at exactly this passage. Some read 19:25-27 as genuine hope in bodily resurrection after death, understanding Job's despair in chapters 7 and 14 as an earlier, lower point in the dialogue's emotional and theological progression -- by chapter 19, on this view, Job has moved toward a dawning, if still uncertain, hope that transcends his earlier statements. This reading is consistent with the book's overall dramatic arc of Job's faith deepening even amid continued suffering, rather than requiring every statement across thirty-plus chapters of impassioned poetry to express a single, static, fully consistent doctrine at every point. Others read 19:25-27 not as bodily resurrection at all, but as Job's hope for vindication before he dies -- expecting a legal 'redeemer' (goel, the same term used for a kinsman-redeemer elsewhere in this project's treatment of Ruth) to appear and publicly clear his name even in his final, dying moments, or immediately upon death, without requiring a later bodily return to life. This reading would not conflict with 7:9-10 and 14:7-12 at all, since no return to embodied life on earth is in view. A minority reads even 19:25-27 with more skepticism about its content, given the genuine difficulty and disputed translation of the underlying Hebrew at several key points in these verses. This project does not adjudicate between these readings.

### 10. 'The Satan' in Job's heavenly court -- the same figure as the New Testament's cosmic adversary?

**References:** Job 1:6-12; Job 2:1-6; 1 Peter 5:8; Revelation 12:9-10

**The claim:** Job 1-2 depicts 'the satan' (Hebrew ha-satan, most naturally translated 'the accuser' or 'the adversary,' a title rather than necessarily a personal name in this context) as a member of the divine council who appears before God alongside 'the sons of God,' engages God directly in a kind of formal legal exchange, and requires explicit divine permission before acting, with clearly stated limits on what he may do (1:12, 2:6). This differs in tone from the New Testament's more starkly adversarial, already-defeated-but-still-dangerous cosmic enemy imagery -- 'your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion' (1 Peter 5:8), 'the accuser of our brothers... has been thrown down' (Revelation 12:10) -- raising a question about whether this represents the same figure understood consistently, or a developing biblical concept.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Job 1-2's portrayal of 'the satan' and the New Testament's later portrayal of the devil plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing progressive revelation of a consistent, subordinate figure across the canon, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this as a case of progressive revelation rather than inconsistency: the Old Testament's relatively few and restrained references to a satan figure (also appearing in Zechariah 3:1-2 and 1 Chronicles 21:1, addressed elsewhere in this project) consistently present a being who operates only within boundaries God sets and permits, already establishing the core theological point (that this adversary is not remotely God's equal or independent rival, but a subordinate, permitted actor) that the New Testament's fuller, more developed portrait builds upon and intensifies rather than contradicts. The New Testament's more vivid, urgent warnings about the devil's activity are generally read as reflecting a later stage of revelation describing the same essentially subordinate figure's greater, more open hostility and activity in the period following Christ's incarnation, death, and resurrection -- events the New Testament itself describes as provoking increased demonic desperation (Revelation 12:12, 'he knows that his time is short') -- rather than describing an entirely different character than Job's more restrained, court-appearing adversary.

### 11. Can God be found through human reason and searching, or is he beyond such discovery?

**References:** Job 11:7; Romans 1:20; Acts 17:27

**The claim:** Zophar asks Job rhetorically, 'Canst thou by searching find out God? canst thou find out the Almighty unto perfection?' (11:7), implying the answer is no. Yet Romans 1:20 states that God's 'invisible things... are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made,' and Acts 17:27 describes God as having ordered the nations 'that they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Zophar's rhetorical question and Paul's statements address two different claims that are not in tension once distinguished: Job 11:7 denies that human searching can grasp God's nature exhaustively or 'unto perfection' -- fully comprehending the infinite, unsearchable depths of who God is, a limitation Scripture affirms elsewhere as well (Isaiah 55:8-9; Romans 11:33-34's 'how unsearchable are his judgments'). Romans 1:20 and Acts 17:27, by contrast, affirm that real, genuine (if partial and incomplete) knowledge of God's existence and basic attributes -- his eternal power and divine nature -- is available to reasoning creatures through creation and general revelation, sufficient to leave people 'without excuse' rather than sufficient to exhaustively comprehend the Almighty. Partial, genuine knowledge through searching and total, perfect comprehension through searching are different claims; Job 11:7 denies only the latter.

### 12. Do the wicked prosper and live long lives, or are their days cut short?

**References:** Job 12:6; Job 21:7-13; Psalm 55:23; Proverbs 10:27

**The claim:** Job repeatedly observes, against his friends' confident retribution theology, that 'the tabernacles of robbers prosper, and they that provoke God are secure' (12:6), and asks pointedly, 'Wherefore do the wicked live, become old, yea, are mighty in power?' (21:7), going on to describe them living out full, comfortable lives surrounded by descendants and prosperity (21:8-13). Other wisdom texts instead affirm that 'bloody and deceitful men shall not live out half their days' (Psalm 55:23) and that 'the fear of the LORD prolongeth days: but the years of the wicked shall be shortened' (Proverbs 10:27).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This tension is a central, deliberate theme of the book of Job itself, not an oversight -- Job raises exactly this observation as part of his sustained argument against the friends' claim that suffering and prosperity map directly and reliably onto sin and righteousness in this present life, a theological system the book's own conclusion (see this document's entry on God commending Job's speech as 'right') indicates was fundamentally mistaken as a rigid, universal rule. The Proverbs and Psalms statements are best read as proverbial wisdom describing a real and general statistical tendency (recklessness, violence, and deceit do tend, as a general pattern, to shorten life and invite downfall) rather than an ironclad, exceptionless law -- proverbial wisdom literature throughout Scripture is widely understood to state general patterns and probabilities rather than universal guarantees admitting no exceptions, exactly the kind of general truth Job's own extended, deliberately provocative counter-examples in this book are examining and qualifying rather than flatly denying. Ecclesiastes 7:15 and 8:14 make substantially the same observation as Job -- that the righteous sometimes perish in their righteousness and the wicked sometimes prolong their life in their wickedness -- confirming this tension between general proverbial pattern and troubling individual exception was a recognized and openly wrestled-with feature of Israel's own wisdom tradition, not a contradiction requiring resolution so much as a real feature of life in a fallen world that biblical wisdom literature candidly explores from more than one angle.

## Numbers and Text

### 13. Restored possessions doubled, restored children not doubled

**References:** Job 1:2-3; Job 42:12-13

**The claim:** Job's original possessions are given in 1:2-3 (7,000 sheep, 3,000 camels, 500 yoke of oxen, 500 donkeys, and 7 sons and 3 daughters). Following his restoration, 42:12 states the LORD gave Job exactly double each livestock figure (14,000 sheep, 6,000 camels, 1,000 oxen, 1,000 donkeys) -- but 42:13 gives him again only 7 sons and 3 daughters, not the 14 sons and 6 daughters a strict doubling pattern would require.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the original and restored figures for livestock and for children exactly as given, without adjusting either; the harmonization is theological/interpretive, not a matter of translation.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not read the children as an exception to a strict doubling rule, but as reflecting a different theology of loss specifically regarding human life versus property: livestock, once lost, are simply gone and can only be restored through literal replacement and increase, which the text describes as precisely doubled; children, by contrast, are generally understood by commentators (working from the broader biblical hope of an afterlife and resurrection, addressed in entry 8 above) as not having ceased to exist in the same final sense -- Job's first ten children remain, in this reading, truly his, so that the second ten (born after the restoration) genuinely do give him a doubled total of twenty children in the fullest reckoning, even though only ten are alive with him at the story's end. This reading treats the apparent 'non-doubling' as the text's own subtle theological statement about the different nature of human loss compared to material loss, rather than as an inconsistency in an otherwise strict numerical pattern.

### 14. 'Though he slay me, I will hope in him' -- or 'I have no hope'? A famous textual crux with genuinely opposite readings

**References:** Job 13:15

**The claim:** Job 13:15 contains one of the most famous verses in the book, traditionally rendered (following the KJV) 'Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him' -- an oft-quoted expression of unshakeable faith. However, the Hebrew text itself contains a well-known Kethiv/Qere textual variant (a case where the text as written, kethiv, differs from a marginal note indicating how it was traditionally read aloud, qere): the written consonants spell lo meaning 'not,' which would render the verse 'Behold, he will slay me; I have no hope' -- essentially the opposite meaning from the traditional, more famous rendering -- while the marginal qere note reads a nearly identical-sounding word meaning 'to him,' supporting the traditional 'I will hope in him.' English translations genuinely and substantively differ on which reading to follow, producing two versions of this verse with opposite meanings.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'Even though He kills me, I will hope in Him. Nevertheless, I will argue my ways to His face,' following the qere (positive) reading straightforwardly, without a bracketed alternative for the kethiv reading (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'Even if he kills me, I will hope in him,' following the qere reading (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'Though he slay me, I will hope in him,' following the qere reading (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him,' following the qere (positive) reading.
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'Though He slay me, yet will I trust Him,' also following the qere reading.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'Though He slay me, I will hope in Him,' following the qere reading (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'Though He slay me, I will hope in Him,' following the qere reading, tracking NASB (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'Though he slay me, yet will I hope in him,' following the qere reading (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'God might kill me, but I have no other hope,' following the kethiv (negative) reading -- a genuine departure from the majority of translations in this survey (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'Behold, he will slay me; I have no hope; yet I will defend my ways to his face,' following the kethiv (negative) reading (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording, making it the one translation among the ten surveyed here that most starkly departs from the traditional, more famous rendering.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction between two biblical texts but a single, well-documented textual variant within the manuscript tradition of one verse, openly acknowledged and discussed by scholars across the theological spectrum -- unlike most entries in this project, where a majority reading can be identified with some confidence, translators and commentators remain genuinely split on which reading is more likely original. Those favoring the negative ('I have no hope') reading note it fits more naturally with the bitter, confrontational tone of Job's surrounding speech in chapter 13 (where Job repeatedly expresses readiness to argue his case even at the risk of death, rather than expressing calm trust) and follows the text-critical principle that the more difficult, less theologically comfortable reading is often more likely to be original (since scribes would be more likely to soften a shocking statement than to introduce one). Those favoring the positive ('I will hope in him') reading note it has the support of the ancient reading tradition (the qere) transmitted alongside the Masoretic text for this exact purpose, and fits a coherent theological arc in which Job's faith, however severely tested, does not ultimately fail even at its lowest points.

## Law and Practice

### 15. Job's daughters inherit alongside their brothers -- before the Law's default rule required sons

**References:** Job 42:13-15; Numbers 27:1-11

**The claim:** Job 42:15 states that Job's father 'gave [his daughters] an inheritance among their brothers' -- his three daughters receiving a share of the family inheritance alongside their seven brothers. Numbers 27:1-11, by contrast, records a specific legal ruling that daughters could inherit only in the absence of any surviving sons, treating female inheritance as an exception requiring special provision rather than the ordinary practice Job's family appears to follow as a matter of course.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Job 42:15's account of the daughters' inheritance and Numbers 27's ruling exactly as given; the harmonization concerns the historical setting and scope of each provision, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most conservative scholars place Job's own lifetime in a patriarchal-era setting, likely prior to or roughly contemporary with the very earliest parts of Israel's history and well before the Mosaic legislation at Sinai -- consistent with several other features of the book (its portrayal of Job himself, rather than a dedicated priesthood, offering sacrifices for his family, 1:5, a pattern addressed similarly elsewhere in this project regarding pre-Sinai patriarchal religious practice). On this reading, Job's generous inclusion of his daughters in the family inheritance reflects the customary practice of his own particular family and era, which the Mosaic Law given centuries later did not need to prohibit and did not contradict -- Numbers 27 establishes a default rule protecting daughters' inheritance specifically in the absence of sons, without thereby forbidding a father from including daughters alongside sons if he so chose, a practice the Law nowhere prohibits even after Sinai.
