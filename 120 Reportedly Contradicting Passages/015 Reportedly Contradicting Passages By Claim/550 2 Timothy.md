# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 Timothy

This document catalogs 5 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 2 Timothy,
traditionally regarded as Paul's final letter, either entirely internal to the letter or in
comparison with the companion 1 Timothy and Joel documents, 2 Peter, and Hebrews. For each
item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV**
specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording
materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the
standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entry:** Entry 1 addresses 2 Timothy 3:16, the
single most frequently cited biblical text for the doctrine of Scripture's own divine
inspiration -- foundational, in a sense, to how this entire project's source material has
itself been treated throughout this series -- including a genuine question about the verse's
own historical scope, given that the New Testament canon had not yet been completed at the
time of writing.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 4, and 3 are presented with genuine scholarly and theological
diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim -- including, for the new entry below, a direct check of all ten translations' specific
rendering of 2 Timothy 2:26's genuinely ambiguous pronoun. Where no such check turned up a
meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten translations render the passage similarly.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly or
theological diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Interpretation

### 1. 'All Scripture is breathed out by God' -- the classic proof-text for biblical inspiration, and a genuine question about its own scope

**References:** 2 Timothy 3:14-17

**The claim:** 2 Timothy 3:16 states, 'All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness' -- the single most frequently cited biblical text for the doctrine of Scripture's own divine inspiration, foundational to how this entire project's source material has itself been treated throughout this series. A genuine question arises regarding this verse's own scope: since the New Testament canon had not yet been completed, and in some cases not yet even written, at the time Paul wrote this letter, did Paul's own statement about 'all Scripture' being God-breathed refer only to the already-existing Hebrew Scriptures (the Old Testament), or did it also anticipate or apply to the New Testament writings that would later be recognized as Scripture alongside them -- including, by strict extension, this very letter itself?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Timothy 3:16 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a matter of the verse's own precise historical scope and its subsequent theological application, not a contradiction, and is not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated question of scope, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. Most commentators agree Paul's own immediate, primary referent was the Hebrew Scriptures -- the Law, Prophets, and Writings that Timothy had known 'from childhood' (3:15), which constituted the settled, closed canon of Scripture available to both Paul and Timothy at the time of writing. The genuinely debated question concerns whether Paul's statement, or the New Testament more broadly, extends this same category and authority to the apostolic writings that would become the New Testament. Those arguing for a broader application note that the New Testament elsewhere applies the term 'Scripture' explicitly to New Testament-era writings, not exclusively the Old Testament -- 2 Peter 3:16 refers to Paul's own letters as being twisted 'as they do the other Scriptures,' explicitly placing Paul's letters within the same category, and 1 Timothy 5:18 quotes a saying of Jesus (recorded in Luke 10:7) alongside a citation from Deuteronomy, introducing both together as 'Scripture' -- suggesting the early church itself, within the New Testament period, was already extending the category of God-breathed Scripture to include apostolic and dominical teaching alongside the Hebrew Bible, even before the full New Testament canon was formally recognized. This project does not adjudicate the precise, technical scope of Paul's own intended meaning in this specific verse, noting only that the broader New Testament and early church's own developing use of the term 'Scripture' provides some basis for extending the same category, and the same claim to divine inspiration, to the New Testament writings collectively, even though this specific verse's own most immediate historical referent was almost certainly the Old Testament alone.

### 2. 'The last days' bringing 'times of difficulty' -- an imminent prediction, or a description of the church age generally?

**References:** 2 Timothy 3:1-9

**The claim:** 2 Timothy 3:1-5 warns that 'in the last days there will come times of difficulty. For people will be lovers of self, lovers of money, proud, arrogant, abusive... having the appearance of godliness, but denying its power' -- a description frequently applied by contemporary readers to describe specifically their own current era's moral and social conditions, raising a question of whether Paul intends a specific, imminent future period, or a general, ongoing description characterizing the entire period between Christ's first and second coming.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Timothy 3:1-9 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing 'the last days' as describing the whole period between Christ's two comings, consistent with its use elsewhere in the New Testament, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a matter of how 'the last days' language functions throughout the New Testament more broadly -- most commentators read this phrase, consistent with its use elsewhere in the New Testament (compare Acts 2:17's application of Joel's 'last days' prophecy, addressed in the companion Joel document, to the entire church age inaugurated at Pentecost, and Hebrews 1:2's description of the incarnation itself as occurring 'in these last days'), as describing the whole period from Christ's first coming to his return, rather than a specific, narrowly future window immediately preceding the second coming exclusively. On this reading, Paul's description of moral decay and false godliness is not a claim uniquely and specifically predictive of one particular future generation (including, necessarily, the reader's own present moment), but a general characterization of the kind of opposition and moral corruption the church should expect to encounter at any point throughout this entire 'last days' period between Christ's two comings -- consistent with the passage's own immediately following instruction to Timothy to remain grounded in the Scriptures and sound teaching specifically because such conditions would be a persistent, recurring feature of church life throughout this whole era (3:14-17), not a one-time crisis limited to a specific future period alone.

### 3. 'If we are faithless, he remains faithful' -- does this mean God saves the faithless regardless?

**References:** 2 Timothy 2:11-13

**The claim:** 2 Timothy 2:11-13 records what Paul introduces as a 'trustworthy saying': 'if we have died with him, we will also live with him; if we endure, we will also reign with him; if we deny him, he also will deny us; if we are faithless, he remains faithful -- for he cannot deny himself.' The final clause raises a genuine interpretive question: does 'he remains faithful' mean God remains faithful to save even those who prove faithless, in apparent tension with the immediately preceding clause's warning that Christ 'will deny' those who deny him, or does it mean something else regarding God's own unchanging character?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Timothy 2:11-13 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing God's own unchanging faithfulness to his word (including both promises and warnings) from a promise of unconditional salvation regardless of one's response, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated question connecting to the broader theological question of whether genuine believers can permanently and finally fall away from salvation, addressed at several points in ongoing denominational disagreement between different Christian traditions (broadly, Reformed traditions holding to the perseverance of true believers, and other traditions holding that genuine apostasy is possible). Most commentators, across this broader disagreement, read 'he remains faithful' in this specific verse as referring to God's own unchanging, consistent character and faithfulness to his own word and warnings -- including the very warning just stated in the preceding clause ('if we deny him, he also will deny us') -- rather than as a promise that God will faithfully save those who deny him regardless of their unfaithfulness. On this reading, 'he cannot deny himself' functions as the key interpretive clue: God's faithfulness to his own consistent character and word means he will faithfully carry out exactly what he has said, including both the promise of reigning with him for those who endure, and the warning of being denied by him for those who deny him -- 'faithless' human unfaithfulness does not cause God's own truthful, consistent character to waver in either direction, rather than the verse promising unconditional salvation regardless of a person's ultimate response. Some interpreters within traditions affirming the perseverance of genuine believers hold a related but distinct reading -- that 'if we are faithless' describes momentary lapses of faith characteristic of genuine believers (rather than final, decisive apostasy or outright denial, addressed separately in the previous clause), with God's faithfulness in this specific instance referring to his continued faithfulness to preserve genuine believers through such momentary failures. This project does not adjudicate the underlying question of whether genuine, final apostasy is possible for a true believer, consistent with its treatment of comparable questions elsewhere in this series.

## Literary Composition

### 4. The authorship question -- addressed at length in the companion 1 Timothy document, with a further nuance specific to this letter

**References:** 2 Timothy 1:1

**The claim:** 2 Timothy identifies its own author as Paul (1:1), connecting to the broader authorship debate regarding the Pastoral Epistles (1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, and Titus) addressed at length in the companion 1 Timothy document.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Timothy as a continuous text attributed to Paul; see the companion 1 Timothy document for the fuller discussion of the Pastoral Epistles' authorship question.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion 1 Timothy document, which addresses the general critical arguments against, and conservative arguments for, common Pauline authorship of all three Pastoral Epistles. A further nuance specific to 2 Timothy is worth noting here: some scholars who question the authenticity of 1 Timothy and Titus are nonetheless more open to regarding 2 Timothy specifically as more plausibly authentic, or as containing a larger proportion of genuine Pauline material, given this letter's unusually personal, biographical character -- its specific, detailed personal references (to Timothy's mother Eunice and grandmother Lois, 1:5; to Paul's own abandonment by certain named companions and support from others, 4:9-16; and its overall tone of a dying man's final, intimate testimony) are sometimes read as more difficult to attribute to a later, pseudonymous author writing in Paul's name than the more generalized instructional material found in 1 Timothy and Titus. This project does not adjudicate the underlying authorship question for any of the three letters, noting only this additional, letter-specific nuance within the broader debate.

## Theological Tensions

### 5. Can the devil simply capture and control people at his own will, with no possibility of resistance or escape?

**References:** 2 Timothy 2:25-26; James 4:7; 1 Peter 5:8-9

**The claim:** 2 Timothy 2:26 describes certain opponents as 'taken captive by him at his will' (KJV) -- language some read as teaching that the devil can seize and control people according to his own unchecked pleasure. Elsewhere, Scripture assumes real human capacity to resist the devil successfully: 'Resist the devil, and he will flee from you' (James 4:7), and believers are told to resist him, 'stedfast in the faith' (1 Peter 5:8-9).

**How the ten translations render it:** The ten translations vary in a way directly relevant to this entry: most render the verse's final phrase as some form of 'held captive by him to do his will' (leaving the pronoun's referent -- the devil or God -- as ambiguous in English as it is in the underlying Greek, which itself uses two different pronouns at this point, a detail noted by several scholarly commentaries). None of the ten resolves the ambiguity definitively in the translation itself; the question is a matter of Greek grammatical interpretation rather than one this document's ten translations settle differently from one another.

**Reconciliation:** This particular verse involves a genuinely debated point of Greek grammar noted by several commentators: the underlying Greek text uses two different pronouns in this sentence, and scholars are divided on whether the final 'his will' refers back to the devil (meaning the opponents are held captive to carry out the devil's purposes) or to God (meaning they are held captive by the devil generally, but the verse's actual point is that they may be rescued in order to do God's will instead) -- some older commentators and translators have even proposed the intervening pronoun refers to 'the Lord's servant' mentioned two verses earlier, who does the capturing (rescuing) rather than the devil. Whichever reading is correct, the verse's own immediate context resolves the broader concern regardless: 2 Timothy 2:25 explicitly holds out the real hope that 'God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth,' and that they may 'recover themselves out of the snare of the devil' -- the passage's entire point is that escape and repentance are genuinely possible, not that the devil holds an inescapable, deterministic grip on anyone regardless of God's grace or their own response. Any real bondage to the devil this verse describes is therefore presented as neither absolute nor beyond hope of rescue, fully consistent with James 4:7 and 1 Peter 5:8-9's assumption that successful resistance to the devil, through God's grace, remains genuinely possible.
