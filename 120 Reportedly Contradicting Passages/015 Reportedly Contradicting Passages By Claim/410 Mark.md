# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Mark

This document catalogs 17 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the Gospel of
Mark, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with Matthew, Luke, John, Acts, 1
Samuel, Malachi, and Isaiah. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically
raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the
translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in
English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this Gospel's most significant entries:** Entries 1 and 2 address the single most
significant textual-critical question in the entire New Testament -- the ending of Mark's
Gospel -- and a case where that genuine textual uncertainty carries direct, documented
real-world safety consequences (snake-handling religious practice). Both are presented with
full candor about the genuine, ongoing scholarly division rather than a single confident
resolution.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 4, and 10 are presented with genuine scholarly diversity of
opinion rather than a single forced resolution. Entry 7 is presented as a minor but genuine
point of specific verbal difference between the Synoptic accounts, addressed candidly rather
than forced into an artificially seamless harmony.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Textual History

### 1. The ending of Mark -- the single most significant textual-critical question in the New Testament

**References:** Mark 16:1-20

**The claim:** The two oldest surviving complete Greek manuscripts of Mark's Gospel, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus (both fourth century), end the Gospel abruptly at 16:8 -- 'and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid' -- with no resurrection appearance of Jesus at all. The great majority of later Greek manuscripts (roughly 99%) instead include an extended 'longer ending' (16:9-20), containing resurrection appearances, the Great Commission, and the well-known 'signs' passage (addressed in entry 2 below). A small number of other manuscripts contain a different, considerably shorter alternate ending. This is widely regarded as the single most significant open textual-critical question in the entire New Testament.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, LSB:** Every modern translation surveyed includes some form of explanatory note regarding the manuscript evidence for Mark's ending, though the specific presentation (in-line without comment, bracketed, footnoted, or set off with introductory explanation) varies; this reflects the genuinely significant and actively studied nature of this specific textual question, not a matter any single translation resolves definitively.
- **CSB:** CSB includes 16:9-20 with a similar explanatory footnote.
- **ESV:** ESV includes 16:9-20 but sets it off with a note explaining 'some of the earliest manuscripts do not include 16:9-20.'
- **KJV:** KJV includes Mark 16:9-20 within the main numbered text without any bracketing or footnote regarding the manuscript question, reflecting the Textus Receptus tradition underlying this translation.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also includes 16:9-20 in the main text, with a footnote noting that Sinaiticus and Vaticanus lack these verses.
- **NASB:** NASB includes 16:9-20 in double brackets, with an extended footnote explaining the manuscript evidence on both sides.
- **NIV:** NIV includes 16:9-20 with a note stating 'the earliest manuscripts and some other ancient witnesses do not have verses 9-20.'
- **NLT:** NLT includes 16:9-20 with a footnote on the earliest manuscripts' omission.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE's parent translation, the original 1952 RSV, was notable for initially relegating 16:9-20 to a footnote below the main text rather than including it in the numbered verses; later editions, including RSV2CE, restore the longer ending to the main text with an explanatory note.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and actively debated question among serious textual scholars, and this entry presents the real division rather than asserting a single confident answer. The majority of contemporary textual critics, across a wide range of theological commitments, conclude the longer ending (16:9-20) was not part of Mark's original composition. Their reasons include its absence from the two oldest complete Greek manuscripts and several other early witnesses, including the earliest Syriac and some Old Latin and Armenian manuscripts; its absence from citation or discussion by early church fathers including Origen and Cyprian, who otherwise comment extensively on Mark; explicit statements by Eusebius and later by Jerome that 'nearly all' the Greek manuscript copies available to them lacked these verses; a noticeable shift in vocabulary and style from the rest of Mark's Gospel; and, in Codex Vaticanus specifically, a uniquely placed blank column immediately following 16:8 -- the only such blank space left anywhere in that manuscript's entire New Testament. Some scholars read this blank space as evidence the scribe was aware of a longer ending circulating elsewhere but did not have it in his own source text. A substantial minority of scholars and textual critics defend the longer ending's authenticity instead. They point to its presence in the overwhelming majority, approximately 1,600, of surviving Greek manuscripts; citation by the church father Irenaeus already in the second century, earlier than either Vaticanus or Sinaiticus were copied; its presence across a wide range of ancient translations (Old Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Gothic); and specific manuscript features some read differently than the majority, arguing Vaticanus's blank column reflects a scribe's deliberate 'memorial space' for a known but absent longer text, rather than ignorance of it. This project does not adjudicate this specific, ongoing scholarly debate. It represents one of the clearest cases in the entire Bible where a textual variant is substantial in length and content, rather than a single word or clause, and remains genuinely unresolved among serious scholars committed to careful textual analysis.

## Ethical Tensions

### 2. 'They will pick up serpents... and if they drink any deadly poison, it will not hurt them' -- a disputed text with documented real-world consequences

**References:** Mark 16:17-18

**The claim:** Within the longer ending of Mark addressed in entry 1 above, verses 17-18 promise that specific miraculous 'signs' will accompany believers, including that 'they will pick up serpents with their hands, and if they drink any deadly thing, it will not hurt them.' This specific passage has served as the primary scriptural basis for snake-handling religious practices in some American Pentecostal and Holiness churches (predominantly in Appalachia), a practice that has resulted in a number of documented deaths over the past century, including that of at least one prominent snake-handling pastor.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Mark 16:17-18 (where included, per entry 1's discussion) plainly and consistently as given; the ethical caution addressed here concerns interpretation and application, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry is included specifically because it represents a case where the genuine textual-critical uncertainty addressed in entry 1 above carries direct, serious, real-world practical and safety consequences, rather than remaining a purely academic question. Most commentators across the theological spectrum, including many who are open to or defend the longer ending's authenticity on other grounds, caution strongly against reading this specific passage as prescribing or encouraging deliberate, intentional snake-handling or poison-drinking as a test or demonstration of faith. Two separate reasons are given, regardless of one's view on entry 1's textual question. First, given the substantial textual uncertainty surrounding this entire section, building an intentionally dangerous religious practice on a passage a large proportion of serious textual scholars do not believe was part of Mark's original Gospel is generally regarded as poor practice on textual-critical grounds alone. Second, even among those who accept the passage's authenticity, most commentators read it descriptively -- as anticipating specific, unplanned instances of divine protection the apostolic generation would experience in the course of ordinary ministry, a documented pattern also described in Acts 28:3-6, where Paul is bitten by a viper without effect while gathering firewood, an unplanned and unsought encounter. It is not read prescriptively, as an invitation or endorsement to deliberately seek out venomous animals or poison as a test of faith -- a reading most conservative commentators explicitly reject as presuming upon God's protection rather than genuinely trusting it.

## Theological Interpretation

### 3. The 'Messianic Secret' -- Jesus's repeated commands to silence, alongside open, public ministry

**References:** Mark 1:34, 44; Mark 3:12; Mark 5:19, 43; Mark 7:36; Mark 8:30; Mark 9:9

**The claim:** Mark's Gospel repeatedly depicts Jesus commanding demons, healed individuals, and even his own disciples to keep silent about his identity or his miracles (1:34, 44; 3:12; 5:43; 7:36; 8:30; 9:9) -- a recurring pattern scholars call the 'Messianic Secret.' Yet elsewhere in the same Gospel, Jesus performs miracles openly before large crowds, and at least one formerly demon-possessed man is explicitly told to publicize what happened to him rather than remain silent (5:19-20, the Gerasene demoniac instructed to 'go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you'), raising a question of whether Mark's Gospel is internally consistent on this point.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of Mark's secrecy commands and the Gerasene demoniac's contrasting instruction plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing a deliberate, contextually-varying narrative pattern, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a well-recognized and deliberately structured literary and theological pattern within Mark's own Gospel, the subject of extensive scholarly study since the early twentieth century (most influentially, William Wrede's identification of the 'Messianic Secret' motif). Most commentators read the secrecy commands as concentrated specifically around contexts where premature or politically dangerous public recognition of Jesus as 'Messiah' or 'Son of God' -- titles carrying strong nationalist and revolutionary connotations in first-century Judea -- could provoke a premature, misunderstanding-driven confrontation with Roman or Jewish authorities before Jesus' own ministry, and particularly his suffering and crucifixion, had unfolded according to his own intended timeline (this reading is supported by the explicit connection Mark draws between Peter's confession, the first silencing command related to Jesus' messianic identity specifically, and Jesus' first prediction of his own suffering and death, 8:29-31). The Gerasene demoniac's case, by contrast, occurs in Gentile territory (the Decapolis) far from Jerusalem's immediate religious and political tensions, where the same danger of premature, nationalistically-charged messianic recognition was not present, allowing for open, encouraged testimony without the same risk. On this reading, the pattern is neither inconsistent nor accidental, but reflects Mark's own deliberate narrative strategy regarding when and where public proclamation of Jesus' full identity was, and was not, strategically appropriate given his unfolding mission.

## Numbers and Text

### 4. 'In the days of Abiathar the high priest' -- naming a different priest than 1 Samuel's own account

**References:** Mark 2:25-26; 1 Samuel 21:1-6

**The claim:** Mark 2:26 has Jesus refer to David eating the consecrated showbread 'in the days of Abiathar the high priest.' 1 Samuel 21:1-6, however, the passage Jesus is clearly referencing, names the priest who gave David the bread as Ahimelech, Abiathar's own father -- Abiathar himself becomes high priest only later, after Ahimelech's death at Saul's hands (1 Samuel 22:20).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'in the days of Abiathar the high priest' (or a close equivalent) at Mark 2:26, following the Greek text as received; the resolution of this question depends on the specific sense of the underlying Greek preposition and is a matter of grammatical and historical interpretation, not a matter most translations resolve differently from one another in their main text.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and openly debated textual and grammatical question, and this entry presents the range of proposed solutions rather than a single certain answer. Some commentators, including the influential textual critic Bart Ehrman in his popular work Misquoting Jesus, read this as a straightforward factual error on the part of either Jesus, Mark, or an early scribal transmission of Mark's text. Other scholars propose a grammatical solution based on the precise Greek preposition Mark uses. Epi with the genitive is well-attested in a temporal sense -- 'in the time of' or 'during the reign of,' as at Acts 11:28's 'in the days of Claudius' (epi Klaudiou) -- but can also, less commonly, introduce a scriptural passage by the name of its central figure, as at Mark 12:26's 'in the passage about the bush.' On this reading, defended by New Testament scholars including Craig Blomberg and David Garland, Jesus may be referring not to the specific chronological moment of Ahimelech's own priesthood, but to the broader scriptural section or passage traditionally associated with and titled by Abiathar's name. Abiathar, on this reading, was a more historically prominent and narratively significant figure overall than his father, given his much longer subsequent association with David through the rest of David's reign. This reflects a documented ancient Jewish convention of referring to a scriptural passage or section by its more prominent associated figure, comparable to referring to an entire scriptural unit 'in the passage about the burning bush' without claiming every detail within that unit occurred at the bush itself. This project does not adjudicate between reading this as a straightforward factual imprecision or as reflecting this specific grammatical convention, noting that both positions are held by serious biblical scholars.

### 5. 'As it is written in Isaiah the prophet' -- yet the quotation combines Malachi and Isaiah

**References:** Mark 1:2-3; Malachi 3:1; Isaiah 40:3

**The claim:** Mark 1:2-3 introduces a scriptural quotation with the attribution 'as it is written in Isaiah the prophet,' followed by a combined citation: the first portion ('Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way') matches Malachi 3:1, not Isaiah; only the second portion ('the voice of one crying in the wilderness...') matches Isaiah 40:3, the prophet actually named.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** Most modern translations based on the earlier, more widely preferred Greek manuscript tradition render 'Isaiah the prophet' specifically (preserving the more difficult, and textually earlier, reading), while KJV and NKJV, following the later Byzantine tradition underlying the Textus Receptus, render the smoothed-over plural 'the prophets' -- a genuine translation difference reflecting two different underlying Greek manuscript traditions at this specific verse.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'as it is written in Isaiah the prophet,' the more specific, singular reading, following the earlier and more widely preferred manuscript tradition (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'as it is written in the prophets,' the plural reading, following the Byzantine textual tradition underlying this translation.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders 'as it is written in the Prophets,' the plural reading.
- **NASB:** NASB also renders 'as it is written in Isaiah the prophet.'
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'as it is written in Isaiah the prophet.'

**Reconciliation:** This is treated the same way as the closely comparable case addressed at greater length in the companion Matthew and Zechariah documents (regarding Matthew 27:9-10's attribution of a Zechariah citation to 'Jeremiah'): most commentators read Mark as naming the more prominent and better-known of the two prophets he draws on for this composite citation (Isaiah being by far the more widely read, quoted, and theologically significant of the two prophetic books in first-century Jewish and early Christian usage), attributing a blended quotation to its most substantial or best-known single source, a recognized and non-deceptive ancient citation convention rather than a claim that every word of the following quotation appears verbatim in that specific book. This textual detail is itself independently confirmed as a recognized ancient difficulty: a number of surviving Greek manuscripts of Mark 'correct' the reading to the more general 'in the prophets' (plural) rather than 'in Isaiah the prophet' specifically -- a documented textual variant most scholars read as a later scribal attempt to smooth over the apparent difficulty, rather than as evidence for what Mark himself originally wrote, since the more difficult, specific reading ('in Isaiah the prophet') is both more strongly attested in the earliest and best manuscripts and the kind of reading scribes would be more likely to soften than to introduce.

### 6. Bartimaeus at Jericho -- Mark's own specific version of a detail addressed at greater length in the companion Matthew document

**References:** Mark 10:46-52; Matthew 20:29-34; Luke 18:35-43

**The claim:** Mark's account of the healing at Jericho names a single blind man, Bartimaeus, son of Timaeus (10:46) -- addressed at greater length in the companion Matthew document, which discusses Matthew's parallel account of two blind men at the same event.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Mark 10:46-52 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Matthew document for the fuller discussion of this question.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Matthew document, which addresses the standard harmonization: Mark's narrower focus on a single, specifically named individual (Bartimaeus, apparently known well enough within the early Christian community Mark wrote for to be named directly, unlike almost every other unnamed individual healed throughout Mark's Gospel) does not constitute a claim that no second person was also healed on the same occasion, which Matthew's account alone records. Mark's specific naming of Bartimaeus is generally read as reflecting his own particular access to, or interest in, this individual's specific identity and subsequent reputation, rather than as evidence against a second, unnamed companion's presence.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. 'Take nothing for their journey except a staff' -- vs. Matthew's and Luke's parallel instructions

**References:** Mark 6:8-9; Matthew 10:9-10; Luke 9:3

**The claim:** Mark 6:8-9 records Jesus instructing the twelve disciples as he sends them out that they should 'take nothing for their journey except a staff -- no bread, no bag, no money in their belts.' Matthew's parallel account of the same instruction (10:9-10) and Luke's (9:3) both explicitly include the staff among the items the disciples are told NOT to take, directly opposite to Mark's specific allowance of a staff.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Mark's 'except a staff' and Matthew's and Luke's contrary instructions exactly as given in each Gospel, without harmonizing the specific detail in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and linguistic interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this small but genuine discrepancy as reflecting either a difference in how each Gospel author translated or paraphrased Jesus' original Aramaic instruction into Greek, or a real distinction in what was meant. On the second possibility, the disciples were forbidden to specifically acquire or provide for the journey an extra staff beyond what they may have already been using, which Matthew and Luke may be emphasizing, but were permitted to continue carrying an ordinary walking staff already in their possession, which Mark may be emphasizing. Ancient travelers commonly used a walking staff as a matter of course, making it plausible that Jesus' instruction concerned not adding to one's provisions or preparing specially for the journey -- no extra staff, no bag, no bread, no money -- rather than stripping away the ordinary walking implement already in a traveler's hand. Matthew and Luke's fuller lists may simply compress this nuance into a simpler 'do not take' formula, while Mark's version preserves the specific allowance more precisely. This project presents this as a minor but genuine point of specific verbal difference between the Synoptic accounts of the same instruction, addressed candidly rather than forced into an artificially seamless harmony. The core instruction -- travel light, without provisions, relying on hospitality -- remains consistent and unambiguous across all three accounts regardless of how this specific detail is resolved.

### 8. Jesus's own family: 'he is out of his mind' -- a detail some read as evidence of Mark's unusually early and unpolished character

**References:** Mark 3:20-21

**The claim:** Mark 3:20-21 records that when Jesus' own family heard about the crowds and demands surrounding his ministry, 'they went out to seize him, for they were saying, He is out of his mind' -- a blunt, unflattering detail about Jesus' own family's skepticism and concern for his mental state, a detail neither Matthew nor Luke's own parallel material includes in comparably direct terms.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Mark 3:20-21 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a question of comparative Gospel composition and historical reliability, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable feature some scholars read as supporting Mark's priority and historical reliability relative to the other Synoptics, via the widely recognized 'criterion of embarrassment' applied throughout historical-critical study of the Gospels (the principle that a detail casting an unflattering or difficult light on a movement's own central figures or their families is generally more likely to be historically authentic than invented, since early Christian tradition would have had every motive to omit or soften such a detail rather than create it). Matthew's and Luke's own comparative silence regarding this specific, blunt characterization is read by many scholars not as a contradiction of Mark's account, but as each later author's own selective and more polished handling of the same underlying tradition, consistent with a broader pattern some scholars identify of Mark's Gospel retaining rawer, less theologically smoothed details that Matthew and Luke, writing somewhat later and drawing on Mark as a source (per the majority scholarly view of Markan priority), each independently chose to omit or soften in their own retelling.

### 9. The young man who fled naked -- a unique, unexplained detail

**References:** Mark 14:51-52

**The claim:** Mark 14:51-52 includes a brief, strange detail found in no other Gospel: at Jesus' arrest in Gethsemane, 'a young man followed him, with nothing but a linen cloth about his body. And they seized him, but he left the linen cloth and ran away naked.' The passage offers no explanation of who this young man was or why the detail is included at all.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Mark 14:51-52 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a notable, unexplained narrative detail unique to this Gospel, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction (nothing elsewhere in Scripture addresses or conflicts with this detail), but is included here as a notable and much-discussed feature unique to Mark's Gospel. The most common scholarly speculation, though never stated explicitly in the text itself and impossible to confirm with certainty, holds this may be a subtle, indirect autobiographical detail -- the Gospel's own traditional author, John Mark, discreetly including a personal eyewitness memory of his own presence at the arrest scene without drawing further attention to himself by name, a device with some rough parallel in how the Gospel of John refers to its own author only as 'the disciple whom Jesus loved' rather than by name. This remains speculative and unprovable from the text itself, and this project does not assert it as established fact, noting only that it is the most commonly proposed explanation among commentators addressing this curious, otherwise unexplained detail.

### 10. What did Jesus do immediately after his baptism?

**References:** Mark 1:12-13; John 1:29-2:11

**The claim:** Mark 1:12-13 has the Spirit 'immediately' drive Jesus into the wilderness for forty days following his baptism. John's Gospel, by contrast, describes a sequence of events shortly after Jesus' baptism -- John the Baptist's testimony, the calling of the first disciples, and the wedding at Cana 'the third day' (John 1:29-2:11) -- without any mention of a wilderness period intervening.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** John's Gospel is widely recognized to select and arrange its material with substantial theological and topical purpose rather than attempting an exhaustive chronicle of every event in sequence (John's own closing words, 21:25, explicitly acknowledge his selective scope). The events John narrates in 1:29-2:11 are most naturally read as occurring after Jesus' forty-day wilderness temptation (which John's Gospel simply does not narrate at all, consistent with its frequent omission of material the Synoptics already cover), with 'the third day' of the Cana wedding counted from the events John has just finished narrating, not from the baptism itself. Mark's 'immediately' describes the Spirit's driving Jesus into the wilderness right after the baptism, a detail John's Gospel is not disputing by simply not repeating it.

### 11. Where did Jesus heal a blind man -- Bethsaida, Jericho, or elsewhere?

**References:** Mark 8:22-26; Mark 10:46-52; John 9:1-7

**The claim:** Mark 8:22-26 records Jesus healing a blind man at Bethsaida. Mark 10:46-52 records a different blind man, Bartimaeus, healed near Jericho. John 9:1-7 records still another blind man (blind from birth) healed near Jerusalem, at the pool of Siloam.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These are simply three separate healing miracles, performed on three different blind individuals, at three different locations and points during Jesus' ministry -- there is no claim anywhere in the Gospels that Jesus healed only one blind person, and a itinerant healing ministry conducted over a period of roughly three years would naturally include multiple such encounters in different towns. Each account is internally consistent regarding its own specific location and circumstances; the existence of more than one blind-healing narrative across the Gospels reflects the range of Jesus' recorded ministry, not a contradiction about a single event.

### 12. Who can cast out devils in the name of Jesus?

**References:** Mark 9:38-41

**The claim:** Mark 9:38 has John tell Jesus, 'Master, we saw one casting out devils in thy name, and he followeth not us: and we forbad him, because he followeth not us.' Jesus responds, 'Forbid him not... he that is not against us is on our part' (9:39-40) -- an inclusive posture toward an unaffiliated exorcist some read as in tension with other, more exclusive-sounding statements about who genuinely acts for Christ.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This same episode (paralleled at Luke 9:49-50) is addressed at greater length in this project's companion Matthew document, which discusses it alongside Matthew 12:30's differently-worded and differently-situated statement, 'he that is not with me is against me' -- the two statements address different situations (direct hostility toward Christ himself, versus cautious openness toward someone acting favorably, if informally, in his name) rather than offering competing rules for the same case. Within this specific episode on its own terms, Jesus' point to the disciples is that authentic, effective ministry done in his name and for his purposes should not be suppressed merely because it comes from someone outside their immediate circle -- a caution against sectarian exclusivity, not a claim that absolutely anyone invoking Jesus' name, regardless of their actual allegiance or conduct, is thereby validated (compare Matthew 7:22-23's warning about those who invoke Jesus' name without genuine relationship to him, addressed elsewhere in the companion Matthew document).

### 13. Did the Temple veil tear before, or after, Jesus' death?

**References:** Mark 15:37-38; Matthew 27:50-51; Luke 23:45-46

**The claim:** Mark 15:37-38 and Matthew 27:50-51 both narrate the veil tearing immediately after Jesus' death cry, in that sequence. Luke 23:45-46, however, mentions the veil tearing before narrating Jesus' final words and death: 'the sun was darkened, and the veil of the temple was rent in the midst. And when Jesus had cried with a loud voice, he said, Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit... he gave up the ghost.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** All three accounts describe events happening within moments of each other in immediate succession -- darkness, the veil tearing, Jesus' final cry, and his death -- and the apparent ordering difference reflects each writer's own narrative arrangement rather than a substantive disagreement about what happened or when. Luke's placement most plausibly reflects a topical grouping of the supernatural signs accompanying the crucifixion (the darkness and the veil tearing together) before returning to narrate Jesus' own final words and death, a common and unremarkable feature of ancient narrative composition that does not always report simultaneous or near-simultaneous events in strict linear order. Matthew and Mark's placement (veil tearing immediately following the death cry) and Luke's grouping are both compatible with the same tightly-clustered sequence of events occurring within the same brief span of time.

## Chronology

### 14. 'It was the third hour when they crucified him' -- vs. John's 'about the sixth hour'

**References:** Mark 15:25; John 19:14

**The claim:** Mark 15:25 states plainly, 'it was the third hour when they crucified him' (roughly 9:00 a.m. by standard ancient reckoning from sunrise). John 19:14, however, describes Pilate still presenting Jesus for judgment and sentencing 'about the sixth hour' (roughly noon) -- meaning the crucifixion itself, which follows this scene in John's own narrative, could not have begun before roughly noon, several hours later than Mark's stated timing.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'the third hour' at Mark 15:25 and 'about the sixth hour' at John 19:14 exactly as given, without adjusting either figure to match the other or converting between ancient time-reckoning systems in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and chronological interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated chronological question, and several harmonizations have been proposed without full scholarly consensus. Some commentators propose that Mark and John use two different ancient time-reckoning systems -- Mark following the more common Jewish reckoning (counting hours from approximately 6:00 a.m. sunrise, making the 'third hour' roughly 9:00 a.m.), while John, writing later and possibly for a broader Greco-Roman audience, uses Roman civil time reckoning (counting from midnight, making the 'sixth hour' roughly 6:00 a.m.) -- which would place John's timing several hours earlier than a same-system reading would suggest, narrowing rather than eliminating the gap with Mark's account. Other commentators propose that at least one of the two numbers reflects an early textual transmission error (a Greek gamma, digamma, or other numeral abbreviation being easily miscopied for another in the course of manuscript transmission), a possibility with some limited manuscript support at John 19:14 specifically, where a small number of ancient manuscripts and versions read 'third hour' rather than 'sixth,' matching Mark. Still others read both time references as approximate, rounded indications rather than precise clock-times (ancient timekeeping generally lacked the precision moderns associate with specific hours, and both Gospels' authors may simply be giving a general sense of 'mid-morning' versus 'around midday' without intending exact, mutually exclusive precision). This project does not adjudicate between these proposed solutions, noting that this remains one of the more genuinely debated chronological details in the Passion narratives.

### 15. How many times did the rooster crow, and how many denials, in Peter's denial narrative?

**References:** Mark 14:30, 66-72; Matthew 26:34, 69-75; Luke 22:34, 54-62; John 13:38; John 18:15-27

**The claim:** Mark's account of Peter's denial is more specific than the other three Gospels: Jesus tells Peter 'before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times' (Mark 14:30), and Mark then narrates a rooster crowing once after Peter's first denial (14:68) and a second time after his third denial (14:72). Matthew, Luke, and John all record Jesus's prediction using only a single crowing ('before the rooster crows,' Matthew 26:34; Luke 22:34; John 13:38), and none of the three narrates any crowing until after Peter's third and final denial. This raises the specific question of whether Mark's 'twice' and its earlier crowing are compatible with, or contradict, the other Gospels' apparent single-crowing framework -- and it is also the site of a genuine textual variant, since some manuscripts (and the translations based on them) include the detail of a first crowing at 14:68 while others omit it.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP's rendering at 14:68 follows the modern critical text's omission of the first crowing, while retaining 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72 (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB omits the first crowing at 14:68, reading '...he went out to the entryway,' though it retains 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72 (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV omits the first crowing at 14:68, reading simply, '...and he went out into the gateway' (with no mention of a rooster at this point), following the modern critical Greek text, though it retains 'twice' at 14:30 and 'the second time' at 14:72 (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV includes the first crowing at 14:68, '...and he went out into the porch; and the cock crew,' and renders 14:30 and 14:72 with 'twice' (verified against the published text), following the Byzantine textual tradition.
- **NKJV:** NKJV likewise includes the first crowing at 14:68, '...and he went out on the porch, and a rooster crowed,' and renders 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72 (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB omits the first crowing at 14:68, reading '...And he went out onto the porch' with no rooster mentioned, though it retains 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72 (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB omits the first crowing at 14:68, following NASB's pattern, though it retains 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72 (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV omits the first crowing at 14:68, reading '...he went out into the entryway,' though it retains 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72 (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT includes the detail at 14:68, 'and he went out into the entryway. Just then, a rooster crowed' (verified against the published text), and retains 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72, differing from ESV/NASB/NIV/CSB in this specific respect.
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV omits the first crowing at 14:68, following the modern critical text, while retaining 'twice' at 14:30 and 14:72 (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** Regarding the underlying event: most commentators regard this as complementary detail rather than contradiction. Matthew, Luke, and John are not claiming there was only a single crowing that night -- they simply do not record the earlier one, focusing their narration on the crowing that immediately followed Peter's third denial and triggered his recollection of Jesus's words, which is the crowing all four Gospels agree occurred at that point. Mark's additional detail of an earlier crowing after the first denial is widely attributed to Peter's own testimony standing behind Mark's Gospel (early tradition, including Papias as reported by Eusebius, held that Mark's account reflects Peter's own preaching) -- a natural source for this kind of small, vivid, personally remembered detail that the other Evangelists, working from different sources or with different narrative aims, did not include. Separately, the specific manuscript variant at Mark 14:68 (whether the verse mentions a first crowing at all) is a genuine, independently documented textual question: the phrase is present in the Byzantine textual tradition underlying the Textus Receptus (and so in KJV and NKJV) but absent from several important early manuscripts followed by the modern critical text (and so omitted by ESV, NASB, NIV, CSB, LSB, and RSV2CE, though retained by NLT). Regardless of which reading is preferred at 14:68, Mark's overall 'twice' framework at 14:30 and 14:72 is well attested and is not, on its own, in conflict with the other Gospels' simpler references to 'the rooster crowing' generally.

## Theological Tensions

### 16. Are those who correctly believe Jesus is the Christ of God saved, given that even demons make this same confession?

**References:** Mark 1:23-24; James 2:19

**The claim:** Mark 1:23-24 has an unclean spirit in the synagogue correctly identify Jesus: 'I know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God.' James 2:19 states, 'the devils also believe, and tremble' -- both showing that demonic spirits possess accurate knowledge of Jesus' true identity.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction but as the New Testament's own explicit illustration of the difference between correct intellectual knowledge or belief about Jesus' identity and genuine, saving faith -- James 2:19 makes exactly this point directly, using the demons' correct-but-unsaving 'belief' as a foil to argue that mere factual assent, without corresponding trust, love, and obedience, is not the kind of faith that saves (James 2:14-26's broader argument). The demon in Mark 1:23-24 possesses accurate theological knowledge of who Jesus is while remaining in active, hostile rebellion against him -- precisely the point both passages illustrate: correct belief about Jesus and saving faith in him are not automatically the same thing, and Scripture consistently distinguishes them rather than treating accurate knowledge alone as sufficient.

### 17. When was the Holy Ghost given -- to David, centuries before Christ, or only at Pentecost?

**References:** Mark 12:36; Acts 2:1-4; John 7:39

**The claim:** Mark 12:36 has Jesus say, 'David himself said by the Holy Ghost,' describing the Spirit's activity in inspiring David centuries before Christ. Yet John 7:39 states that 'the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified,' and Acts 2:1-4 describes the Spirit being poured out on the church for the first time at Pentecost -- both suggesting the Spirit's giving was a new, specific event rather than something already true in David's day.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These describe two different modes or stages of the Holy Spirit's activity across redemptive history, not competing claims about a single event: throughout the Old Testament, the Spirit selectively and temporarily empowered specific individuals -- prophets, judges, kings such as David -- for specific tasks, without this constituting the universal, permanent indwelling of every believer the New Testament describes as a distinct, later development. John 7:39 and Acts 2 both describe this qualitatively new and more expansive stage -- the Spirit's outpouring on all believers as a permanent indwelling presence, inaugurated at Pentecost following Christ's glorification -- as something genuinely new in redemptive history, without denying the Spirit's real, if more selective and temporary, activity in the lives of Old Testament figures like David, which Mark 12:36 straightforwardly describes.
