# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 Kings

This document catalogs 13 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of 2
Kings, either entirely internal to 2 Kings or between 2 Kings and a directly related passage
elsewhere (2 Chronicles, Luke, John, Hosea). For each item, this document states the claim as
it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the
claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entry 2 (the youths mauled by bears) is presented without fully
resolving the discomfort many readers feel -- several mitigating considerations are offered,
but this entry does not claim they fully settle the matter, consistent with this project's
practice of not manufacturing false certainty where the evidence does not clearly support it.

**Verification status:** Entry 5 (the Hezekiah/Josiah "none like him" statements) and entry 11
(Hezekiah's added years and Manasseh's birth chronology) were both freshly checked against the
published texts and multiple commentary sources for this document. Entry 12 (the Jehoiachin
and Ahaziah regnal-age disputes) reuses wording already verified in this project's general
document set, cross-referenced rather than re-derived. The remaining entries are narrative,
ethical, and theological questions where all ten translations render the same underlying text
and the harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Ethical Tensions

### 1. Elijah calls down fire on Ahaziah's captains -- yet Jesus rebukes James and John for wanting the same

**References:** 2 Kings 1:9-12; Luke 9:51-56

**The claim:** When King Ahaziah sends a captain and fifty men to summon Elijah, Elijah calls down fire from heaven that consumes them -- and does so again to a second company of fifty (1:9-12). Centuries later, when a Samaritan village refuses to welcome Jesus, James and John ask, 'Lord, do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them, just as Elijah did?' -- and Jesus 'turned and rebuked them' (Luke 9:54-55, in manuscripts that include this reading), apparently condemning the very action Elijah is remembered for and, in 2 Kings, not condemned for.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** Most modern translations render Luke 9:55 briefly ('he turned and rebuked them') without the longer KJV/NKJV addition about the Son of Man's mission, treating it as a later scribal expansion; this textual variation does not affect the core reconciliation, which rests on the differing authority and context of the two episodes rather than on the specific wording of Jesus' rebuke.
- **ESV:** ESV omits the longer reading from the main text, rendering simply 'he turned and rebuked them,' with a footnote on some manuscripts' longer addition.
- **KJV:** KJV includes the longer reading at Luke 9:55-56, 'and said, Ye know not what manner of spirit ye are of. For the Son of man is not come to destroy men's lives, but to save them,' following the Textus Receptus.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also includes this longer reading, typically with a footnote on its absence from the earliest manuscripts.
- **NASB:** NASB omits the longer reading from the main text, with a footnote on the manuscript variation.
- **NIV:** NIV omits the longer reading, footnoting some manuscripts' addition.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish these on both authority and context: Elijah acted as a recognized prophet directly confronting a king (Ahaziah) who had already, in the same episode, sent soldiers specifically to arrest a man of God consulting a foreign deity instead of the LORD (1:2-4, 6) -- an act of judgment against royal defiance of a specific prior prophetic warning, in a period (compare 1 Kings 18's earlier public contest with Baal's prophets) marked by direct, visible confrontations between the LORD's prophets and apostate royal power. James and John, by contrast, propose calling down destructive judgment on an entire village for a social slight (declining hospitality, Luke 9:53) during Jesus' mission of proclaiming grace and salvation, not judgment -- Jesus' rebuke targets the disciples' vengeful spirit and the mismatched purpose of his own mission (some manuscripts of Luke's Gospel add, 'the Son of Man came not to destroy men's lives but to save them,' though this longer reading is not found in the earliest manuscripts), not a retroactive condemnation of Elijah's specific prophetic act centuries earlier under entirely different circumstances and authority.

### 2. Forty-two youths mauled by bears for mocking Elisha -- proportionate judgment, or excessive?

**References:** 2 Kings 2:23-24

**The claim:** As Elisha travels to Bethel, a group of young men come out of the city and jeer at him, 'Go up, you baldhead! Go up, you baldhead!' Elisha curses them in the name of the LORD, and two female bears come out of the woods and maul forty-two of them (2:23-24) -- a response some read as wildly disproportionate to a childish taunt.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, LSB, RSV2CE:** Translations vary between renderings suggesting quite young children ('little children,' 'small boys') and renderings that leave the age more open ('youths,' 'young men'), which affects how sharply the proportionality question is felt in English; the underlying Hebrew term's wide semantic range is a genuine feature of the text, not a translation error in either direction.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'small boys,' similar in effect to the KJV.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'little children' at 2:23, a rendering some point to as intensifying the apparent disproportion, though the same Hebrew word (ne'arim) is rendered 'young men' elsewhere in the KJV itself (e.g., of men old enough for military service).
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'youths,' a somewhat older-skewing term than the KJV's 'little children.'
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'young lads.'
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'boys,' though a footnote in some editions notes the Hebrew term's wider possible age range.
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'boys,' with a footnote on the term's range.

**Reconciliation:** Several considerations are commonly offered by commentators, without fully resolving the discomfort many readers feel. First, the Hebrew term rendered 'young men' or 'youths' (ne'arim) has a wide semantic range covering ages from young children to men of military age elsewhere in the Old Testament, and the size of the group -- large enough to produce 42 mauled individuals -- suggests an organized, threatening mob of a socially significant size rather than a handful of small children. Some scholars read this as a coordinated act of public contempt for a recognized prophet's God-given authority, following immediately upon Elijah's dramatic ascension and testing whether Elisha carried the same divine backing, rather than harmless child's play. Second, 'baldhead' may carry a specific insulting association with Elisha's prophetic office or with lepers and social outcasts in the ancient Near East, intensifying the insult's seriousness as a rejection of his prophetic legitimacy and, by extension, of the God who sent him, immediately following the dramatic events at the Jordan. Third, the episode is nonetheless treated by many conservative commentators as a genuinely severe judgment whose full rationale is not completely spelled out in the text. This entry does not claim the discomfort many readers feel is fully resolved by these considerations; it is included as one of the harder passages in this book to reconcile with a simple reading of proportionate justice.

## Theological Tensions

### 3. Elijah taken up into heaven -- yet 'no one has ascended into heaven'

**References:** 2 Kings 2:11; John 3:13

**The claim:** 2 Kings 2:11 narrates Elijah being taken up 'by a whirlwind into heaven' in a chariot and horses of fire, witnessed by Elisha. Jesus later states, 'no one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man' (John 3:13) -- apparently denying that anyone, including Elijah, had ever previously ascended there.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading John 3:13's specific christological point in its own context, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read John 3:13 in its specific context -- Jesus contrasting his own unique, first-hand, ongoing heavenly origin and authority to speak of heavenly things with any merely earthly teacher's secondhand knowledge (the immediate context, John 3:10-12, addresses Nicodemus's inability to grasp 'heavenly things') -- rather than as a general historical denial that Enoch (Genesis 5:24, Hebrews 11:5) or Elijah were ever physically taken from earth. On this reading, Jesus is making a unique christological claim about his own eternal origin and return (he 'descended,' uniquely, and therefore uniquely can speak with full authority of heavenly realities), not adjudicating a historical question about who else had, at some point, been taken up bodily -- a straightforward instance of a strong rhetorical statement making its specific theological point without functioning as an exhaustive historical inventory of every person who has ever left the earth.

### 4. Jehu commended for destroying Ahab's house -- yet later condemned for the same bloodshed

**References:** 2 Kings 9:1-10:31; 2 Kings 10:30; Hosea 1:4

**The claim:** 2 Kings 9-10 narrates Jehu's violent purge of Ahab's entire dynasty and the worship of Baal in Israel, carried out at the LORD's own command relayed through Elisha's prophetic associate (9:1-10). The LORD explicitly commends Jehu afterward: 'because you have done well in carrying out what is right in my eyes, and have done to the house of Ahab according to all that was in my heart, your sons of the fourth generation shall sit on the throne of Israel' (10:30). Yet the prophet Hosea, roughly a century later, has the LORD declare, 'I will punish the house of Jehu for the blood of Jezreel' (Hosea 1:4) -- seemingly condemning the very bloodshed 2 Kings has God commending.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the commendation in 2 Kings 10:30 and Hosea's later condemnation plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the commanded objective from the manner of its execution, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish between the divinely commanded and approved objective of Jehu's purge (removing an idolatrous royal house and Baal worship, which 2 Kings 10:30 commends) and the excessive, personally ambitious, and often needlessly brutal manner in which Jehu carried it out, which the wider narrative of 2 Kings 9-10 itself does not uniformly present as praiseworthy in every particular (his violent killing of King Ahaziah of Judah and 42 of his relatives, unrelated bystanders to Ahab's house, 10:12-14; and, per Hosea's own commentary, an excess of bloodshed beyond what was strictly commanded). Hosea's later condemnation is generally read as addressing this excessive manner and its long-term consequences for the nation (including the political instability and violence Jehu's methods normalized) rather than retracting the specific commendation given for the accomplishment of the LORD's stated purpose in 10:30 -- a pattern, some note, similar to how Assyria is elsewhere both used as God's instrument of judgment and separately condemned for its own excessive cruelty in carrying it out (Isaiah 10:5-15).

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 5. Hezekiah and Josiah: both described as unmatched among all the kings of Judah

**References:** 2 Kings 18:5; 2 Kings 23:25

**The claim:** 2 Kings 18:5 states of Hezekiah, 'after him was none like him among all the kings of Judah, nor any that were before him.' 2 Kings 23:25 states of Josiah, 'before him there was no king like him... nor did any like him arise after him' -- both kings receiving what reads as an identical, mutually exclusive claim to be uniquely unmatched among all the kings of Judah.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses plainly and consistently as given, preserving each one's specific, distinct ground for commendation (Hezekiah's trust; Josiah's whole-hearted turning per the Law); the harmonization rests on reading each superlative in its own specific context, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** A careful comparison of the two verses in their immediate context shows the author is commending each king for a specific, different quality, not making the identical claim twice: 2 Kings 18:5's superlative is explicitly and specifically about Hezekiah's trust ('he trusted in the LORD... so that after him was none like him'), while 2 Kings 23:25's superlative is explicitly and specifically about Josiah's wholehearted turning to the Law of Moses ('there was no king like him, who turned to the LORD with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might, according to all the Law of Moses'). The two statements are not stated as generic, all-purpose rankings of overall righteousness that would directly compete with one another, but as each king being unmatched in the particular respect being praised in that passage -- a pattern of specific, qualified superlatives rather than a single absolute ranking, and one which several commentators note is reinforced by the author's evident awareness of having made a similar statement about Hezekiah only five chapters earlier, making a simple oversight unlikely.

### 6. Was the LORD's promised victory over Moab actually fulfilled?

**References:** 2 Kings 3:16-19; 2 Kings 3:24-27

**The claim:** Before the joint Israelite-Judean-Edomite campaign against rebelling Moab, Elisha relays the LORD's promise: 'he will also give the Moabites into your hand, and you shall attack every fortified city... and ruin every good piece of land' (3:18-19). The campaign proceeds successfully at first (3:24-25), but ends abruptly when the king of Moab, in a desperate last act, sacrifices his own son on the city wall, and 'there came great wrath against Israel,' causing the Israelite-led coalition to withdraw without completing the conquest (3:26-27) -- raising the question of whether the LORD's promised victory was actually delivered.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the promise and the campaign's actual, abrupt conclusion plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the specific promised outcomes (achieved per the text's own account) from the campaign's incomplete final scope, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that the specific terms of the promise (attacking fortified cities, ruining agricultural land) were substantially fulfilled per the narrative's own account in verse 25 before the campaign's abrupt end, and that the promise itself never explicitly guaranteed the total destruction or annexation of Moab (unlike the more categorical conquest commands given for Canaan). The 'great wrath' that causes the withdrawal (3:27) is widely discussed but not fully explained by the text itself -- proposals include Israel's own horrified reaction to witnessing human sacrifice (a natural, human response of revulsion cutting the campaign short on its own initiative, not a supernatural judgment against Israel), or a perceived spiritual danger the coalition attributed to the act and chose not to press further against; either way, the narrative does not present the promised military successes described in verse 18-19 as having failed to occur, only that the campaign concluded without pursuing the fullest possible outcome once this deeply disturbing event occurred.

### 7. Why was Huldah the prophetess consulted about the newly found Book of the Law?

**References:** 2 Kings 22:11-20

**The claim:** When the Book of the Law is discovered during Josiah's temple repairs, the king's officials are sent to consult 'Huldah the prophetess' (22:14) for the LORD's word on the matter -- rather than any of the male prophets some scholars believe were already active in Judah around this same period, raising a question about why a specific prophetess, otherwise unmentioned elsewhere in Scripture, was the one approached.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this passage plainly and consistently as given, without further comment on the selection; there is no translation variance on this point.

**Reconciliation:** The text itself gives no indication that this reflects any unusual or controversial choice -- it is narrated entirely without comment, exactly as other Old Testament episodes narrate consultations with recognized prophets and prophetesses without remark on their sex (compare Deborah, Judges 4:4-5, and Miriam, Exodus 15:20, addressed at greater length in the companion Judges document). Most historians simply note that Huldah is presented as the most immediately available and evidently well-regarded prophetic voice in Jerusalem at that specific moment for the officials sent (who may have gone to whichever recognized prophet was most readily accessible near the temple and palace complex, where Huldah is noted to reside, 22:14), without this implying any absence of other prophetic figures elsewhere or any exclusion of them from consideration -- the text simply does not explain why Huldah specifically was chosen over any other prophet who may have been active, and speculation beyond what the text states should be held loosely.

## Literary Composition

### 8. Manasseh's evil reign in Kings -- and his captivity and repentance, absent from Kings but present in Chronicles

**References:** 2 Kings 21:1-18; 2 Chronicles 33:10-13

**The claim:** 2 Kings 21 narrates Manasseh's exceptionally wicked, 55-year reign at length -- idolatry, child sacrifice, filling Jerusalem with innocent blood -- concluding simply that he 'slept with his fathers' (21:18), with no mention of any repentance or change of heart. 2 Chronicles 33:10-13, covering the same reign, adds an account entirely absent from Kings: Manasseh being taken captive to Babylon in bonds, humbling himself and praying to the LORD there, being restored to his throne, and undertaking reforms afterward (33:15-16).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both accounts plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing each book's distinct selective purpose, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction (Kings never states or implies that Manasseh did not repent, only that it does not mention it) but as a difference in selection and emphasis consistent with each book's distinct purpose: Kings, part of a larger narrative (running from Joshua through the end of the monarchy) whose overriding concern is explaining the exile theologically by tracing Judah's cumulative covenant unfaithfulness -- and which explicitly attributes the coming Babylonian exile to Manasseh's sins specifically, even after his own repentance, in 2 Kings 23:26-27 and 24:3-4 -- has a clear thematic reason to emphasize Manasseh's guilt and its lasting consequences rather than his personal restoration. Chronicles, written after the exile for a returning community needing a message of hope and the possibility of restoration through repentance, has an equally clear thematic reason to include Manasseh's captivity and repentance as an encouraging pattern for its own audience. Both accounts of the same reign are accurate as far as they go; each simply emphasizes what serves its book's larger theological argument.

### 9. Josiah's death: a brief note in Kings, a fuller and differently detailed account in Chronicles

**References:** 2 Kings 23:29-30; 2 Chronicles 35:20-24

**The claim:** 2 Kings 23:29-30 briefly states that Pharaoh Neco 'killed him at Megiddo,' with Josiah's servants then carrying his body back to Jerusalem in a chariot. 2 Chronicles 35:20-24 gives a considerably fuller account: Josiah disguising himself before the battle, being fatally wounded by archers, telling his servants to take him out of the battle because he was severely wounded, being moved to a second chariot, and dying en route to Jerusalem, followed by a description of Judah's national mourning and Jeremiah's lament for him.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both accounts plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is a matter of recognizing supplementation rather than contradiction, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a standard case of one account (Kings) summarizing briefly what a second, independent account (Chronicles) narrates at greater length and in more circumstantial detail, rather than two conflicting versions of the same event -- the core facts agree entirely (Josiah is fatally wounded in battle against Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo and dies before or shortly after reaching Jerusalem), and Chronicles' additional detail (the disguise, the specific manner of wounding, the chariot transfer, the national mourning) supplements rather than contradicts Kings' shorter account, in the same pattern seen with several other episodes in this project where Chronicles supplies fuller detail on events Kings narrates more briefly.

## Chronology

### 10. Hezekiah's illness and the Babylonian envoys: before, or after, Sennacherib's invasion?

**References:** 2 Kings 20:1-19; 2 Kings 18:13-19:37; Isaiah 38-39

**The claim:** 2 Kings narrates Hezekiah's illness, miraculous recovery, and the visit of Babylonian envoys in chapter 20 -- after the account of Sennacherib's invasion and the Assyrian army's destruction in chapters 18-19. Several details, however (including Hezekiah being told he would live '15 more years,' 20:6, and Sennacherib's invasion being dated to Hezekiah's 14th year, 18:13, which would place his death well within that added 15-year span rather than after it), have led many scholars to conclude the events of chapter 20 chronologically preceded, rather than followed, the Assyrian invasion narrated in chapters 18-19, with the book's structure grouping the material topically (a pattern also addressed regarding the Gospels and other historical books elsewhere in this project) rather than in strict chronological sequence.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both sections plainly and consistently as given, in the same chapter order; the harmonization concerns recognizing topical rather than strictly chronological arrangement, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, including many with a high view of the text's historical reliability, read the narrative as arranged thematically rather than strictly chronologically at this point: chapters 18-19 group together the extended, dramatic Assyrian crisis and its resolution as a single unit, while chapter 20's account of the illness, healing, and envoys -- which the numbers themselves suggest occurred earlier, likely around the time of or shortly before the Assyrian crisis -- is placed afterward for topical reasons (perhaps to end the Hezekiah narrative with the notice of the coming Babylonian threat, which sets up the rest of the book's movement toward exile). This is treated as a standard feature of ancient historical narrative organization (also addressed regarding Matthew's frequent topical, rather than strictly chronological, ordering elsewhere in this project) rather than an error, since the text nowhere explicitly claims a strict 'chapter 19 happened, then chapter 20 happened' sequence.

### 11. Hezekiah's added fifteen years and Manasseh's birth -- does the arithmetic actually hold together?

**References:** 2 Kings 18:2; 2 Kings 20:6; 2 Kings 21:1

**The claim:** 2 Kings 18:2 states Hezekiah reigned 29 years total. 2 Kings 20:6 has the LORD promise him 15 additional years of life at the time of his illness. 2 Kings 21:1 states Manasseh, Hezekiah's son and successor, was 12 years old when he began to reign -- inviting a check of whether these figures are internally consistent (since Manasseh, evidently Hezekiah's heir at the time of the illness, would need to have been born within a chronology that fits both the illness's timing and Hezekiah's total reign length).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the relevant figures (29-year reign, 15 additional years, Manasseh's age 12 at accession) identically; no translation differences are involved in this case.

**Reconciliation:** Working through the text's own explicit figures produces a chronology that fits without contradiction: if Hezekiah's illness and the promise of 15 more years occurred in roughly his 14th regnal year (a date consistent with 18:13's placement of Sennacherib's invasion, closely associated with this period), this leaves 15 years remaining until his death in his 29th year, exactly matching 20:6's figure. Manasseh, born at age 12 by the time of his own accession in Hezekiah's final (29th) year, would need to have been born sometime within roughly the first three years after Hezekiah's recovery (Hezekiah's years 17-20 or so) -- comfortably within the 15-year window granted after the illness, consistent with a widely held (though not explicitly stated in the text) inference that Hezekiah had no son before his illness, which some read as adding poignancy to his grief at the prospect of dying childless (20:1-3). This entry is included as a caution that not every apparent 'chronology problem' raised in popular lists withstands a careful check of the text's own explicitly stated figures, consistent with similar cautions offered elsewhere in this project.

## Numbers and Text

### 12. Regnal ages disputed between Kings and Chronicles: Jehoiachin and Ahaziah

**References:** 2 Kings 24:8; 2 Chronicles 36:9; 2 Kings 8:26; 2 Chronicles 22:2

**The claim:** 2 Kings 24:8 states Jehoiachin was 18 when he began to reign; the parallel at 2 Chronicles 36:9 (Masoretic Hebrew) states 8. Separately, 2 Kings 8:26 states Ahaziah was 22 when he began to reign; the parallel at 2 Chronicles 22:2 (Masoretic Hebrew) states 42 -- which would make him older than his own father.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Kings 24:8's '18' and 2 Kings 8:26's '22' identically and without dispute; see the general document set's fuller treatment for how each translation handles the corresponding Chronicles verses.

**Reconciliation:** Both cases are treated at greater length in this project's general document set, which addresses the specific translation-by-translation wording in detail (some translations harmonize 2 Chronicles 36:9 and 22:2 toward the Kings figures in their main text; others retain the Masoretic Chronicles figures with a footnote). What is notable for this book specifically is that in both cases, 2 Kings' own figure (18, and 22, respectively) is not itself in dispute among any of the ten translations surveyed in this project -- the textual variation occurs entirely on the Chronicles side of each parallel. Most textual scholars regard the Chronicles figures in the Masoretic tradition as scribal errors (a dropped or altered digit), with the Septuagint, Syriac, and other ancient witnesses supporting the Kings figures at both points.

### 13. Elijah and Elisha's parallel miracles -- coincidence, or a deliberate 'double portion' pattern?

**References:** 1 Kings 17:8-24; 2 Kings 2:8-14; 2 Kings 4:1-37, 42-44

**The claim:** Elijah and Elisha, master and prophetic successor, perform a strikingly similar set of miracles across 1 and 2 Kings: Elijah miraculously multiplies a widow's flour and oil so it does not run out (1 Kings 17:8-16), then raises her son from the dead (1 Kings 17:17-24); Elisha, decades later, miraculously multiplies a different widow's oil to pay her debts (2 Kings 4:1-7), then raises the son of the Shunammite woman from the dead (2 Kings 4:18-37). This raises a literary question -- whether these represent independent, coincidentally similar events, or a deliberate narrative pattern in which Elisha's ministry is presented as intentionally paralleling and even doubling his mentor's.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both sets of miracle accounts (1 Kings 17:8-24 and 2 Kings 4:1-37) plainly and consistently as given; the literary relationship between the two prophets' ministries is a matter of narrative structure and theological interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated by scholars as a contradiction but as a deliberate literary and theological pattern, widely discussed in academic Old Testament scholarship (notably an article by N. Levine, 'Twice as Much of Your Spirit: Pattern, Parallel and Paronomasia in the Miracles of Elijah and Elisha,' Journal for the Study of the Old Testament 85, 1999). The parallel is explicitly anchored in the text itself: when Elisha is about to succeed Elijah, he specifically requests 'a double portion of your spirit' (2 Kings 2:9), and traditional counts of each prophet's recorded miracles find Elijah credited with eight and Elisha with sixteen -- exactly double, matching the terms of Elisha's own request. The oil-and-resurrection sequence is one of several such doubled or intensified parallels between the two prophetic careers (others include each prophet dividing the Jordan River, 2 Kings 2:8, 14, and each multiplying food for a crowd, 1 Kings 17:14-16 and 2 Kings 4:42-44). Notably, the two accounts are not identical: Elijah's oil-and-resurrection miracles both benefit the same woman in Zarephath, forming a single connected episode, while Elisha's benefit two different women in two separate incidents -- a distinction some scholars read as Elisha's ministry deliberately extending Elijah's pattern to a wider circle of beneficiaries, consistent with the broader 'doubling' theme running through the two prophetic careers.
