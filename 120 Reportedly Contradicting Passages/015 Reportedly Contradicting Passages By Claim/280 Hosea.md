# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Hosea

This document catalogs 11 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Hosea, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with Matthew, Romans, 1
Corinthians, 1 Peter, 1 Samuel, and 2 Kings. For each item, this document states the claim as
it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim
appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's central metaphor:** Hosea's extended marital metaphor for God's
relationship with Israel generates several of this document's entries (1, 2, and 9), and this
document does not force a single resolution on the literal-versus-visionary question
surrounding Hosea's own marriage (entry 1).

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. God commands the prophet to marry a promiscuous woman -- literal command, vision, or allegory?

**References:** Hosea 1:2-3

**The claim:** Hosea 1:2 opens the book with God's startling instruction: 'Go, take to yourself a wife of whoredom and have children of whoredom, for the land commits great whoredom by forsaking the LORD.' Read as a literal historical command, this raises the question of whether God directly instructed a prophet to marry an already, or soon-to-be, unfaithful woman -- and whether this is compatible with the sexual and marital ethics affirmed elsewhere in Scripture.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 1:2-3 plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here concerns literary genre and historical reality, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators are genuinely divided on how literally to read this opening command, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Many conservative commentators read the marriage as a literal historical event -- Hosea genuinely married a woman named Gomer, who either was already promiscuous or (a reading some find more likely, given the phrase 'children of whoredom' applying even to the offspring) became unfaithful only after the marriage, with the entire domestic tragedy that follows serving as a lived, embodied object lesson of Israel's own covenant unfaithfulness toward God -- a costly, painful, and personally humiliating prophetic sign-act, comparable to several other prophets' divinely commanded symbolic actions addressed elsewhere in this project (Isaiah walking stripped and barefoot, Isaiah 20; Ezekiel's various acted-out prophecies, addressed in the companion Ezekiel document), none of which are read as endorsing the specific acts themselves as ordinarily appropriate, but as extraordinary, once-only prophetic communication given the gravity of the message. Other commentators read the account as a symbolic vision or parable presented in narrative form rather than a literal historical marriage -- consistent with how several other prophetic sign-acts throughout Scripture are understood by some interpreters as visionary rather than literally, physically enacted (a question this project also touches on regarding Ezekiel's more extreme symbolic acts) -- in which case the theological point (God's own costly, faithful love toward an unfaithful people) stands independently of whether Hosea's own domestic life was literally affected in the way the narrative describes.

### 2. Hosea 1 and Hosea 3: the same troubled marriage, or two different women?

**References:** Hosea 1:2-9; Hosea 3:1-3

**The claim:** Hosea 1 introduces Hosea's marriage to 'Gomer, the daughter of Diblaim' (1:3) and their three symbolically-named children. Hosea 3:1 then instructs, 'Go again, love a woman who is loved by another man and is an adulteress,' without naming her -- raising a question about whether this is a renewed instruction concerning the same Gomer (now estranged, unfaithful, and perhaps enslaved or otherwise bound to another man), or an instruction to take up with an entirely different, unnamed woman in a second marriage.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both chapters plainly and consistently as given, without explicitly identifying or distinguishing the woman named in chapter 3 in the text itself; the question addressed here is a matter of narrative interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The great majority of commentators read chapter 3 as describing Hosea's continued, costly love for the same Gomer introduced in chapter 1, now living in unfaithfulness and evident degradation (possibly having become a slave or indentured servant to another man, given the price Hosea pays to redeem her, 3:2), rather than as an account of an entirely separate second marriage -- the phrase 'go again' (3:1) most naturally connects the episode to the earlier marriage narrative already established, and the entire point of the extended metaphor (God's persistent, redeeming love for an unfaithful covenant partner, even after repeated betrayal) is heightened, not diminished, by reading chapter 3 as Hosea renewing and reaffirming his commitment to the very same woman who had already proven unfaithful, mirroring God's own continued pursuit of Israel despite the nation's repeated idolatry. A minority of commentators, noting the text's own lack of an explicit statement identifying the woman in chapter 3 as Gomer by name, leave open the possibility of two separate but thematically parallel prophetic marriages, without this significantly changing the passage's overall theological message either way.

## Theological Tensions

### 3. 'I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice' -- the source text Jesus twice quotes

**References:** Hosea 6:6; Matthew 9:13; Matthew 12:7; Leviticus 1-7

**The claim:** Hosea 6:6 has God declare, 'For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings' -- language that, like several similar prophetic statements addressed elsewhere in this project (Isaiah 1:11-17, 1 Samuel 15:22, Jeremiah 7:22-23), appears to flatly reject the sacrificial system in favor of covenant loyalty, and which Jesus himself quotes twice in the Gospels (Matthew 9:13, 12:7) to justify his own practice of associating with sinners and his disciples' Sabbath conduct -- raising the same general question about whether this is compatible with the Torah's own extensive sacrificial legislation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 6:6 and its New Testament citations plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same emphatic-priority reading addressed at greater length in the companion Isaiah, 1 Samuel, and Jeremiah documents, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This verse is treated at greater length regarding the general form of this tension addressed in this project's companion Isaiah, 1 Samuel, and Jeremiah documents, all of which point to the same resolution: this is best read as a recognized pattern of emphatic Hebrew relative-priority language (a construction that can state 'A, not B' to mean 'A takes clear priority over B' rather than 'B is categorically illegitimate'), rather than a considered, systematic rejection of the sacrificial system Hosea's own audience continued to practice. Hosea's specific concern throughout the surrounding chapters is Israel's persistent pattern of maintaining external religious ritual and covenant language ('Ephraim... Judah, your love is like a morning cloud,' 6:4) alongside ongoing covenant unfaithfulness in actual conduct -- ritual observance divorced from the 'steadfast love' (Hebrew hesed, covenant loyalty) and 'knowledge of God' that were always meant to accompany and give meaning to it. Jesus' own use of this verse in the Gospels applies the same underlying principle -- that covenant faithfulness expressed in mercy toward others takes priority over rigid ritual or legal formality when the two come into apparent conflict -- to his own specific first-century context, consistent with, not contradicting, Hosea's original point.

### 4. 'Out of Egypt I called my son' -- a historical statement about Israel, applied messianically to Jesus

**References:** Hosea 11:1; Matthew 2:14-15; Exodus 4:22-23

**The claim:** Hosea 11:1 states, 'When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son' -- a clear, backward-looking reference to the historical Exodus, describing the nation of Israel collectively as God's 'son' brought out of Egyptian slavery (echoing Exodus 4:22-23's similar language). Matthew 2:14-15 quotes this verse and applies it directly to the infant Jesus' own return from Egypt after Herod's death, stating this 'fulfilled' Hosea's words -- yet Hosea's own original statement is not phrased as a future prediction at all, but as a description of a past historical event concerning the entire nation, raising a sharper version of the question this project has addressed elsewhere (regarding Isaiah 7:14) about how New Testament 'fulfillment' language relates to an Old Testament passage's original context and grammatical form.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 11:1 and Matthew 2:14-15 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing typological, pattern-based fulfillment as a distinct and legitimate category from direct predictive prophecy, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the clearest and most frequently discussed examples of what scholars call 'typological' fulfillment, in which a New Testament writer identifies a pattern or correspondence between an earlier historical event and a later one, without claiming the earlier text was originally intended as direct predictive prophecy about the later event. Most commentators read Matthew's use of Hosea 11:1 this way: Matthew perceives and draws out a deliberate theological correspondence between Israel's own national story (called God's 'son,' delivered from Egypt, tested in the wilderness) and Jesus' own life (recapitulating and fulfilling Israel's history and calling as God's true, faithful Son in a way the historical nation itself ultimately did not, a theme Matthew's Gospel develops at length elsewhere, including Jesus' own forty days in the wilderness echoing Israel's forty years). This 'fulfillment' is generally understood as pattern-completion or typological escalation (a greater, truer instance of a divinely-intended pattern already present in the earlier history) rather than as a claim that Hosea himself, writing centuries earlier, consciously intended his own words as a specific, direct prediction about a future messiah's biography -- a recognized and legitimate category of scriptural fulfillment distinct from, though related to, direct predictive prophecy (compare the discussion of typology elsewhere in this project regarding Jonah's three days and Isaiah's Suffering Servant).

### 5. 'They made kings, but not through me' -- vs. the New Testament's affirmation that all authority is established by God

**References:** Hosea 8:4; Romans 13:1

**The claim:** Hosea 8:4 has God declare regarding the northern kingdom's various rulers, 'They made kings, but not through me. They set up princes, but I knew nothing of it' -- explicitly disowning divine sanction for these specific rulers (largely usurpers and assassins across Israel's unstable royal history, addressed at points in the companion 1-2 Kings documents). This raises a question in light of Romans 13:1's broader statement that 'there is no authority except from God,' and that 'those that exist have been instituted by God.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 8:4 and Romans 13:1 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing God's overarching sovereignty from specific approval of a given ruler's illegitimate accession, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish, as with several related tensions addressed elsewhere in this project (regarding Pharaoh's hardened heart, and Assyria as God's 'rod' in the companion Isaiah document), between God's ultimate, sovereign permission and overarching providential governance of all historical events (which Romans 13:1 affirms, in the specific context of establishing that civil government as an institution, and general obedience to it, reflects God's own ordained purpose for maintaining social order) and God's specific, positive approval or endorsement of any particular ruler's manner of gaining or exercising that position. Hosea 8:4 specifically denies that the northern kingdom's chain of usurping, often violently self-installed kings (in contrast to the divinely established and repeatedly affirmed Davidic line in Judah) had God's specific approval or legitimate anointing for how they came to power -- a statement about the illegitimate manner of their accession, not a denial that God remained sovereign over the overall political situation their usurpations produced, which Romans 13:1's broader theological point about God's ultimate governance over human authority structures in general does not require reading as an endorsement of every specific ruler's own particular, sometimes illegitimate path to power.

## Numbers and Text

### 6. 'Shall I ransom them from Sheol?' -- rhetorical questions ending in judgment, or a triumphant declaration of victory over death?

**References:** Hosea 13:14; 1 Corinthians 15:54-55

**The claim:** Hosea 13:14 is rendered very differently across English translations in a way that produces two starkly different overall meanings. Some translations (following a declarative reading) have God announce a triumphant, future victory over death: 'I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death. O death, I will be your plagues! O grave, I will be your destruction!' -- essentially the reading Paul draws on when he triumphantly quotes this passage regarding the resurrection in 1 Corinthians 15:54-55. Other translations (following an interrogative reading) render the same verse as a series of rhetorical questions expecting a negative answer, concluding not in triumph but in judgment: 'Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol?... O Death, where are your plagues?... Compassion is hidden from my eyes' -- a reading consistent with the surrounding chapter's otherwise unrelenting oracle of judgment against Israel (13:1-13, 13:15-16 describing Israel's coming devastation).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders interrogatively, with explicit bracketed clarification: 'Compassion is hidden from My eyes [because of their failure to repent]' (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB similarly opens declaratively ('I will ransom them from the power of Sheol. I will redeem them from death') but concludes 'Compassion is hidden from my eyes,' a hybrid rendering landing on the judgment side (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders interrogatively: 'Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol? Shall I redeem them from Death? O Death, where are your plagues? O Sheol, where is your sting? Compassion is hidden from my eyes' -- the judgment reading (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders this declaratively: 'I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction' -- the triumphant reading, matching the sense Paul draws on in 1 Corinthians 15 (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders declaratively: 'I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death. O Death, I will be your plagues! O Grave, I will be your destruction!' (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB also renders interrogatively, matching ESV's structure and concluding 'Compassion will be hidden from My sight' (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders interrogatively, matching NASB's structure exactly: 'Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol? Shall I redeem them from death? O Death, where are your thorns? O Sheol, where is your sting? Compassion will be hidden from My sight' (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders the opening declaratively ('I will deliver this people from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death') but the middle interrogatively ('Where, O death, are your plagues?'), concluding with explicit judgment: 'I will have no compassion' -- a hybrid rendering that nonetheless lands on the judgment side by its conclusion (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders interrogatively and most explicitly on the judgment side: 'Should I ransom them from the grave? Should I redeem them from death? O death, bring on your terrors! O grave, bring on your plagues! For I will not take pity on them' (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders interrogatively: 'Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol? Shall I redeem them from Death? O Death, where are your plagues? O Sheol, where is your destruction? Compassion is hid from my eyes' (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuine and significant translation-driven difference in meaning, not merely a stylistic variation, and this entry does not resolve fully between the two readings, since the underlying Hebrew grammar itself is ambiguous enough to support both (Hebrew does not always mark a rhetorical question with a distinct grammatical particle the way English typically does, meaning the same consonantal text can be read either as a series of triumphant declarations or as a series of ironic, unanswered questions depending on how the reader supplies the intonation). Those favoring the interrogative/judgment reading note this fits the immediate surrounding context far more naturally, since the verses immediately before and after this one describe Israel's coming, unmitigated devastation, making a sudden, unqualified declaration of triumphant deliverance seem contextually abrupt; the verse's own final clause ('compassion is hidden from my eyes,' present in some form in nearly every translation regardless of how the earlier lines are rendered) also reads more naturally as continuing a judgment theme than concluding a triumphant declaration. Those favoring the declarative/triumphant reading note that Paul's inspired use of this passage in 1 Corinthians 15 treats it as a genuine statement of resurrection triumph, which some hold should inform how the Hebrew's inherent ambiguity is best resolved -- on this view, Paul's application is not a case of typological reapplication (as addressed in entry 4 above regarding Hosea 11:1) but a direct, intended reading of the verse's own actual sense, meaning Hosea's oracle of judgment gives way, even within this single verse, to a further, ultimate divine promise of resurrection triumph that transcends the immediate judgment context.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. Jehu's dynasty condemned for the very bloodshed once commended

**References:** Hosea 1:4; 2 Kings 10:30

**The claim:** Hosea 1:4 has God declare he will 'punish the house of Jehu for the blood of Jezreel' -- the very bloodshed 2 Kings 10:30 explicitly commends Jehu for carrying out at God's own command.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 1:4 consistently; see the companion 2 Kings document for the fuller discussion of this tension with 2 Kings 10:30.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 2 Kings document, which addresses 2 Kings 10:30 as the primary text commending Jehu's accomplishment of God's stated purpose (destroying Ahab's idolatrous dynasty) while distinguishing this from the excessive, personally ambitious manner in which Jehu carried it out -- a distinction Hosea's later condemnation, writing roughly a century afterward, is generally read as addressing, consistent with the pattern of a commanded objective being commended even while its excessive or self-serving execution is separately condemned.

### 8. 'There I began to hate them' -- amid a book otherwise devoted to God's persistent, pursuing love

**References:** Hosea 9:15; Hosea 2:19; Hosea 11:8-9; Hosea 14:4

**The claim:** Hosea 9:15 has God declare, regarding Israel's persistent idolatry centered at Gilgal, 'all their evil is in Gilgal; there I began to hate them' -- language of divine hatred that stands in some tension with the same book's dominant, overarching theme of God's covenant love and persistent pursuit of an unfaithful people, expressed at length elsewhere in the book (2:19, 'I will betroth you to me forever'; 11:8-9, 'how can I give you up, Ephraim?... my heart recoils within me, my compassion grows warm and tender'; 14:4, 'I will heal their apostasy; I will love them freely').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 9:15 and the book's other statements of divine love plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the coexistence of genuine anger and genuine, ultimately triumphant covenant love within a single extended relational metaphor, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this, as with several similar apparent tensions between divine love and divine judgment addressed elsewhere in this project (regarding Psalm 89's own internal whiplash between covenant celebration and covenant complaint, and Ezekiel's stated preference for repentance amid extensive judgment oracles), as reflecting the coexistence, rather than contradiction, of genuine, settled covenant love with genuine, provoked anger and judgment at persistent, unrepented betrayal -- the same range of emotional and relational response a faithful spouse might experience toward a persistently unfaithful partner, which is precisely the extended marital metaphor structuring the entire book. Hosea 11:8-9, appearing later in the book than 9:15, explicitly dramatizes God's own internal struggle between righteous anger and compassion, concluding decisively in favor of mercy ('I will not execute my burning anger... for I am God and not a man') -- read together, the book's own progression suggests 9:15's language of 'hatred' describes a genuine, real, and provoked response to persistent sin at a specific point in Israel's history, not a settled, permanent replacement of the book's dominant, ultimately triumphant theme of covenant love.

### 9. 'I gave you a king in my anger' -- and the question of the monarchy's legitimacy

**References:** Hosea 13:10-11; 1 Samuel 8:4-9; 1 Samuel 12:16-19

**The claim:** Hosea 13:10-11 has God ask rhetorically, 'Where now is your king, to save you...? I gave you a king in my anger, and I took him away in my wrath' -- language describing the establishment of Israel's monarchy itself as an act of divine anger rather than blessing, connecting to the same question addressed at greater length in this project's companion 1 Samuel document regarding whether Israel's original request for a king (1 Samuel 8) was itself sinful.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 13:10-11 consistently; see the companion 1 Samuel document for the fuller discussion of the underlying question regarding the monarchy's establishment.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 1 Samuel document, which addresses the standard resolution: the sin identified in 1 Samuel 8 and 12 (and echoed here in Hosea 13:10-11) is located not in the institution of monarchy itself (which Deuteronomy 17:14-20 had already anticipated and legislated for as a legitimate future development) but in the specific, distrustful motive behind Israel's request -- wanting a king 'like all the nations' rather than trusting the LORD's own direct kingship and provision. Hosea 13:10-11, writing centuries later after witnessing the northern kingdom's monarchy's often violent, unstable history (addressed at points in the companion 1-2 Kings documents) culminate in national disaster, retrospectively frames the entire institution, at least for the northern kingdom specifically, as having been granted in a spirit of divine concession and judgment on Israel's own distrustful motive from the outset -- consistent with, rather than contradicting, the earlier account's own explanation of why the request was problematic in the first place.

### 10. Will Ephraim return to Egypt, or not?

**References:** Hosea 8:13; Hosea 9:3; Hosea 11:5

**The claim:** Hosea 8:13 and 9:3 both state that Ephraim (the northern kingdom) will 'return to Egypt' as part of its coming judgment and exile. Hosea 11:5, by contrast, states, 'He shall not return into the land of Egypt, but the Assyrian shall be his king.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read 8:13 and 9:3's 'return to Egypt' as figurative rather than a literal geographic prediction of exile specifically to Egypt -- Egypt, as the site of Israel's original bondage before the Exodus, functions throughout Hosea (and the wider prophetic tradition) as a symbol for a reversal of Israel's redemption: a return to slavery, humiliation, and the pre-covenant condition from which God had originally delivered the nation, regardless of the specific foreign power actually carrying out the coming exile. Hosea 11:5 then supplies the specific, literal historical detail -- Assyria, not Egypt, was in fact the power that conquered and exiled the northern kingdom (a straightforward historical fact confirmed in 2 Kings 17) -- clarifying that the 'return to Egypt' language of the earlier chapters describes the theological and symbolic character of the coming judgment (a reversal of redemption) rather than a literal prediction of exile to Egypt specifically, which the same book's own later, more historically precise statement correctly identifies as Assyria instead.

## Theological Interpretation

### 11. Judgment-names for Hosea's children, reversed as a promise -- and Paul's application of the reversal to Gentile inclusion

**References:** Hosea 1:6, 9; Hosea 2:1, 23; Romans 9:24-26; 1 Peter 2:10

**The claim:** Hosea names his second and third children 'Lo-Ruhamah' ('not pitied') and 'Lo-Ammi' ('not my people') as living symbols of God's judgment on the northern kingdom of Israel specifically (1:6, 9). The same book later promises a reversal of these very names as a sign of future restoration: 'you shall be called Sons of the living God... say to your brothers, You are my people, and to your sisters, You have received mercy' (2:1, 23). Paul, in Romans 9:24-26, and Peter, in 1 Peter 2:10, both quote this reversal and apply it not to a restored ethnic Israel specifically, but to the inclusion of Gentile believers (who, unlike Israel, had never previously been 'God's people' at all in the original covenant sense) within the New Testament church.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hosea 1:6, 9, 2:1, 23, and the New Testament citations plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing a legitimate extension of an underlying theological principle to a broader category than the original immediate referent, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this New Testament application as a legitimate broadening or extension of Hosea's own underlying principle -- God's gracious ability and willingness to call and constitute as 'his people' those who were not previously so, whether an alienated, judged portion of ethnic Israel (Hosea's own original, immediate reference) or Gentiles entirely outside the original Sinai covenant (Paul's and Peter's application) -- rather than a claim that Hosea's specific words were originally, directly about Gentile inclusion. This is treated as a related but distinct case from the typological pattern-fulfillment addressed in entry 4 above (regarding Hosea 11:1): here, the New Testament writers extend and generalize an underlying theological principle (God's grace extending 'not my people' status into 'my people' status) to a wider category of persons than the prophecy's own immediate, historical referent, a recognized and legitimate form of scriptural application distinct from, though related to, direct predictive fulfillment.
