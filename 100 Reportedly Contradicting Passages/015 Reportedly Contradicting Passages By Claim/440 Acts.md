# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Acts

This document catalogs 14 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the Acts of the
Apostles, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with Paul's own letters
(especially Galatians), the companion Matthew document, and Josephus's independent historical
record. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the
**KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own
wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the
standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses a genuine internal
tension across three accounts, by the same author, of the same event (Paul's conversion) --
presented candidly, including the observation that the most popular grammatical solution is
itself judged invalid by many scholars upon closer examination of Luke's own usage elsewhere.
Entry 4 addresses one of the most substantively debated chronological questions in New
Testament scholarship, directly affecting how Paul's own letters are correlated with Acts'
narrative.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 3, and 4 are presented with genuine scholarly diversity of
opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Paul's conversion, told three times: did his companions hear the voice, or not?

**References:** Acts 9:1-19; Acts 22:6-16; Acts 26:12-18

**The claim:** Acts records Paul's Damascus road conversion three separate times -- once in Luke's own third-person narration (9:1-19), and twice more in Paul's own first-person retelling to different audiences (22:6-16, before a Jerusalem crowd; 26:12-18, before King Agrippa). The three accounts differ in some specific details, most notably regarding Paul's traveling companions: 9:7 states the men 'heard the voice but saw no one,' while 22:9 states 'those who were with me saw the light but did not hear the voice of the one who spoke to me' -- an apparent direct contradiction about whether the companions heard the voice, all three accounts appearing within the same book by the same author.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 9:7 and Acts 22:9 exactly as given in the Greek text, without harmonizing the apparent difference in the text itself; this is a matter of Greek grammatical and historical interpretation, not a matter the ten translations resolve differently from one another in their main text.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and actively debated question even among scholars committed to biblical harmonization, and this entry presents the range of positions candidly rather than asserting a single fully settled resolution. The most commonly offered grammatical solution notes that the Greek verb 'to hear' takes a different grammatical case for its object in each verse (the genitive case in 9:7, the accusative case in 22:9), a distinction classical Greek sometimes used to differentiate merely perceiving a sound (genitive) from hearing with full, articulate comprehension (accusative) -- on this reading, both statements are true simultaneously: the companions heard a sound (9:7) but did not comprehend the specific words being spoken (22:9), similar to an English speaker asking 'do you hear what I'm saying?' to mean 'do you understand,' rather than merely 'is sound reaching your ears.' This solution is widely repeated and defended, including by conservative commentators and, notably, in Watchtower Bible literature citing the same grammatical argument. However, a detailed translators' commentary on this exact verse concludes this specific grammatical solution is 'judged by most scholars as invalid,' since a close examination of Luke's own usage of the verb 'to hear' elsewhere throughout Acts shows he does not consistently or reliably distinguish the two grammatical cases along these lines -- meaning the case distinction, while grammatically possible in principle, may not reflect Luke's own deliberate intent at these two specific verses. Some scholars (including at least one directly quoted as regarding this as a genuine, real contradiction) read the differences among all three conversion accounts as reflecting Luke's own use of a recognized ancient historiographical convention -- narrating the same core event with some variation in specific detail across different retellings for different rhetorical purposes and audiences, a documented practice in ancient historical writing that did not carry the same expectation of verbatim consistency modern readers sometimes impose -- rather than as a claim that either specific account is factually mistaken. This project does not adjudicate definitively between the grammatical-resolution reading and the reading that treats this as a genuine, if minor, point of narrative variation, noting that serious scholars occupy positions across this entire range.

## Chronology

### 3. Paul's own account of his post-conversion movements (Galatians 1:15-18) vs. Acts' narrative sequence

**References:** Acts 9:19-26; Galatians 1:15-18

**The claim:** Acts 9:19-26 narrates Paul's activity immediately following his conversion in relatively compressed terms: he stays with the disciples in Damascus, immediately begins proclaiming Jesus in the synagogues there, and, after escaping a plot against his life, goes to Jerusalem. Paul's own first-person account in Galatians 1:15-18 describes a different, more specific sequence: after his conversion, he states he 'did not immediately consult with anyone... nor did I go up to Jerusalem,' but instead 'went away into Arabia, and again returned to Damascus,' only going up to Jerusalem 'after three years' -- a specific chronological detail (the journey to Arabia, and the three-year interval) that Acts' own narrative does not explicitly mention or account for.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 9:19-26 and Galatians 1:15-18 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading Acts' compressed narrative summary as compatible with, rather than excluding, Paul's own fuller chronological account, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this as a case of Acts' own compressed narrative summary being supplemented, rather than contradicted, by Paul's own fuller first-person chronological detail -- Acts 9:19-26's account does not explicitly state that Paul went to Jerusalem immediately after his initial time in Damascus, and the general statement that Paul 'was in Damascus with the disciples for some days' (9:19) is generally read as broad and flexible enough to accommodate the intervening Arabian sojourn and the full three-year period Paul's own letter specifies, without requiring Acts' own account to be corrected -- ancient historical narrative regularly compressed extended periods into brief transitional statements without intending to specify an exhaustive, exact timeline, a documented feature of ancient historiography addressed at several other points in this project. This reading holds that Acts and Galatians describe the same overall sequence of events at two different levels of chronological specificity (Acts' broader summary; Paul's own more precise personal account) rather than two incompatible timelines, since nothing in Acts' own wording explicitly excludes the additional detail Paul supplies.

### 4. The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) and Paul's own account of a Jerusalem visit (Galatians 2:1-10) -- the same meeting, or two different ones?

**References:** Acts 15:1-29; Galatians 2:1-10; Acts 11:27-30

**The claim:** Acts 15 records a formal council in Jerusalem, involving Paul, Barnabas, Peter, and James, addressing whether Gentile converts must be circumcised, resulting in a public decision and a written decree. Galatians 2:1-10 describes what reads as a closely similar meeting in Jerusalem, involving the same central figures and addressing the same core question, but Paul's own account describes it as a private meeting, calls his opponents 'false brothers' rather than the more measured 'some believers... who belonged to the party of the Pharisees' Acts 15:5 describes, and -- most significantly for the broader chronological question -- Paul's own accounting of his total visits to Jerusalem in Galatians (two, as of the time of writing) does not straightforwardly align with a face-value reading of Acts, which appears to describe three separate visits (the initial post-conversion visit, 9:26-30; a 'famine relief' visit, 11:27-30; and the Jerusalem Council itself, 15:1-29) by the time of the Council.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 15:1-29, Acts 11:27-30, and Galatians 2:1-10 plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the specific relationship between these passages in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and chronological interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most substantively debated chronological questions in New Testament scholarship, directly affecting how Paul's letters and Acts are correlated with one another, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. A substantial body of scholarship, including the prominent New Testament scholar Craig Keener, holds that Galatians 2:1-10 and Acts 15 describe the same single event, citing their shared core subject matter (Gentile circumcision), shared central participants, and substantially compatible outcomes, and reading the specific differences in tone and setting as reflecting each author's own selective emphasis (Paul, writing to make a specific rhetorical point about his independent apostolic authority, emphasizes the private, personal dimension of the meeting; Luke, writing a more public institutional history, emphasizes its more formal, public resolution) rather than describing two entirely separate events. Other scholars hold that Galatians 2:1-10 instead corresponds to the earlier 'famine relief' visit briefly described in Acts 11:27-30, with the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15 occurring later and going unmentioned in Galatians because it had not yet occurred at the time Paul wrote that letter (a position that requires dating Galatians earlier than commonly assumed, before the Acts 15 Council, a minority but reportedly growing view) -- this reading has the specific advantage of resolving the numerical mismatch between Paul's own count of two Jerusalem visits and Acts' apparent count of three, since on this view Galatians was written before the third visit (the Council itself) had yet taken place. This project does not adjudicate between these positions, noting that this question remains a genuinely live and actively studied one among serious New Testament scholars and directly affects the broader chronological reconstruction of Paul's ministry.

## Theological Tensions

### 5. The Jerusalem Council's decree on food offered to idols -- vs. Paul's own, seemingly more permissive teaching

**References:** Acts 15:19-20, 28-29; 1 Corinthians 8:1-13; 1 Corinthians 10:23-30

**The claim:** The Jerusalem Council's letter to Gentile believers specifies four requirements, including abstaining from 'what has been sacrificed to idols' (15:29) -- language that reads as an absolute, unconditional prohibition. Paul's own later teaching on the same subject, however, appears considerably more nuanced and permissive: in 1 Corinthians 8, Paul acknowledges 'an idol has no real existence' and that food itself is not defiled by having been offered to it, permitting believers with sufficiently mature conscience to eat such food freely so long as it does not cause a weaker believer to stumble; in 1 Corinthians 10:25-27, Paul explicitly tells believers they may eat 'whatever is sold in the meat market' or served at an unbeliever's table 'without raising any question on the ground of conscience,' unless someone specifically points out that it had been offered in sacrifice.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 15:19-20, 28-29 and Paul's teaching in 1 Corinthians 8 and 10 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing actual participation in pagan cultic worship from the separate question of ordinary meat consumption, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as addressing related but distinguishable specific situations rather than as directly contradictory rules. The Jerusalem decree is generally understood as addressing a specific, socially significant practice -- participation in pagan temple feasts and cultic meals themselves, where eating food offered to idols occurred within an explicitly idolatrous ritual context bound up with actual participation in pagan worship (a concern also reflected in Paul's own separate warning against 'the table of demons' and participating in pagan sacrificial worship as such, 1 Corinthians 10:14-22) -- while Paul's more permissive guidance in 1 Corinthians 8 and 10:25-30 addresses the separate, more mundane question of eating ordinary meat purchased in the marketplace or served at a private, non-cultic meal, which had likely originated from an animal sacrificed at a pagan temple as a normal feature of the ancient meat market, but which was not itself being consumed within any actual idolatrous ritual context. On this reading, Paul's own teaching does not contradict the Jerusalem decree but applies and refines its underlying concern (avoiding actual participation in pagan cultic worship) to specific everyday circumstances the decree's brief wording did not itself fully spell out, consistently prohibiting genuine cultic participation in both texts while allowing ordinary consumption of meat with an uncertain history in both.

### 6. Who raised Jesus from the dead -- God the Father, or Jesus himself?

**References:** Acts 2:24; John 10:17-18; Romans 8:11

**The claim:** Acts 2:24 states, 'whom God hath raised up,' attributing the resurrection to God (the Father). John 10:17-18 has Jesus say of his own life, 'I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again' -- describing the resurrection as his own act. Romans 8:11 attributes it to yet a third agent: 'the Spirit of him that raised up Jesus from the dead.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Consistent with the New Testament's broader pattern of describing the Trinity's external, unified works as accomplished by all three divine persons together (a pattern addressed elsewhere in this project regarding the Spirit's sending, John 15:26/14:26), the resurrection is presented throughout the New Testament as a single divine act in which Father, Son, and Spirit are each truly and appropriately said to act: the Father is most frequently credited as the one who raises the Son (the New Testament's dominant description, reflecting the Father's role in vindicating and exalting the Son), the Son is described as possessing his own inherent authority and power to take up his life again (reflecting his own full deity and life-giving power), and the Spirit is described as the one through whom this resurrection life and power was accomplished (reflecting the Spirit's role as the one who gives and empowers life throughout Scripture). These are complementary descriptions of the different persons' roles within one unified divine act, not three competing claims about a single, exclusive actor.

### 7. If a husband believes, is his wife automatically saved also?

**References:** Acts 16:31-34; Romans 10:9-10

**The claim:** Acts 16:31 has Paul tell the Philippian jailer, 'Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved, and thy house' -- language some read as promising the jailer's entire household salvation contingent only on his own individual faith. Elsewhere, Scripture consistently ties salvation to each individual's own personal faith and confession (Romans 10:9-10, 'if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart... thou shalt be saved').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The immediately following verses in the same narrative show this promise being fulfilled through the household's own individual faith, not apart from it: Paul and Silas 'spake unto him the word of the Lord, and to all that were in his house' (16:32), and the household is then described as having 'believed in God, with all his house' (16:34) -- the jailer's own household members are shown hearing the gospel message and coming to their own genuine faith, not merely being declared saved by association with the jailer's belief alone. Acts 16:31 is best read as a promise regarding the genuine availability and offer of salvation extending to the jailer's household (encouraging him that his own conversion opens the door for his family as well), a promise the narrative itself immediately shows being realized through the household's own subsequent, individual belief, fully consistent with the New Testament's consistent requirement of personal faith for salvation.

### 8. Can women serve as church leaders or teachers, given Priscilla's role in instructing Apollos?

**References:** Acts 18:24-26; 1 Timothy 2:12

**The claim:** Acts 18:26 describes Priscilla, alongside her husband Aquila, taking part in instructing Apollos, a learned and eloquent teacher, 'the way of God more perfectly.' 1 Timothy 2:12 states, 'I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question touches one of the most significant, actively contested practical and theological questions across contemporary Christian denominations regarding women's roles in church teaching and leadership, and this document does not adjudicate between the major positions held by serious, Scripture-affirming traditions. Complementarian traditions (holding that Scripture reserves the teaching/authoritative office within the gathered church specifically for qualified men) typically read Priscilla's role in Acts 18:26 as private, informal instruction of an individual outside the context of formal, authoritative church teaching that 1 Timothy 2:12 addresses -- Priscilla and Aquila together (with Aquila's own presence and participation noted) correct Apollos privately, not from an authoritative teaching office within a gathered congregation, a distinction this tradition holds resolves the apparent tension. Egalitarian traditions (holding that Scripture permits women to serve in the full range of teaching and leadership roles) instead read Priscilla's substantive theological instruction of a prominent male teacher as itself evidence that women exercised genuine teaching authority in the earliest church, and read 1 Timothy 2:12 as addressing a specific, local, and possibly temporary situation in Ephesus (the letter's specific destination) rather than establishing a universal, permanent restriction -- some further noting that Priscilla is listed before her husband in several New Testament references (including this one), a detail some read as reflecting her particular prominence in ministry. Both positions are held by serious, careful interpreters, and this document presents the question without resolving it.

### 9. In whose name is baptism to be performed -- Jesus's name alone, or the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit?

**References:** Acts 19:5; Acts 2:38; Matthew 28:19

**The claim:** Acts 19:5 (as well as Acts 2:38 and other passages in Acts) describes baptism performed 'in the name of the Lord Jesus,' mentioning Jesus alone. Matthew 28:19 instead records Jesus's own explicit instruction to baptize 'in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost' -- a full Trinitarian formula.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The majority reading among mainstream Christian traditions across history holds that the phrases in Acts describing baptism 'in the name of Jesus' are summarizing the essential, distinctively Christian character and content of the baptism -- identifying it as baptism into allegiance to and union with Christ specifically, as opposed to, for instance, John's earlier baptism of repentance (the specific point of contrast in Acts 19:1-5's immediate context, involving disciples who had previously received only John's baptism) -- rather than prescribing the exact liturgical wording to be recited at each baptism in place of the explicit formula Jesus himself gives in Matthew 28:19. On this reading, Matthew 28:19 supplies the actual baptismal formula the church has historically used, while Acts' shorter references summarize the substance and Christian character of what baptism accomplishes, without the two texts being in competition. A minority position, held by some 'Oneness' Pentecostal and related traditions, instead holds that the wording in Acts reflects the authentic apostolic practice and formula, and that baptism should be performed using the specific words 'in the name of Jesus' rather than the traditional Trinitarian formula -- a position also connected to this tradition's broader, non-Trinitarian theology regarding the relationship between the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This document notes the practical, denominationally significant nature of this disagreement without adjudicating it, while observing that the overwhelming majority of the church across history and denominational lines has read the two sets of texts as compatible rather than competing, using Matthew 28:19's fuller formula liturgically while affirming that Acts' shorter references describe the same baptism under a different, summarizing description.

## Names and Identity

### 10. 'Herod' -- at least four distinct rulers across the New Testament sharing the same family name

**References:** Acts 12:1-23; Matthew 2:1-19; Luke 3:1; Acts 25:13-26:32

**The claim:** The New Testament refers to several different members of the Herodian dynasty simply as 'Herod,' without always distinguishing which specific ruler is meant: Herod the Great, who ordered the slaughter of the infants at Bethlehem (Matthew 2:1-19); his son Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee, who executed John the Baptist and interrogated Jesus before the crucifixion (Luke 3:1, 23:6-12); Herod Agrippa I, grandson of Herod the Great, who executed the apostle James and imprisoned Peter (Acts 12:1-23); and Herod Agrippa II, son of Agrippa I, before whom Paul later makes his defense (Acts 25:13-26:32) -- four distinct historical individuals across roughly a century, connected by dynasty and by the frequent, unqualified use of the single family name 'Herod' for each.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each passage's use of 'Herod' exactly as given, without adding further distinguishing titles beyond what the underlying Greek text itself supplies; this is a matter of historical identification, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a genuine and potentially confusing feature of New Testament nomenclature worth clarifying directly, similar to comparable name-disambiguation questions addressed at several other points in this project. Each specific individual is independently and securely attested in extra-biblical historical sources (principally Josephus), with a clear, well-documented genealogical and chronological relationship among them (Herod the Great fathered Antipas; Antipas's brother Aristobulus fathered Agrippa I; Agrippa I fathered Agrippa II), allowing modern readers to distinguish each 'Herod' by the specific context, associated events, and other named individuals in each passage, even where the biblical text itself does not always supply a fuller distinguishing title. This is a documented feature of the historical situation itself (a single politically dominant family holding power across multiple generations, with Rome favoring continuity of dynastic rule in the region) rather than any inconsistency in the New Testament's own reporting.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 11. Herod Agrippa I's death -- independently corroborated by Josephus's own historical account

**References:** Acts 12:20-23

**The claim:** Acts 12:20-23 describes Herod Agrippa I's sudden death: after accepting a crowd's flattering acclamation that his voice was 'the voice of a god, and not of a man,' 'immediately an angel of the Lord struck him down, because he did not give God the glory, and he was eaten by worms and breathed his last.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 12:20-23 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a matter of historical corroboration, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but is included here, similar to several other cases of independent historical corroboration addressed elsewhere in this project (Belshazzar's status in the companion Daniel document, the Lysanias identification in the companion Luke document), as a notable point where Acts' own account is independently and substantially corroborated by extra-biblical history. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing independently of any Christian source and with no theological motive to confirm the biblical account, separately records Agrippa I's death in strikingly similar terms (Antiquities of the Jews 19.343-350): during a public festival in Caesarea, the assembled crowd flatteringly hailed Agrippa as a god, he failed to rebuke them, was immediately seized with severe abdominal pain, and died several days later -- a substantially matching account of both the specific occasion (public acclamation as divine) and the general nature of his sudden, agonizing death, from an entirely independent, non-Christian historical source.

## Numbers and Text

### 12. The number of men in Paul's arresting escort -- and other minor numerical details across Acts' narrative

**References:** Acts 23:23

**The claim:** Acts 23:23 records the Roman commander Claudius Lysias dispatching an unusually large security detachment to protect Paul en route to Caesarea: 'two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen, and two hundred spearmen' -- a total of 470 armed men escorting a single prisoner, a detail some have questioned as implausibly large for the stated purpose.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 23:23 exactly as given; this entry addresses a question of historical plausibility, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not treat this as an error, noting that the specific, unusually large number is entirely consistent with the surrounding narrative's own stated reason for such an extensive escort: Lysias had just learned of a specific, deadly conspiracy involving more than forty men who had bound themselves by oath to ambush and kill Paul before he could be safely transferred (23:12-15, 21) -- a large, organized plot Lysias, as the responsible Roman officer, would have had strong institutional motive to overwhelm decisively with a correspondingly large show of force, particularly given the political sensitivity of allowing a Roman citizen (which Paul was, 22:25-29) to be murdered while in Roman custody. This is generally read as a plausible, well-motivated response to a specific, serious threat rather than an implausible exaggeration, and Luke's own narrative provides the specific context that makes the number's scale readily explicable.

### 13. How many years did the Israelites serve foreign rulers before the Exodus, per Stephen's speech and Exodus's own numbers?

**References:** Acts 7:6; Genesis 15:13; Exodus 12:40-41; Galatians 3:17

**The claim:** In his speech before the Sanhedrin, Stephen states that God told Abraham his descendants would be 'sojourners in a land belonging to others, who will enslave them and afflict them four hundred years' (Acts 7:6, closely echoing Genesis 15:13). Exodus 12:40-41 instead gives a total of 430 years for 'the time that the people of Israel lived in Egypt.' Paul, in Galatians 3:17, uses the 430-year figure but applies it to the span between God's promise to Abraham and the giving of the Law at Sinai -- a different starting point than the descent into Egypt itself. This is the same underlying textual question addressed at greater length in the companion Exodus document (entry 12), here specifically in the form Stephen's speech gives it.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 7:6, Genesis 15:13, Exodus 12:40-41, and Galatians 3:17 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of historical reconstruction addressed at length in the companion Exodus document, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As discussed more fully in the Exodus document's own treatment of this question, the standard harmonization holds that the 400-year figure (Genesis 15:13; Acts 7:6) and the 430-year figure (Exodus 12:40-41; Galatians 3:17) are not strictly interchangeable totals for the same span, and ancient Jewish tradition itself (reflected in the Septuagint's expanded wording at Exodus 12:40, in Josephus, and in rabbinic sources) generally understood 430 years to run from Abraham's original call and covenant to the Exodus (including the patriarchal period in Canaan before the family's descent into Egypt), while treating 400 years as a rounded figure specifically for the sojourn and bondage within Egypt itself, a shorter span nested within the longer 430-year total. On this reading, Stephen (citing Genesis 15:13's 400-year figure for time within Egypt) and Paul (citing the 430-year total from the promise to the Law) are both using figures that were already conventionally understood and distinguished in first-century Jewish exegesis, rather than contradicting either Exodus or each other.

### 14. 'It is more blessed to give than to receive' -- a saying of Jesus not found in the Gospels

**References:** Acts 20:35

**The claim:** In his farewell address to the Ephesian elders, Paul quotes Jesus directly: 'remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he said, It is more blessed to give than to receive.' This precise saying, however, does not appear anywhere in the four canonical Gospels, raising the question of its source and reliability.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Acts 20:35 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of the saying's historical sourcing, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, since none of the Gospels claims to record every word Jesus spoke -- John's Gospel explicitly states the opposite: 'there are also many other things that Jesus did; were every one of them to be written, I suppose that the world itself could not contain the books that would be written' (John 21:25). Scholars use the technical term agrapha ('unwritten things') for authentic sayings of Jesus preserved outside the four Gospels, of which this is the clearest New Testament example; Paul, writing to an audience (the Ephesian elders) who could presumably confirm or challenge his citation, presents it as a known, authoritative saying rather than something he invented for the occasion. Since Luke, the author of Acts, was also the author of the Gospel of Luke, this passage is sometimes cited as evidence against the idea that the Gospel writers felt free to invent material and attribute it to Jesus -- if they had, this saying would more naturally have appeared within the Gospel of Luke itself, at one of the several points where Jesus teaches on giving and generosity (e.g., Luke 6:38), rather than being reserved for a citation elsewhere in Luke's own second volume.
