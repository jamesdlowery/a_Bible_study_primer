# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 Corinthians

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 2 Corinthians,
either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with 1 Corinthians, Galatians,
Deuteronomy, and the companion Luke document. For each item, this document states the claim as
it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim
appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 1 catalogs the wide range of
proposals for the identity of Paul's famous "thorn in the flesh," one of the most genuinely
unresolved ambiguities in the entire Pauline corpus. Entry 5 addresses a compositional question
comparable in kind to 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 (addressed in its own companion document), though
resting on internal literary evidence (including a specific, influential connection to the Dead
Sea Scrolls) rather than manuscript variation.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1 and 5 are presented with genuine scholarly diversity of opinion
rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Numbers and Text

### 1. Paul's 'thorn in the flesh' -- one of the most famous unresolved ambiguities in Paul's letters

**References:** 2 Corinthians 12:7-10

**The claim:** 2 Corinthians 12:7 describes 'a thorn... in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to harass me' given to Paul specifically to keep him from becoming conceited after his extraordinary spiritual experience (addressed in entry 3 below). Paul never identifies what this 'thorn' actually was, and the resulting ambiguity has generated centuries of scholarly speculation without any single proposal commanding consensus.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Corinthians 12:7's 'thorn in the flesh' consistently, without further identifying its specific nature in the text itself; the identity of the thorn remains genuinely unresolved regardless of translation, and is not settled by any of the ten translations' specific wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a genuinely and famously unresolved ambiguity Paul himself apparently felt no need to clarify for his original readers, who evidently already knew what he meant, even though later readers do not. This entry catalogs the range of leading proposals rather than endorsing one. Proposed identifications fall into three broad categories. The first is physical ailments: a chronic eye condition, sometimes connected to Galatians 4:13-15 and 6:11's references to Paul's own eyesight and unusually large handwriting; epilepsy; malaria; severe migraines or headaches, a view held by the early church fathers Tertullian and Jerome; and a speech impediment, connected by some to the Corinthians' own criticism of Paul's unimpressive public speaking (2 Corinthians 10:10, 1 Corinthians 2:3). The second is psychological or spiritual struggles: recurring temptation, a view held by Martin Luther, John Calvin, and other early Reformers, and, in a minority and much-disputed modern proposal, suppressed sexual temptation. The third is external opposition: Paul's own persistent human opponents or persecutors, a reading with some support from the Greek Septuagint's use of similar 'thorn' imagery elsewhere for national enemies (Numbers 33:55), and championed in the early church by John Chrysostom, who connected the 'messenger of Satan' language directly to Paul's own repeated conflicts with rivals and persecutors. This project does not adjudicate between these proposals, consistent with how genuinely unresolved biblical questions have been treated throughout this series. The term Paul uses for 'thorn' (skolops) more literally suggests a sharp stake or splinter causing ongoing, piercing pain -- an image compatible with several of the above proposals without decisively favoring any single one.

### 2. 'Forty lashes less one' -- a documented safety margin below, not a departure from, the Torah's stated maximum

**References:** 2 Corinthians 11:24; Deuteronomy 25:1-3

**The claim:** 2 Corinthians 11:24, part of Paul's catalog of his own sufferings, states, 'Five times I received at the hands of the Jews the forty lashes less one' -- a specific, seemingly odd figure of thirty-nine, rather than the full forty stripes Deuteronomy 25:3 permits as a maximum punishment ('forty stripes he may give him and no more').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Corinthians 11:24's 'forty lashes less one' (or 'thirty-nine lashes') consistently; this entry addresses a matter of historical corroboration, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction, but reflects a well-documented Second Temple and later rabbinic Jewish practice of deliberately administering thirty-nine, rather than the full forty, stripes specifically as a safety margin to avoid any risk of accidentally exceeding Deuteronomy's own stated maximum through a miscount during the actual administration of the punishment -- Deuteronomy 25:3 explicitly forbids exceeding forty stripes, and Jewish legal tradition (reflected later in the Mishnah, tractate Makkot) responded to this explicit limit by capping the actual practice one stripe below the maximum as a built-in buffer against inadvertent violation, rather than as any departure from or contradiction of the Torah's own stated command. Paul's specific figure, far from representing an inconsistency, is therefore a precise, historically corroborated detail confirming his description's accuracy regarding an actual, documented first-century Jewish legal practice.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 3. 'A man in Christ... caught up to the third heaven' -- Paul's own mysterious, third-person account of his ecstatic experience

**References:** 2 Corinthians 12:1-6

**The claim:** 2 Corinthians 12:2-4 describes a remarkable experience -- being 'caught up to the third heaven,' into 'paradise,' hearing 'things that cannot be told, which man may not utter' -- in a deliberately distanced, third-person voice ('I know a man in Christ who fourteen years ago was caught up...'), before Paul's own surrounding argument makes clear this experience was in fact his own (12:7 immediately connects the 'thorn in the flesh,' addressed in entry 1 above, to 'the surpassing greatness of the revelations' just described). This raises questions about both Paul's unusual rhetorical choice to describe his own experience in the third person, and the passage's apparent assumption of a multi-layered cosmology ('the third heaven') without further explanation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Corinthians 12:1-6 plainly and consistently as given, preserving Paul's own third-person framing; this entry addresses a matter of rhetorical device and ancient cosmology, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a deliberate rhetorical device, widely recognized by commentators as consistent with the surrounding context's own explicit theme -- Paul has just spent an extended, reluctant section (11:16-33) emphasizing that he only boasts about his weaknesses and sufferings, not his spiritual privileges, and the third-person distancing in 12:1-6 is generally read as Paul's own deliberate, self-conscious effort to minimize personal glory even while relating a genuinely extraordinary experience he feels compelled to mention to counter rival teachers' own competing claims to spiritual authority in Corinth -- speaking of 'a man in Christ' rather than boasting directly in his own name and person. On the cosmological question, Paul's reference to 'the third heaven' is generally read as reflecting a documented feature of Second Temple Jewish cosmology and apocalyptic literature, which commonly described multiple heavens (proposals in various ancient Jewish texts range from three to seven), without this being a point of doctrinal significance Paul develops further -- he uses a cosmological framework familiar to his contemporary Jewish audience to describe the ecstatic, revelatory nature of his experience, rather than making a precise, systematic theological claim about the exact structure of the heavens that later readers are meant to develop into fixed doctrine.

### 4. Paul's stated aversion to self-commendation -- alongside his own extensive self-defense

**References:** 2 Corinthians 3:1; 2 Corinthians 10:1-13:10

**The claim:** 2 Corinthians 3:1 asks pointedly, 'Are we beginning to commend ourselves again? Or do we need, as some do, letters of recommendation to you, or from you?' -- a rhetorical question implying such self-commendation is inappropriate or unnecessary. Yet much of this same letter's final four chapters (10-13, sometimes called Paul's 'Fool's Speech') consists of an extended, detailed defense of Paul's own apostolic credentials, sufferings, and spiritual experiences, explicitly responding to rival teachers in Corinth (the 'super-apostles,' 11:5, 12:11) who had evidently questioned Paul's own authority and standing.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Corinthians 3:1 and chapters 10-13 plainly and consistently as given, preserving Paul's own repeated, explicit acknowledgment of the tension; this entry addresses a deliberate rhetorical strategy Paul himself flags directly, not an unnoticed contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a tension Paul himself explicitly and repeatedly acknowledges and addresses directly within the very passage in question, rather than one requiring outside harmonization -- Paul introduces his own extended self-defense in chapters 10-13 with explicit, repeated apologies for engaging in exactly the kind of self-commendation he elsewhere criticizes: 'I have been a fool! You forced me to it' (12:11), 'let no one think me foolish... but if you do, accept me as a fool, so that I too may boast a little' (11:16), and 'I have been talking like a fool' (11:21) -- Paul frames his entire extended self-defense as a reluctant, situationally necessary departure from his own normal practice and preference, forced upon him specifically because the Corinthians' own susceptibility to rival teachers' competing, self-promoting claims left him no better option for defending the genuine gospel he had originally brought them. This self-aware, repeatedly flagged irony is widely read by commentators as Paul's own deliberate rhetorical strategy, not an unnoticed inconsistency -- he draws his readers' attention to the awkwardness of his own self-defense precisely to underscore how absurd and regrettable he considers the entire situation that has forced it upon him.

## Literary Composition

### 5. 'Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers' -- a passage some scholars regard as a non-Pauline interpolation

**References:** 2 Corinthians 6:14-7:1

**The claim:** 2 Corinthians 6:14-7:1 ('Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers... Come out from among them, and be separate, says the Lord... let us cleanse ourselves from every defilement of body and spirit') has been the subject of over a century of scholarly controversy regarding whether Paul actually wrote it, comparable in kind (though with a different specific textual basis) to the questions addressed regarding 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 in the companion 1 Corinthians document.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Corinthians 6:14-7:1 in its standard location within the letter, without marking it as a separate insertion in the text itself; this is a matter of compositional and literary interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Unlike the 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 case, this passage's authenticity is not questioned on manuscript grounds (no surviving Greek manuscript of 2 Corinthians omits it or places it elsewhere) but on internal, compositional grounds, and this entry presents the debate candidly rather than a single certain answer. Many scholars, beginning influentially with a widely cited 1961 article by the Catholic biblical scholar Joseph Fitzmyer connecting this passage's specific vocabulary and themes to the Dead Sea Scrolls' own sectarian literature from Qumran (particularly its stark language of separation between light and darkness, righteousness and lawlessness, and its concern for ritual purity), have proposed that this paragraph originated as a separate, non-Pauline (or at least non-2-Corinthians) fragment -- possibly connected to the otherwise lost earlier letter Paul references in 1 Corinthians 5:9 -- that was inserted at this point by a later editor, noting the passage's abrupt interruption of Paul's own surrounding, continuous appeal for reconciliation with the Corinthians (which reads smoothly if this passage is removed, flowing directly from 6:13 to 7:2) and its use of several terms found nowhere else in Paul's authenticated letters. Other serious scholars defend the passage's authenticity and its placement here, arguing Paul's own letters elsewhere show a documented tendency toward sudden digressions before returning to his main argument (a pattern this project has addressed regarding Ecclesiastes and other biblical books' compositional structure at several points), and that the passage's specific concerns (maintaining appropriate distinction from pagan practices) fit naturally within Paul's broader argument in this section of the letter. This project does not adjudicate this question, noting it remains a live matter of ongoing academic discussion.

## Theological Interpretation

### 6. 'Away from the body and at home with the Lord' -- Paul's own teaching on the intermediate state

**References:** 2 Corinthians 5:1-10

**The claim:** 2 Corinthians 5:1-10 offers one of Paul's clearest statements on what happens between a believer's death and final bodily resurrection, describing a preference to be 'away from the body and at home with the Lord' (5:8) -- connecting to the broader question of the intermediate state addressed at several points elsewhere in this project, including regarding Luke 23:43's 'today you will be with me in paradise' and its relationship to John 20:17.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Corinthians 5:1-10 plainly and consistently as given; this entry connects to the broader intermediate-state discussion addressed at other points in this project, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This passage is generally read as Paul's own clearest affirmation that a believer's conscious fellowship with Christ begins immediately upon death, prior to and distinct from the future, final bodily resurrection his other letters describe at length (1 Corinthians 15, 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18) -- 'away from the body' describing this intermediate, disembodied (or, in some theological traditions, provisionally embodied) state of conscious presence with Christ, to be followed later by the full, final bodily resurrection at Christ's own return. This is generally read as consistent with, rather than contradicting, the other biblical statements addressed elsewhere in this project regarding the nature of the period between an individual's death and the final resurrection, with this passage offering one of the New Testament's clearest and most personal statements of a believer's confident hope for that intermediate period specifically.

## Theological Tensions

### 7. Who blinds unbelievers' minds -- Satan, or God himself?

**References:** 2 Corinthians 4:3-4; Romans 11:7-8; Isaiah 6:9-10

**The claim:** 2 Corinthians 4:3-4 attributes unbelievers' spiritual blindness to 'the god of this world,' Satan, who 'hath blinded the minds of them which believe not.' Elsewhere, Scripture attributes a similar blindness or hardening directly to God: Romans 11:7-8 states 'the rest were blinded,' quoting that 'God hath given them the spirit of slumber,' and Isaiah 6:9-10 has God himself commission the blinding of the people's eyes and hearts.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This reflects the same layered pattern of causation addressed at several other points in this project (regarding Pharaoh's hardened heart, and the 'lying spirit' sent to Ahab's prophets): Satan is presented as the active, proximate agent energetically working to blind and deceive unbelievers, exploiting and reinforcing their unbelief as part of his ongoing opposition to the gospel -- the specific, immediate cause 2 Corinthians 4:4 describes. At a deeper level, Scripture also presents God as exercising a sovereign, judicial confirmation of blindness upon those who have already persistently and willfully rejected available truth (the consistent pattern in both Romans 11:7-8's and Isaiah 6:9-10's contexts, addressing Israel's own repeated, prior rejection of God's revealed word) -- a judgment that hands a person over to the very blindness they have already chosen, rather than an arbitrary imposition on someone otherwise inclined to believe. Both descriptions can be true simultaneously and at different levels: Satan actively blinds as the immediate agent of ongoing deception, while God, in his sovereign governance of history, judicially confirms and hardens those already given over to persistent unbelief -- complementary rather than competing accounts of the same spiritual reality.
