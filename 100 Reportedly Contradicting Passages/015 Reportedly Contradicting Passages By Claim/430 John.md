# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in John

**A note on this document's place in the series:** This document completes this project's
coverage of the four canonical Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John).

This document catalogs 21 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the Gospel of
John, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with the Synoptic Gospels
(Matthew, Mark, Luke) and the companion Malachi document. For each item, this document states
the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this Gospel's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses the second most
significant textual-critical question in the New Testament (after Mark's ending, addressed in
the companion Mark document) -- the story of the woman caught in adultery, whose manuscript
history is, if anything, even more unsettled than Mark 16:9-20's, given its remarkable mobility
across different locations in different manuscript traditions. Entry 2 addresses one of the
most significant chronological tensions between John and the Synoptics regarding the Last
Supper's relationship to Passover.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7 are all presented with genuine scholarly
diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Textual History

### 1. The woman caught in adultery (7:53-8:11) -- the second most significant textual-critical question in the New Testament

**References:** John 7:53-8:11

**The claim:** The familiar story of Jesus and the woman caught in adultery ('let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her') is absent from the earliest and best manuscripts of John's Gospel, including the earliest papyri containing this section (P66 and P75) and the great fourth-century codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, all of which move directly from 7:52 to 8:12 with no gap. Where the passage does appear in later manuscripts, it appears in strikingly different locations -- most commonly here, but also after John 7:36, after John 7:44, at the very end of John's Gospel, and, in an entire family of manuscripts, after Luke 21:38 instead of anywhere in John at all.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, LSB, RSV2CE:** Every modern translation surveyed includes some form of explanatory note regarding the manuscript evidence for this passage, reflecting the same pattern addressed regarding Mark's ending in the companion Mark document, though the scholarly consensus against this passage's originality to John (while its underlying historicity as an independent tradition is often affirmed separately) is somewhat more one-sided than the ongoing division addressed regarding Mark 16:9-20.
- **CSB:** CSB includes the passage with a similar explanatory footnote regarding the earliest manuscripts.
- **ESV:** ESV includes the passage set off with a note explaining 'the earliest manuscripts do not include 7:53-8:11.'
- **KJV:** KJV includes John 7:53-8:11 within the main numbered text without any bracketing or footnote regarding the manuscript question.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also includes the passage in the main text, with a footnote noting several manuscripts do not contain 7:53-8:11 or add it elsewhere.
- **NASB:** NASB includes the passage in double brackets, with an extended footnote on the manuscript evidence.
- **NIV:** NIV includes the passage with a note stating 'the earliest manuscripts... do not have John 7:53-8:11.'
- **NLT:** NLT includes the passage with a footnote on the earliest manuscripts' omission.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated with a level of scholarly consensus somewhat more one-sided than the comparable case addressed in the companion Mark document regarding Mark 16:9-20, though the underlying situation is structurally similar and, if anything, even more textually unsettled given the passage's remarkable mobility across different manuscript traditions. The great majority of textual scholars across the theological spectrum, including many with a high view of the passage's own value and even its historical plausibility, conclude the passage was not originally part of John's Gospel. Their reasons include its absence from the earliest papyri and codices; its complete absence from the writings of the early Greek-speaking church fathers (no Eastern father comments on this passage until the twelfth century); a marked shift in vocabulary and style from the rest of John; and the passage's own extraordinary textual instability, appearing in multiple different locations across different manuscript families. Textual critics generally read this last pattern as evidence of a well-loved floating tradition being inserted at different points by different scribal traditions who felt strongly that so memorable a story deserved to be preserved somewhere in the Gospels, rather than evidence of an original passage whose location was simply forgotten. A significant number of these same scholars, including advocates for the passage's exclusion from John's original text, nonetheless conclude the underlying story itself likely reflects an authentic, independently circulating early tradition about Jesus with a reasonable claim to historical plausibility, even if it did not originally belong in this specific location in this specific Gospel. Some express this by comparing the passage's own character -- Jesus responding to a legalistic trap with a memorable act, writing on the ground, and a pointed rejoinder -- as consistent with, even if not originally recorded within, the wider Gospel tradition.

## Chronology

### 2. Was the Last Supper a Passover meal, or did it occur the evening before Passover began?

**References:** John 13:1-2; John 18:28; John 19:14, 31; Mark 14:12; Matthew 26:17; Luke 22:7-8

**The claim:** All three Synoptic Gospels explicitly and repeatedly identify the Last Supper as the Passover meal itself, prepared 'on the first day of Unleavened Bread, when they sacrificed the Passover lamb' (Mark 14:12; similarly Matthew 26:17, Luke 22:7-8). John's Gospel, however, opens its account of the same meal 'before the Feast of the Passover' (13:1), and later states that when the Jewish authorities brought Jesus to Pilate the following morning, they themselves refused to enter the governor's headquarters 'so that they would not be defiled, but could eat the Passover' (18:28) -- implying the Passover meal itself still lay ahead of them at that point -- and John further describes the day of the crucifixion itself as 'the day of Preparation of the Passover' (19:14), with the bodies needing removal before the Sabbath 'for that Sabbath was a high day' (19:31), consistently suggesting Jesus was crucified before, not after, the Passover meal was eaten -- one of the most significant chronological tensions between John and the Synoptics addressed anywhere in New Testament scholarship.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render John's specific time markers (13:1, 18:28, 19:14, 31) and the Synoptics' explicit Passover-meal identifications exactly as given in each Gospel, without harmonizing the apparent discrepancy in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and calendrical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and extensively debated question, and this entry presents the range of proposed solutions rather than a single certain answer, several of which are actively defended by serious scholars today. Some commentators propose that John's references to 'the Passover' in 18:28 and 19:14 refer not to the Passover meal itself, but to the broader week-long festival of Unleavened Bread and its associated meals and offerings (particularly the chagigah, or festival peace-offering, eaten the following morning per Numbers 28:18-19) -- on this reading, 'eating the Passover' in 18:28 refers to this broader festival participation, not the specific Seder meal already eaten the previous evening, removing the apparent contradiction by reading 'Passover' in its wider, attested sense rather than its narrowest one. Others propose that different Jewish groups in first-century Judea followed different calendrical reckonings (some evidence exists for at least two coexisting methods of dating Passover in this period), meaning the Synoptics and John may each accurately reflect a different group's own calendar, with Jesus and his disciples observing Passover according to one reckoning while the Temple authorities observed the same feast a day later according to another. Still others read the tension as more difficult to fully resolve, and note that some scholars, including several who hold a high view of the Gospels' historical reliability, conclude John may be using the Last Supper and crucifixion's close proximity to the Passover lambs' slaughter for a deliberate theological purpose -- emphasizing Jesus' own identity as 'the Lamb of God' (John 1:29, 36) dying at the very hour the Passover lambs were being sacrificed, a theologically resonant detail this Gospel alone develops at length -- without this necessarily requiring every one of John's specific time markers to be read as a strictly independent, corrected chronological claim overriding the Synoptics' own explicit and repeated identification of the meal as Passover itself. This project does not adjudicate between these proposed solutions, several of which remain actively defended and debated among serious scholars.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 3. The crucifixion's timing: John's 'about the sixth hour' -- addressed here from John's own specific vantage point

**References:** John 19:14; Mark 15:25

**The claim:** John 19:14 states Pilate presented Jesus for judgment 'about the sixth hour' -- meaning the crucifixion itself, following this scene, could not have begun before roughly this time. Mark 15:25, addressed from Mark's own vantage point in the companion Mark document, states plainly that 'it was the third hour when they crucified him,' several hours earlier by a same-system reading.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'about the sixth hour' at John 19:14 exactly as given; see the companion Mark document for the fuller discussion of this question, including the specific manuscript variant bearing on it.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Mark document, which addresses the leading proposed harmonizations: that Mark and John may use two different ancient time-reckoning conventions (Jewish reckoning from sunrise versus Roman civil reckoning from midnight), narrowing though not eliminating the gap; that one of the two specific numerals may reflect an early manuscript transmission error, a possibility with some limited textual support at John 19:14 itself, where a small number of ancient manuscripts and versions read 'third hour' rather than 'sixth'; or that both figures are best read as approximate, rounded indications of time rather than requiring precise, mutually exclusive accuracy. This project does not adjudicate between these proposed solutions.

### 4. The Temple cleansing: at the beginning of Jesus' ministry, or its very end?

**References:** John 2:13-22; Matthew 21:12-13; Mark 11:15-17; Luke 19:45-46

**The claim:** John's Gospel places Jesus' dramatic cleansing of the Jerusalem Temple -- overturning the money-changers' tables and driving out the sellers of sacrificial animals -- near the very beginning of his public ministry, immediately following the wedding at Cana (2:13-22). All three Synoptic Gospels instead place what reads as the same or a very similar event during the final week of Jesus' life, immediately following the Triumphal Entry, directly precipitating the chief priests' and scribes' decision to have him killed (Matthew 21:12-13, Mark 11:15-17, Luke 19:45-46).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render John 2:13-22 and each Synoptic account of the later Temple cleansing plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the two into a single timeline in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and literary interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated question, and this entry presents the two leading positions rather than adjudicating between them. Some commentators hold this represents a single historical event that either John or the Synoptics have relocated within their own narrative for topical or theological reasons rather than strict chronological ones -- a documented feature of ancient historical and biographical writing addressed at several other points in this project (regarding Matthew's own generally topical, rather than strictly chronological, arrangement of certain material) -- with John's own Gospel widely recognized by scholars as organized more thematically than chronologically at several points, potentially placing this dramatic, symbolically loaded episode early in his narrative to establish a key theological theme (Jesus' authority over the Temple and its practices) from the outset, rather than because it necessarily occurred there in strict chronological sequence. Other commentators, noting substantial differences in the specific details, wording, and immediate narrative context between John's version and the Synoptics' (including that John's account includes a distinctive saying about destroying and rebuilding 'this temple' in three days, 2:19-21, absent from the Synoptics' own accounts of the later cleansing), hold that Jesus more plausibly performed this same kind of symbolic, prophetic action on two separate occasions -- once early in his ministry as an opening, programmatic statement, and again during his final week as a climactic, more directly confrontational repetition -- a reading some find further supported by the observation that money-changing and animal-selling practices, once disrupted, would naturally have resumed by the time of a later Passover visit, making a second such cleansing plausible on straightforward historical grounds. This project does not adjudicate between these two positions.

### 5. John the Baptist's own denial of being Elijah -- the primary source for the tension addressed in the companion Malachi document

**References:** John 1:19-23; Malachi 4:5; Matthew 11:14; Matthew 17:10-13

**The claim:** John 1:21 records the direct, unqualified denial at the heart of the question addressed at length in this project's companion Malachi document: when religious authorities ask John the Baptist, 'Are you Elijah?' his own recorded answer is 'I am not' -- standing in apparent tension with Jesus' own later, explicit identification of John with the promised return of Elijah (Matthew 11:14, 17:10-13).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render John 1:19-23 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Malachi document for the fuller discussion of this question's relationship to Jesus' own later statements about John.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Malachi document, which addresses the standard resolution: John's own denial (recorded here, in his Gospel's primary source) truthfully answers a question about literal personal identity (John was not the ninth-century-BC prophet Elijah returned to life), while Jesus' later statements affirm John's typological, functional fulfillment of Elijah's anticipated prophetic role and ministry -- a distinction the New Testament itself states explicitly, prior to either John's denial or Jesus' affirmation, at Luke 1:17, where the angel Gabriel announces John's coming ministry specifically 'in the spirit and power of Elijah,' rather than as Elijah's own literal return.

### 6. The raising of Lazarus -- a major, dramatic miracle unique to John, absent from all three Synoptic Gospels

**References:** John 11:1-46

**The claim:** John 11 narrates an extended, dramatic account of Jesus raising Lazarus from the dead after four days in the tomb -- an event John's own narrative presents as directly and immediately precipitating the formal decision by the chief priests and Pharisees to have Jesus killed (11:45-53), making it, on John's own account, one of the most consequential events of Jesus' entire ministry. Yet none of the three Synoptic Gospels mentions this event, or Lazarus, anywhere at all, despite its apparently central narrative and historical significance.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render John 11:1-46 plainly and consistently as given; the Synoptic Gospels' own silence regarding this event is a matter of each Gospel's own selective inclusion of material, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that all four Gospels are demonstrably selective in what they choose to include from a much larger body of available tradition regarding Jesus' ministry (a point John's own Gospel makes explicitly regarding itself, 20:30, 21:25, stating that 'Jesus did many other things' beyond what any single Gospel records), meaning the Synoptics' silence about a specific event does not constitute a denial that it occurred, a principle this project has applied at several other points regarding differing Gospel content (most directly, the companion Matthew document's discussion of material unique to that Gospel, such as 'on this rock I will build my church'). Some scholars additionally propose that the Synoptic Gospels' own general geographic and thematic focus (concentrated more heavily on Jesus' Galilean ministry, with a comparatively compressed account of his Judean and Jerusalem-based activity, which is where John situates the Lazarus narrative, in nearby Bethany) may account for their comparative silence regarding an event occurring within this less-emphasized portion of Jesus' overall ministry from their own narrative perspective, rather than requiring any explanation involving doubt about the event's historicity. This project does not treat the Synoptics' silence as itself evidence against the event's occurrence, consistent with the general principle that an author's silence about an episode is not equivalent to a denial that it took place.

### 7. Is Jesus's testimony about himself true, or not?

**References:** John 5:31; John 8:14, 18

**The claim:** John 5:31 has Jesus say, 'If I bear witness of myself, my witness is not true.' Yet John 8:14 has Jesus say the apparent opposite: 'Though I bear record of myself, yet my record is true.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two statements address different aspects of the same underlying legal and evidentiary framework rather than making flatly contradictory metaphysical claims: John 5:31 is best read as a conditional, rhetorical concession made within the specific terms of Jewish legal practice, which required corroborating testimony from more than one witness (a requirement Jesus explicitly addresses in the immediately following verses, 5:32-39, citing John the Baptist, his own works, and the Father's testimony through Scripture as corroborating witnesses) -- Jesus's point is that self-testimony alone, unsupported by other witnesses, would not meet the legal bar his opponents themselves would require, not that his testimony is inherently or actually false. John 8:14 asserts the actual, objective truth of his testimony regardless of that legal-evidentiary technicality, explicitly grounding this claim in his own unique knowledge of his origin and destiny ('I know whence I came, and whither I go') that his human opponents lack. Addressing legal sufficiency by his opponents' own standards (5:31) and asserting the actual truth of his testimony (8:14) are compatible statements about two different questions, not a contradiction about a single claim.

### 8. Did any of the disciples ask Jesus where he was going, given that two of them are recorded doing exactly that?

**References:** John 16:5; John 13:36; John 14:5

**The claim:** John 16:5 has Jesus say, 'none of you asketh me, Whither goest thou?' Yet earlier in the very same extended conversation, Peter asks, 'Lord, whither goest thou?' (13:36), and Thomas says, 'we know not whither thou goest' (14:5).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read 16:5 as referring to a different, deeper kind of inquiry than the disciples' earlier questions, rather than literally denying that any question about his departure had ever been asked: Peter's and Thomas's earlier questions (13:36; 14:5) were driven by anxious concern about their own situation -- being left behind, losing their way without him -- rather than genuine curiosity about the meaning and purpose of his departure itself. By 16:5, in the more advanced stage of the same extended discourse, Jesus observes that the disciples' sorrow at his announced departure (16:6) has overtaken any further genuine inquiry into where he is actually going and why (namely, to the Father, for their own ultimate benefit, 16:7) -- the earlier, self-focused questions and the later absence of genuine, forward-looking inquiry describe two different postures at two different points in the same conversation, not a single contradictory claim about whether any question was ever asked.

### 9. Did Jesus tell his disciples everything, or did he withhold some things from them?

**References:** John 16:12; John 15:15

**The claim:** John 15:15 has Jesus tell the disciples, 'all things that I have heard of my Father I have made known unto you.' Yet John 16:12, in the same extended discourse, has Jesus say, 'I have yet many things to say unto you, but ye cannot bear them now.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** John 15:15 describes the full, intimate disclosure Jesus had already given the disciples up to that point regarding his relationship with the Father and the purpose of his mission -- a statement about the genuine, complete friendship and openness he extended to them (contrasted in the same verse with a servant who is not told his master's business), not a claim to have exhaustively communicated every possible future teaching or detail. John 16:12 acknowledges that further teaching remained -- likely concerning the fuller significance of his approaching death and resurrection, and the church's future -- which the disciples, in their current limited understanding and emotional state, were not yet ready to receive, with Jesus explicitly promising this further teaching would come later through the Holy Spirit's guidance (16:13, 'he will guide you into all truth'). A genuine, complete relational openness up to a given point (15:15) and an acknowledgment that further teaching, suited to a later stage of readiness, remained to be given (16:12) are compatible statements about progressive revelation, not a contradiction about a single body of content.

## Literary Composition

### 10. Chapter 21: a likely appended epilogue, following what reads as the Gospel's own natural conclusion

**References:** John 20:30-31; John 21:1-25; John 21:24-25

**The claim:** John 20:30-31 reads as a deliberate, formal conclusion to the entire Gospel: 'Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name.' Yet the Gospel continues for an entire further chapter (21), narrating an additional resurrection appearance and the restoration of Peter, before arriving at a second, somewhat different concluding statement (21:24-25) -- including a curious shift to a plural, seemingly external voice: 'This is the disciple who is testifying to these things, and who has written these things, and we know that his testimony is true' -- raising a question of whether chapter 21 was part of the Gospel's original composition or a later addition.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both John 20:30-31 and John 21 (including its own concluding verses) plainly and consistently as given, without marking chapter 21 as a separate addition in the text itself; this is a matter of literary and compositional interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a widely discussed compositional question among scholars, including many who hold a high view of the chapter's canonical authority and inspiration, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. Many scholars read chapter 21 as a later appendix, added either by the Gospel's own author at a subsequent stage of composition, or by a different member of the same Johannine community responsible for transmitting and finalizing the Gospel, noting: the clear, formal concluding character of 20:30-31, which reads naturally as an ending; some differences in vocabulary and style between chapter 21 and the rest of the Gospel; and especially the curious shift to a plural, third-person voice in 21:24 ('we know that his testimony is true'), which most naturally reads as a separate group or individual vouching for the primary author's own testimony, rather than the author's own first-person self-reference. Other scholars hold chapter 21 was part of the Gospel's original design from the outset, noting that no surviving manuscript of John's Gospel exists without chapter 21 (unlike the textual variants addressed regarding Mark 16 and the passage in entry 1 above, where manuscript evidence for an alternate, shorter ending genuinely exists) -- meaning, whatever its compositional history, chapter 21 has always been transmitted as an integral part of this Gospel in every surviving copy, a materially different textual situation than either of the other two disputed passages addressed in this document and the companion Mark document. This project does not adjudicate the underlying compositional question, noting only that it is compatible with either position (an original single-stage composition, or a swiftly appended and subsequently universally accepted epilogue) that chapter 21 has always been part of the Gospel as it has actually been transmitted and received.

## Theological Interpretation

### 11. 'The Word was God' -- a significant translation question given real doctrinal weight, though not reflected among these ten translations

**References:** John 1:1

**The claim:** John 1:1 concludes, in every translation surveyed in this project, with some form of 'the Word was God.' The underlying Greek (theos en ho logos) places the predicate noun 'God' (theos) before the verb and without the definite article that accompanies 'God' earlier in the same verse ('the Word was with God' uses ho theos, with the article) -- a grammatical construction that has occasionally been rendered by translations outside the scope of this survey (most notably the New World Translation produced by and for Jehovah's Witnesses, who hold a distinctive, non-Trinitarian theology regarding Christ's nature) as 'the Word was a god,' treating the absence of the article as indicating a qualitatively lesser or indefinite sense.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations surveyed in this project render John 1:1's concluding clause as 'the Word was God,' without qualification; the minority rendering ('the Word was a god') is not reflected in any of the ten, and is associated primarily with a translation produced outside this survey's scope for a specific, doctrinally distinctive religious tradition.

**Reconciliation:** This is included as a notable case, similar to the 'today you will be with me in paradise' punctuation question addressed in the companion Luke document, where a minority translation choice associated with a specific, doctrinally distinctive tradition falls entirely outside what any of the ten translations surveyed in this project actually render. The overwhelming majority of Greek grammarians and New Testament scholars, across the full range of theological traditions (including many scholars with no Trinitarian doctrinal stake in the outcome), reject the 'a god' rendering as reflecting a basic misunderstanding of a well-documented feature of Greek grammar: a predicate noun placed before the verb in Greek commonly and idiomatically drops the definite article as a matter of ordinary word order and emphasis (a pattern often described by reference to 'Colwell's Rule' and its subsequent refinements in Greek grammatical scholarship), without this indicating an indefinite or qualitatively lesser sense -- the construction is better understood as emphasizing the Word's full possession of the same divine nature as 'God' (the article-bearing noun earlier in the verse) while maintaining a grammatical distinction between the Word and God the Father as two distinguishable persons, consistent with, rather than contradicting, later Trinitarian theological development. This project does not adjudicate the underlying doctrinal question of Christ's precise relationship to the Father, noting only that the specific grammatical argument for 'a god' is rejected by the overwhelming majority of Greek scholarship regardless of theological position, and that none of the ten translations surveyed in this project reflects that minority rendering.

## Numbers and Text

### 12. The pool of Bethesda and the angel who stirred the water -- another disputed textual passage

**References:** John 5:1-9

**The claim:** John 5:3-4 describes, in the King James tradition, sick people waiting beside the pool of Bethesda 'for the moving of the water. For an angel went down at a certain season into the pool, and troubled the water: whosoever then first after the troubling of the water stepped in was made whole of whatsoever disease he had.' Most modern translations either omit this description of the angel entirely or set it apart with a note, based on its absence from the earliest manuscripts.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** Most modern translations, following the earlier and more widely preferred manuscript tradition, omit the description of the angel stirring the water from the main text (placing it in a footnote or omitting it with an explanatory note), while KJV and NKJV, following the Byzantine tradition underlying the Textus Receptus, include it in the main text; this is a genuine, documented textual variant, not a matter of literary translation style.
- **ESV:** ESV omits the angel description from the main text entirely, with a footnote explaining 'some manuscripts insert, wholly or in part' this material.
- **KJV:** KJV includes the full description of the angel stirring the water within the main numbered text as verse 4, without any bracketing or footnote.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also includes this material, with a footnote noting some manuscripts do not contain this description.
- **NASB:** NASB retains the material in the main text, set off in brackets: '[waiting for the moving of the waters; for an angel of the Lord went down at certain seasons into the pool and stirred up the water; whoever then first, after the stirring up of the water, stepped in was made well from whatever disease with which he was afflicted.]' -- not relegated to a footnote (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV omits the description from the main numbered verses, with a footnote explaining the manuscript evidence.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated the same way as the more extensive textual questions addressed in entry 1 above and in the companion Mark document -- most textual scholars conclude this description of an angel periodically stirring the pool's water was not part of John's original text, but was added later by a scribe attempting to explain the otherwise unexplained folk belief regarding the pool's healing properties that the surrounding narrative simply assumes without directly stating (the sick man's own explanation in 5:7, 'I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up,' presupposes exactly this kind of belief, which a later scribe may have felt needed to be made explicit for readers unfamiliar with the local tradition). This is a smaller-scale instance of the same general phenomenon addressed at greater length in entry 1 and the companion Mark document -- a later scribal addition intended to clarify or supplement the existing narrative, subsequently incorporated into a portion of the manuscript tradition.

### 13. Did Jesus explicitly claim to be God, and is this consistent across the Gospels?

**References:** John 8:58; John 10:30; John 14:28; Mark 10:18

**The claim:** John's Gospel records Jesus making statements many read as direct claims to deity: 'before Abraham was, I am' (John 8:58, echoing God's self-designation to Moses in Exodus 3:14, and provoking an attempt to stone him for blasphemy), and 'I and the Father are one' (John 10:30, which his hearers immediately understood as a claim to divinity, John 10:33). Yet elsewhere Jesus says 'the Father is greater than I' (John 14:28), and in the Synoptics, when addressed as 'Good Teacher,' responds, 'Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone' (Mark 10:18) -- language some read as Jesus distancing himself from a claim to deity.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render these passages plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of Christological interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Mainstream Trinitarian theology holds that these passages are not in tension but reflect a consistent picture in which Jesus is understood as fully divine while also being genuinely, personally distinct from the Father -- a distinction of persons within the Godhead rather than a denial of shared divine nature. On this reading, 'the Father is greater than I' describes Jesus's voluntary, functional submission to the Father during his earthly ministry (compare Philippians 2:6-8's language of the Son 'emptying himself'), not an ontological inferiority; and Mark 10:18 is widely read either as a genuine probing question inviting the questioner to consider the implications of calling Jesus 'good' (since only God is truly good), or as an implicit invitation to recognize Jesus's own divinity rather than a denial of it. Non-Trinitarian readings of the same texts exist and are held seriously within some traditions, typically arguing that 'I am' need not invoke Exodus 3:14 specifically, that unity ('one') need not mean identity of essence, and that the Synoptic texts should be given more interpretive weight than John's -- this remains a genuinely and historically contested question, not one this project resolves, though the majority historic Christian position holds these texts together as compatible expressions of one coherent Christology.

### 14. Was Jesus' public ministry one year, or three?

**References:** John 2:13; John 6:4; John 11:55; Matthew 26:2; Mark 14:1; Luke 22:1

**The claim:** John's Gospel explicitly marks three separate Passover feasts across Jesus's ministry (2:13; 6:4; 11:55), a structure most naturally implying a ministry spanning roughly three years. The Synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, and Luke), by contrast, mention only a single Passover -- the one at which Jesus was crucified -- and their compressed narrative structure, read in isolation, could suggest a ministry of only about one year.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the relevant Passover references in John, and the Synoptic Gospels' single explicit Passover reference, plainly and consistently as given; the question of ministry length is a matter of historical reconstruction, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The great majority of scholars, ancient and modern, hold that the Synoptic Gospels' silence about earlier Passovers reflects their own selective, thematically-organized narration rather than a claim that Jesus's ministry lasted only one year -- none of the three Synoptic Gospels explicitly states or implies that only a single Passover occurred during Jesus's ministry; they simply do not mention the earlier ones, consistent with their broadly topical (not strictly chronological or exhaustive) organization of material, a recognized feature of ancient biographical writing. John's explicit, numbered Passover references are generally treated as the more reliable chronological framework specifically because they are explicit rather than inferred from silence. It is worth noting that a minority of early church fathers (including Clement of Alexandria and others) held a one-year-ministry view, so this is not a purely modern question, though the three-year view built on John's explicit markers has become the substantially more widely held position.

### 15. Did Jesus identify himself to the arresting soldiers, or was he identified by Judas's kiss?

**References:** John 18:2-8; Matthew 26:47-50; Mark 14:43-46; Luke 22:47-48

**The claim:** Matthew, Mark, and Luke all describe Judas identifying Jesus to the arresting party by a prearranged kiss ('The one I will kiss is the man; seize him,' Matthew 26:48). John's account instead emphasizes Jesus stepping forward and identifying himself directly: when the crowd says they are seeking 'Jesus of Nazareth,' Jesus responds 'I am he,' at which the arresting party 'drew back and fell to the ground' (John 18:4-6) -- with no narrated kiss.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both sets of accounts plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of combining complementary narrative details, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two accounts are widely read as complementary rather than competing, describing different moments within the same continuous arrest scene rather than two different, incompatible versions of how Jesus was identified. Notably, John's own account does not omit Judas or deny the kiss -- it explicitly notes, in the same verses, that 'Judas, who betrayed him, was standing with them' (John 18:5) -- it simply chooses to foreground Jesus's own self-identification and its striking effect on the arresting party rather than narrating the kiss itself, which the Synoptics had already recorded. A natural, complete sequence combining all four accounts has Judas approach and kiss Jesus first (as the Synoptics describe), after which Jesus steps forward and addresses the larger crowd directly, prompting the dramatic reaction John specifically records. Nothing in either account requires that these two elements -- the kiss and the self-identification -- be treated as mutually exclusive alternatives for the same single moment.

### 16. Was John the Baptist's recognition of Jesus consistent with his earlier attempt to stop Jesus' baptism?

**References:** Matthew 3:13-14; John 1:29-33

**The claim:** Matthew 3:14 has John the Baptist try to prevent Jesus's baptism, saying 'I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?' -- language suggesting John already recognized Jesus's superior identity and status before baptizing him. Yet John's own Gospel has John the Baptist state twice, in the same episode, 'I myself did not know him' (John 1:31, 33), attributing his eventual recognition of Jesus specifically to witnessing the Spirit descend like a dove at the baptism itself -- seemingly after, not before, the baptism took place.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of harmonizing the nature and timing of John's recognition of Jesus, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization distinguishes between different kinds or degrees of recognition rather than treating John's statements as flatly contradictory. Luke's Gospel establishes that John the Baptist and Jesus were relatives (Luke 1:36), so John plausibly knew Jesus personally and may well have sensed or suspected his exceptional spiritual character or status even before the baptism -- consistent with his hesitation in Matthew 3:14. John's own repeated statement 'I did not know him' in John 1:31, 33 is widely read as referring specifically to certain, divinely-confirmed knowledge that this particular man was the promised Messiah upon whom the Spirit would visibly descend -- a specific prophetic confirmation John states he received only at the moment of the baptism itself, distinct from whatever personal acquaintance or general sense of Jesus's character he may already have had. On this reading, Matthew records John's personal, relational hesitancy, while John's Gospel records the specific moment of prophetic, divinely-confirmed recognition -- two compatible layers of the same event rather than a contradiction.

## Theological Tensions

### 17. Does Jesus judge people, or has he come not to judge?

**References:** John 5:22, 27; John 3:17; John 8:15; John 9:39

**The claim:** John 5:22 states, 'the Father judgeth no man, but hath committed all judgment unto the Son.' Elsewhere, Jesus says, 'God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world' (3:17), and 'I judge no man' (8:15) -- seemingly denying that he judges at all.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These describe different aspects of Christ's mission across different points in time rather than competing claims: John 3:17 and 8:15 describe Jesus's purpose and conduct during his first coming specifically -- he did not come on that occasion to condemn or execute judgment, but to offer salvation, and in his earthly ministry generally refrained from pronouncing final judgment on those he encountered. John 5:22, 27 instead describes the authority the Father has granted the Son to exercise judgment, particularly the final judgment still to come (5:27's 'because he is the Son of man' ties this authority to a future exercise of it, and 9:39 similarly describes a judgment that results from people's response to Christ's coming rather than an act of immediate condemnation). Christ's granted authority to judge (which he will fully exercise at the final judgment) and his purpose and conduct during his earthly ministry (which was not to condemn but to save) are compatible, sequential aspects of the same overall mission, not two contradictory claims about a single moment.

### 18. Who is responsible for congenital blindness, and why did Jesus use an unusual method to heal it?

**References:** John 9:1-7; Exodus 4:11; 2 Corinthians 4:4

**The claim:** John 9:1-7 has Jesus's disciples ask whether a man's congenital blindness was caused by his own sin or his parents', with Jesus answering neither, attributing it instead to an opportunity for 'the works of God' to be displayed. This is set by some against other texts attributing disability to God's own direct causation (Exodus 4:11's 'who maketh the dumb, or deaf, or the seeing, or the blind? have not I the LORD?') or, elsewhere, to Satan's influence (2 Corinthians 4:4). Separately, Jesus's specific healing method here -- making clay from spittle and dust, anointing the man's eyes, and sending him to wash in the pool of Siloam -- is notably more elaborate than his more typical healings by a word or touch.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** On the cause of blindness: Jesus's answer in John 9:3 rejects only the disciples' assumed premise that this specific instance of blindness must be traced to a specific personal or parental sin as its direct cause, redirecting attention to God's sovereign purpose in permitting it (that his works might be displayed) -- this is compatible with the broader biblical affirmation of God's ultimate sovereignty over all creation, including disability (Exodus 4:11, addressing God's comprehensive sovereignty in the abstract), without either text claiming God is the immediate, culpable 'cause' of blindness in the same sense 2 Corinthians 4:4 describes spiritual blindness being exploited by Satan for a wholly different, spiritual kind of blindness (unbelief) rather than physical disability. On the healing method: the Gospels record Jesus using a range of different methods across different healings -- a word, a touch, physical media such as clay and washing -- and most commentators read the specific method chosen here as suited to this occasion's particular teaching purpose (the symbolic, sacramental echo of the original creation account, forming from dust and giving sight, and the man's own subsequent, obedient act of washing functioning as a small but genuine step of faith) rather than reflecting any necessity, limitation, or inconsistency in Jesus's healing power, which the Gospels elsewhere show operating with equal effectiveness through simple word or touch alone.

### 19. Is it possible to fall from grace, given Jesus's promise that his sheep will never perish?

**References:** John 10:27-29; Hebrews 6:4-6; Galatians 5:4

**The claim:** John 10:28-29 has Jesus promise, 'I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand... my Father... is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand' -- a strong statement of security some read as guaranteeing that a genuine believer can never subsequently be lost. Other texts, however, warn of the real possibility of falling away (Hebrews 6:4-6's description of those 'once enlightened' who nonetheless 'fall away'; Galatians 5:4's 'ye are fallen from grace').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This touches one of the most substantively and longest debated questions in Christian theology -- whether genuine believers can be eternally lost -- on which serious, Scripture-affirming traditions continue to differ, and this document does not adjudicate between them. Traditions holding to the perseverance of the saints (broadly, the Reformed tradition) read John 10:28-29 as an unconditional guarantee specifically for those who are genuinely and savingly united to Christ, understanding the warning passages (Hebrews 6, Galatians 5) as addressing either a hypothetical case never actually realized among the truly regenerate, or as describing people who had significant exposure to and association with the Christian community without ever possessing genuine, saving faith in the first place (compare 1 John 2:19's 'they went out from us, but they were not of us'). Traditions holding to conditional security (broadly, Arminian and Wesleyan traditions, among others) read the warning passages as describing a real possibility of a genuine believer's apostasy, understanding John 10:28-29's promise as describing God's own steadfast commitment and the security believers enjoy so long as they continue in persevering faith, rather than an unconditional guarantee that operates regardless of a believer's own subsequent unbelief or apostasy. Both readings treat John 10:28-29 as a genuine, weighty promise of security; they differ on its precise scope and conditions, a matter this document leaves to ongoing theological discussion.

### 20. Who sends the Holy Spirit -- the Father, or the Son?

**References:** John 15:26; John 14:26

**The claim:** John 15:26 has Jesus describe the Spirit as one 'whom I will send unto you from the Father.' John 14:26, shortly before, instead has Jesus say, 'the Father will send [the Comforter] in my name.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both statements are affirmed together elsewhere in the New Testament's own account of Pentecost (Acts 2:33 describes the exalted Christ as having 'received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost' and having himself 'shed forth' what the assembled disciples then witnessed), and mainstream Trinitarian theology reads the two statements as complementary rather than competing: the Spirit's sending into the world (his temporal 'mission' to indwell and empower the church, the specific event both verses describe) is an act of the Father and the Son acting together, in unified purpose, consistent with the New Testament's broader pattern of describing the Trinity's external, temporal works as always undertaken by all three persons in unified action even while the specific language used may emphasize one person's particular role in a given text. A related but more technical historical question -- whether the Spirit's own eternal procession (his internal relationship of origin within the Godhead, as distinct from his temporal sending into the world) proceeds from the Father alone or from the Father and the Son together -- became a major point of theological division between the Eastern and Western churches (the 'filioque' controversy, a significant factor in the eleventh-century schism between Rome and Constantinople), a genuine and historically significant doctrinal disagreement this document does not adjudicate, though it concerns the Spirit's eternal relations within the Trinity rather than the temporal sending these two Gospel verses describe.

### 21. Should we believe in things we have not seen, given the importance placed elsewhere on eyewitness testimony?

**References:** John 20:24-29; 1 John 1:1

**The claim:** John 20:29 has Jesus tell Thomas, after Thomas believed only upon seeing and touching the risen Jesus, 'blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed' -- commending faith without direct sight. Yet 1 John 1:1 places great weight on direct, physical eyewitness testimony: 'that which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled, of the Word of life.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These describe two different, complementary categories of legitimate belief rather than competing claims about the value of evidence: the apostles and other direct eyewitnesses (including Thomas, and the author of 1 John) rightly relied on, and continued to testify to, the direct, physical evidence they themselves witnessed -- this firsthand evidence is precisely what 1 John 1:1 emphasizes as the foundation the apostles are passing on to others. John 20:29's blessing is pronounced specifically on those who come after, without the same opportunity for direct, personal, physical encounter, who are commended for trusting the reliable testimony of those who did have such access -- a different, later category of belief (trusting credible eyewitness testimony) rather than a devaluation of the eyewitness evidence itself, which remains the very basis 1 John 1:1 says is being faithfully transmitted for exactly this purpose.
