# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 Timothy

This document catalogs 5 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 1 Timothy,
either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Galatians,
Ephesians, 1 Corinthians, Romans, and Colossians documents. For each item, this document
states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entries 1 and 2 address 1 Timothy 2:11-
15, the central New Testament proof-text restricting women's teaching authority in the church,
and its immediately following, notoriously difficult companion verse ("saved through
childbearing"). Entry 4 addresses the authorship of the Pastoral Epistles, a major and actively
debated critical-scholarship question, checked against specific, quantified linguistic
evidence on both sides.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 2, and 4 are presented with genuine scholarly and theological
diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution, consistent with this project's
treatment of comparably contested denominational and critical questions elsewhere in this
series.

**Verification status:** Entries 2 and 4 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing the genuine range of scholarly positions on each question, including specific
statistical data on the authorship debate and multiple named interpretive proposals for
"saved through childbearing." The remaining entries are addressed from standard harmonization
approaches, several of which build on treatments already established in the companion
Galatians, Ephesians, 1 Corinthians, Romans, and Colossians documents.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly or
theological diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man' -- the central proof-text for a major, ongoing denominational debate

**References:** 1 Timothy 2:11-15

**The claim:** 1 Timothy 2:11-12 states, 'Let a woman learn quietly with all submissiveness. I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; rather, she is to remain quiet,' grounding this instruction in the order of creation ('for Adam was formed first, then Eve,' 2:13) and Eve's own deception (2:14) -- widely regarded as the single most significant New Testament text specifically restricting women's teaching authority within the church, connecting to and intensifying the broader egalitarian/complementarian debate addressed in the companion Galatians, Ephesians, and 1 Corinthians documents regarding gender roles more generally.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Timothy 2:11-14 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most actively and sincerely debated questions in contemporary Christian theology and ecclesiology, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This project does not adjudicate this actively and sincerely debated question, consistent with its treatment of the comparable questions in the companion Galatians, Ephesians, and 1 Corinthians documents. Complementarian interpreters read this passage as a direct, general, and permanently binding restriction on women teaching or exercising doctrinal authority over men within the gathered church, grounded specifically in the created order (rather than in any particular first-century cultural circumstance), consistent with similar instructions this tradition finds in 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 (addressed in its own companion document) and with the qualifications for church overseers later in this same chapter (3:1-7, addressed using exclusively masculine pronouns and the phrase 'husband of one wife'). Egalitarian interpreters read the passage as addressing a specific, historically-conditioned problem in the Ephesian church (where Timothy was serving, 1:3) -- likely connected to specific false teaching being spread by particular women in that congregation (addressed throughout this letter, including 2 Timothy 3:6-7's reference to false teachers 'who capture weak women'), or to the particular educational disadvantages and lack of formal training available to women in that specific first-century context (2:11's opening instruction that women should 'learn,' itself a notable, positive statement for its time) -- reading the passage's prohibition as addressing that specific, remediable situation rather than establishing a timeless, universal restriction unrelated to cultural circumstance. This project takes no position on which reading is correct.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. 'She will be saved through childbearing' -- one of the most genuinely perplexing individual verses in the New Testament

**References:** 1 Timothy 2:15

**The claim:** 1 Timothy 2:15 immediately follows the passage addressed in entry 1 above with a notoriously difficult statement: 'Yet she will be saved through childbearing -- if they continue in faith and love and holiness, with self-control.' Read at face value, this appears to condition women's salvation specifically on childbearing, in obvious tension with the New Testament's consistent, repeated affirmation that salvation comes by grace through faith alone, not through any specific work or biological role (Ephesians 2:8-9), and raising an obvious practical difficulty for women who cannot or do not bear children.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Timothy 2:15 plainly and consistently as given, preserving its genuinely difficult wording; this remains one of the most extensively debated individual verses in the New Testament, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is widely acknowledged, across the theological spectrum, as one of the single most difficult individual verses to interpret in the entire New Testament, and this entry presents the range of leading proposals rather than a single certain answer. Nearly all serious commentators agree the verse cannot mean eternal salvation is earned or secured through the physical act of childbirth, since this would directly contradict the passage's own immediately surrounding argument and Paul's consistent theology elsewhere. Beyond this shared starting point, several distinct interpretations are seriously defended. Some read 'saved' as referring to physical safety and preservation through the genuine dangers of childbirth in the ancient world, conditioned on continued faithful Christian character. Some scholars connect this to the specific first-century Ephesian setting, where the prominent local cult of Artemis was specifically associated with protecting women in childbirth -- meaning Paul may be redirecting the Ephesian women's trust from that pagan goddess to the true God's own protection. Others read 'the childbearing,' with the Greek definite article present in the original, as a specific reference to the birth of Christ, understanding the verse as declaring that women's salvation, like all humanity's, ultimately comes through Christ's own birth through a woman -- a reading with some connection to Genesis 3:15's promise of a coming 'seed' of the woman, though this reading is a minority position since Paul does not use this specific kind of language elsewhere. Still others read the verse more broadly as describing women's faithful embrace of their domestic calling and role, of which childbearing serves as a synecdoche, a specific example standing for the broader pattern, as the outworking and evidence of a genuine, ongoing faith. This is parallel to how good works more generally serve as evidence of authentic faith elsewhere in the New Testament, addressed in the companion Romans document regarding the relationship between faith and works. This project does not adjudicate between these proposals, noting that this remains one of the most genuinely perplexing individual verses addressed anywhere in the New Testament.

### 3. 'The love of money is a root of all kinds of evils' -- a frequently misquoted verse

**References:** 1 Timothy 6:10

**The claim:** 1 Timothy 6:10 is widely and popularly misquoted as 'money is the root of all evil' -- a considerably stronger, more absolute claim than what the verse actually states: 'For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils. It is through this craving that some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs.' The popular misquotation both omits the crucial qualifier ('the love of' money, not money itself) and overstates the verse's own more measured claim ('a root of all kinds of evils,' not literally 'the root of all evil' without exception).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE:** All ten translations render 1 Timothy 6:10 plainly and consistently as given, including both qualifying phrases ('the love of' money, and 'a root of all kinds of' evils); this entry addresses a matter of popular misquotation diverging from the verse's actual wording, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.
- **KJV:** KJV reads 'For the love of money is the root of all evil' -- notably, unlike the other nine translations surveyed, KJV does NOT include the 'kinds of' qualifier and uses the definite article 'the root' rather than 'a root' (verified against the published text). This means KJV's actual wording is closer to the popular misquotation than the other nine translations, differing from it only in retaining 'the love of' money rather than 'money' alone -- a nuance worth noting given this entry's broader point about the verse being popularly misquoted.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable and frequently discussed case, similar to other instances addressed elsewhere in this project (regarding Philippians 4:13's popular decontextualized use), of a memorable verse's popular citation diverging from its actual wording and meaning. Paul's actual statement locates the moral danger specifically in the inward disposition of greed or covetous attachment to wealth ('the love of money'), not in money or wealth as such (which Paul elsewhere treats as morally neutral, capable of being used rightly or wrongly, e.g., 1 Timothy 6:17-19's instructions to the rich about being 'generous and ready to share' rather than a blanket condemnation of wealth itself), and describes this love of money as 'a root' among various roots of evil, rather than claiming it is literally the singular, exclusive source of every evil that exists. The popularly circulated version of this verse, by dropping both qualifications, produces a considerably more sweeping and philosophically different claim than what Paul's own text actually states.

## Literary Composition

### 4. The authorship of the Pastoral Epistles -- a major, actively debated critical question

**References:** 1 Timothy 1:1

**The claim:** 1 Timothy identifies its own author as 'Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus' (1:1), a claim unanimously accepted throughout the early church (the second-century bishop Polycarp already alludes to this letter as authoritative). Since the nineteenth century, however, a substantial body of critical scholarship has questioned Pauline authorship of 1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, and Titus as a group (commonly called the 'Pastoral Epistles'), based on differences in vocabulary, literary style, church organizational structure, and theological emphasis from Paul's other, less disputed letters.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the entirety of 1 Timothy as a continuous text attributed to Paul, without marking any portion as disputed in the text itself; the authorship question addressed here is a matter of historical-critical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a major, long-running, and genuinely active scholarly debate, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. Scholars questioning Pauline authorship (including Bart Ehrman, and, in more detail, Norman Perrin and Werner Georg Kummel) point to specific, quantified linguistic evidence: of roughly 848 distinct words found in the Pastoral Epistles, 306 do not appear elsewhere in Paul's other ten letters, and of those, 175 appear nowhere else in the entire New Testament, with a substantial proportion of the Pastorals' distinctive vocabulary instead matching the general usage of second-century Christian writers -- alongside a markedly different, more measured and less argumentatively combative literary style than Paul's other letters, and church leadership structures (bishops, elders, deacons with detailed formal qualifications) some read as reflecting a later, more institutionally developed stage of church organization than Paul's own lifetime. Scholars defending Pauline authorship (including a detailed 2016 Liberty University academic paper directly responding to Ehrman's specific claims, and the biblical scholars I. Howard Marshall, Philip Towner, and William Mounce, among others) respond that vocabulary-statistics arguments prove less than they initially appear to: comparable rates of unique vocabulary exist across Paul's other, undisputed letters when analyzed the same way, and applying the same statistical method consistently would imply three separate pseudonymous authors (since the three Pastoral letters each have their own distinct proportion of unique vocabulary), a conclusion even critics of Pauline authorship do not typically accept; these scholars further note the unanimous, unbroken testimony of the early church fathers affirming Pauline authorship, with no attested ancient tradition proposing an alternative author. This project does not adjudicate between these positions, noting that this remains one of the most substantively and actively debated authorship questions in New Testament scholarship.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 5. 'Forbidding marriage and requiring abstinence from foods' as a mark of departure from the faith -- vs. Paul's own qualified endorsement of celibacy elsewhere

**References:** 1 Timothy 4:1-5; 1 Corinthians 7:1, 7-9

**The claim:** 1 Timothy 4:1-3 warns that 'in later times some will depart from the faith... through the insincerity of liars whose consciences are seared, who forbid marriage and require abstinence from foods that God created to be received with thanksgiving' -- treating mandatory celibacy and dietary restriction as a mark of apostasy and demonic deception. This raises a question in light of Paul's own qualified endorsement of celibacy as a genuinely good, even preferable, state for those able to embrace it (1 Corinthians 7:1, 7-9, 'it is good for a man not to touch a woman... I wish that all were as I myself am [unmarried]'), and the Old Testament's own extensive dietary legislation (addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding the Levitical food laws).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Timothy 4:1-5 and 1 Corinthians 7:1, 7-9 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing mandatory ascetic legalism from voluntary personal celibacy or dietary discretion, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read 1 Timothy 4:1-5 as condemning not celibacy or dietary discretion as such, but the specific claim that these practices are spiritually obligatory requirements necessary for a more advanced or superior form of Christian devotion -- a form of ascetic legalism the passage explicitly identifies as a doctrinal error, rather than as a general condemnation of anyone who personally chooses celibacy or exercises discretion about food. This distinction is directly supported by comparing this passage with 1 Corinthians 7, where Paul commends celibacy specifically as one legitimate personal calling among others, not superior in itself, and explicitly available to those to whom 'it is given' (1 Corinthians 7:7), rather than mandated for all. It is also supported by Paul's own broader teaching on food, addressed in the companion Romans and Colossians documents, which treats dietary practice as a matter of Christian liberty and personal conscience rather than either an absolute prohibition or a rigid, obligatory asceticism. 1 Timothy 4:1-5 is generally read as condemning specifically the doctrinal insistence that marriage and ordinary food are themselves spiritually inferior or forbidden -- a position at odds with the goodness of creation this same passage explicitly affirms (4:4, 'everything created by God is good') -- rather than condemning any individual's own free personal choice of celibacy or dietary discretion, both of which Paul elsewhere explicitly commends as legitimate.
