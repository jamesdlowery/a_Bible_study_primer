# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Proverbs

This document catalogs 20 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Proverbs, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with 1 Kings, Job,
Ecclesiastes, Ephesians, Romans, Psalm 104, and John. For each item, this document states the
claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages
involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable
the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this book's genre:** Entry 1 (Proverbs 26:4-5) is the interpretive key to this
entire document: the book's own compiler places two directly contradictory pieces of advice
back to back, deliberately teaching that proverbs are compressed, situational wisdom rather
than universal, exceptionless laws. Several other entries in this set (2, 4, and 8) apply this
same genre principle to other frequently misread "guarantee" verses.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts directly.

## Literary Genre

### 1. 'Answer not a fool' -- and 'Answer a fool,' back to back

**References:** Proverbs 26:4-5

**The claim:** Proverbs 26:4 instructs, 'Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest you be like him yourself.' The very next verse instructs the opposite: 'Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes' (26:5) -- two directly contradictory pieces of advice placed immediately next to each other by the book's own compiler, with no attempt to reconcile or choose between them.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses plainly and consistently as directly opposite instructions, without softening or reconciling them in the text; the book itself intends the reader to hold both together as situational wisdom, not to resolve them into a single universal rule.

**Reconciliation:** This is the single clearest, most deliberate example in the entire Bible of the genre principle that governs how every other entry in this document should be read: proverbs, by their very nature, are compressed observations of general wisdom suited to particular situations, not universal, exceptionless laws holding in every circumstance -- and the book's compiler placed these two proverbs back to back precisely to teach discernment rather than rote application. Read together, the pair instructs that whether to engage a fool's folly on its own terms depends entirely on the specific goal and situation: sometimes engaging invites the wise person down to the fool's level and accomplishes nothing (26:4's warning), while other times a pointed, folly-matching response is exactly what exposes the fool's error and prevents onlookers from mistaking foolish confidence for actual wisdom (26:5's warning) -- wisdom, on this reading, consists precisely in knowing which situation one is in, not in having a single rule that covers every case. This same principle -- that a proverb states what is generally and usually true, not what is true without exception in every individual case -- is the key that resolves the majority of alleged contradictions raised elsewhere in this book, including entry 2 below.

### 2. 'Train up a child' -- an absolute guarantee, or a general principle?

**References:** Proverbs 22:6

**The claim:** Proverbs 22:6 states, 'Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it' -- language many readers take as an unconditional promise that godly upbringing guarantees a child's lifelong faithfulness, which appears to be contradicted by the common and painful experience of genuinely godly parents whose children nonetheless abandon the faith, and by biblical examples such as David's own son Absalom's rebellion despite David's own relationship with God.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Proverbs 22:6 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading it as a general principle within the book's own established genre of situational wisdom literature, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with entry 1 above, this is best read in light of the literary genre of the entire book: Proverbs consists overwhelmingly of general observations about how life tends to work when its principles are followed, not unconditional divine guarantees applicable without exception to every individual case -- a genre distinction Proverbs itself repeatedly signals through its own structure (compare the directly contradictory pair at 26:4-5, addressed in entry 1, which the book's own compiler evidently did not consider to undermine either proverb's general validity). Godly training genuinely and reliably shapes a child's character and increases the likelihood of a lifelong godly trajectory -- the proverb's underlying principle remains true and observably confirmed across many lives -- without this constituting a mechanical, guaranteed outcome that overrides a child's own later, freely made choices, a category distinction the book of Proverbs as a whole (rooted in a broader biblical theology of human moral agency and responsibility) does not claim to eliminate.

## Theological Tensions

### 3. 'He who justifies the wicked... is an abomination' -- yet God 'justifies the ungodly'

**References:** Proverbs 17:15; Romans 4:5

**The claim:** Proverbs 17:15 states plainly, 'He who justifies the wicked and he who condemns the righteous are both alike an abomination to the LORD' -- establishing the declaration of a guilty person as innocent as a serious moral wrong. Yet Paul, describing the doctrine of justification by faith, writes that God 'justifies the ungodly' (Romans 4:5) as the very heart of the gospel -- language that, on its face, describes exactly the act Proverbs 17:15 condemns.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Proverbs 17:15 and Romans 4:5 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing a corrupt legal acquittal that denies actual guilt from a genuine legal transaction addressing that guilt through a substitute, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most theologians resolve this by distinguishing the specific wrong Proverbs 17:15 addresses (a human judge or witness falsely declaring a guilty party innocent of the very crime they actually committed, corrupting the judicial process and misrepresenting reality, addressed in a legal/courtroom context) from the theological event Paul describes, in which God does not misrepresent or deny the sinner's actual guilt, but rather provides a genuine legal basis -- Christ's own substitutionary righteousness and atoning death, credited to the believer -- by which the sinner can be truly and justly declared righteous without God compromising his own justice (a tension Paul directly and explicitly addresses just verses earlier in the same letter, Romans 3:25-26, stating Christ's sacrifice was 'to show God's righteousness... so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus'). On this reading, human judges who falsely declare the guilty innocent do so by ignoring or denying the truth of what actually happened, while God's justification of the believing sinner rests on a genuine legal transaction (the imputation of Christ's righteousness) that does not require pretending the sinner's guilt does not exist, but rather addresses and satisfies it through a substitute -- a structurally different kind of 'justification' than the false, reality-denying acquittal Proverbs 17:15 condemns.

### 4. 'The fear of the LORD prolongs life' -- retribution theology, and the observed reality of the righteous dying young

**References:** Proverbs 10:27; Proverbs 3:1-2; Ecclesiastes 7:15; 2 Kings 23:29

**The claim:** Proverbs repeatedly promises long life as a reward for wisdom and the fear of the LORD, and short life as the consequence of wickedness (10:27, 3:1-2, and elsewhere) -- a pattern of confident, general retribution theology. Yet observed experience, and Scripture's own record, regularly shows this pattern failing to hold in individual cases: King Josiah, commended in this project's companion 2 Kings document as uniquely devoted to the LORD, is killed relatively young in battle (2 Kings 23:29), and Ecclesiastes itself explicitly names the difficulty: 'there is a righteous man who perishes in his righteousness, and there is a wicked man who prolongs his life in his evildoing' (Ecclesiastes 7:15).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Proverbs' life-and-death promises and Ecclesiastes' explicit exception plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing Proverbs' own genre as general wisdom rather than unconditional guarantee, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length regarding the closely related tension between Psalm 37 and Psalm 73 addressed in the companion Psalms document. In brief, Proverbs' promises of long life for wisdom and short life for folly are best read, consistent with the book's overall genre (addressed in entries 1 and 2 above), as general statistical and practical observations about how a wise, disciplined, morally careful life tends to reduce risk and extend life on average across a population, rather than as an unconditional guarantee immune to exceptional individual cases -- a wise person genuinely tends to avoid many of the specific dangers (violence, disease from reckless living, enemies made through folly) that shorten the lives of the foolish, without this excluding cases where external factors (war, disease, or, as with Josiah, a single unwise military decision late in an otherwise exemplary life) intervene. Ecclesiastes' own explicit acknowledgment of exceptions is generally read as complementing rather than contradicting Proverbs' general principle, adding the wisdom tradition's own honest caveat to its more typical, generally-true observations.

### 5. Prosperity promised for honoring God with wealth -- vs. Job's suffering despite piety

**References:** Proverbs 3:9-10; Job 1:1-3, 13-19

**The claim:** Proverbs 3:9-10 promises, 'Honor the LORD with your wealth and with the firstfruits of all your produce; then your barns will be filled with plenty, and your vats will be bursting with wine' -- a confident promise of material blessing in response to faithful giving. Job, by contrast, is introduced as the most scrupulously righteous man of his generation (Job 1:1), explicitly including careful, proactive religious devotion on behalf of his whole household (1:5), yet loses all his wealth and his children in a single day (1:13-19) -- an experience that reads as the direct opposite of Proverbs' promised pattern.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Proverbs 3:9-10 and Job's prologue plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing Proverbs' own genre as general wisdom literature, with Job's own extended narrative directly and deliberately addressing the exceptional cases Proverbs' general pattern does not claim to cover, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Job document (addressing the book's own extensive theological engagement with exactly this tension) and touches the same genre distinction addressed in entries 1, 2, and 4 above: Proverbs' financial promises, like its other promises of prolonged life and general flourishing, describe the ordinary, statistically observable pattern of how generous, God-honoring living tends to correlate with material sufficiency in the normal course of life (avoiding the specific financial ruin that often follows from folly, dishonesty, or wastefulness, which Proverbs elsewhere addresses at length), not an unconditional, exceptionless divine guarantee that overrides all other factors, including the kind of extraordinary, specifically permitted testing the book of Job's own unique prologue describes. Job's entire book exists, in significant part, precisely to demonstrate through an extended narrative case study that the general pattern Proverbs describes does not hold in every individual instance, and that suffering is not always evidence of a specific violation of Proverbs' general principles -- the two books are best read as complementary parts of a single wisdom tradition that includes both its general, usually-true observations (Proverbs) and its honest acknowledgment of the exceptions and deeper mysteries surrounding suffering (Job, and similarly Ecclesiastes).

### 6. 'The LORD possessed me' -- or 'created me'? A theologically loaded translation variance, central to the ancient Arian controversy

**References:** Proverbs 8:22-31

**The claim:** Proverbs 8 personifies Wisdom as present with God from before creation, declaring in verse 22 (rendered variously across translations) either that 'the LORD possessed me at the beginning of his way' or that 'the LORD created me at the beginning of his way' -- a translation choice with substantial historical theological weight, since some later interpreters (both the ancient Arian party in the fourth-century church, and modern groups such as Jehovah's Witnesses) have read this passage as describing the origin of the pre-incarnate Christ (identifying Wisdom with the Son, per 1 Corinthians 1:24, 30 and parallels with John 1:1-3 and Colossians 1:15-17), meaning a translation reading 'created' appears to support the view that Christ himself was a created being with a temporal beginning, directly contradicting historic Christian orthodoxy's affirmation of Christ's eternal, uncreated divine nature.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'The LORD created and possessed me at the beginning of His way,' explicitly combining both traditions in the text itself via amplification (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'The Lord acquired me at the beginning of his creation, before his works of long ago' -- a third distinct rendering, differing from both the 'possessed' (KJV/ESV) and 'created' (NASB2020/RSV) traditions (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'The LORD possessed me at the beginning of his work,' with a footnote noting the alternative 'fathered' or 'created' (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'The LORD possessed me in the beginning of his way,' following the traditional 'possessed' reading (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders 'The LORD possessed me at the beginning of His way,' following the same tradition (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** The current NASB text renders 'The LORD created me at the beginning of His way,' departing from the KJV/NKJV tradition and following the Septuagint's 'created' reading (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'Yahweh possessed me at the beginning of His way, Before His deeds of old' -- following the traditional 'possessed' reading, notably departing from NASB's current (2020) 'created' rendering despite LSB's usual close tracking of NASB (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'The LORD brought me forth as the first of his works,' a third rendering avoiding both 'possessed' and 'created' directly, with a footnote on the Hebrew's range of meaning (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'The LORD formed me from the beginning, before he created anything else,' yet another distinct rendering (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'The LORD created me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old,' following the 'created' reading (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** Most conservative and mainstream scholars address this on two separate levels. First, on the underlying Hebrew: the word in question (qanah) most commonly and characteristically means 'to acquire,' 'to possess,' or 'to get' -- its ordinary sense throughout the rest of the Old Testament, e.g. Genesis 4:1 and 14:19 -- and only rarely and disputedly carries a sense approaching 'to create' or 'to form.' The ancient Greek Septuagint's choice to render it with a word meaning 'created' (ektisen) at this specific verse, centuries before the Arian controversy, is what later gave the Arian party their key proof text. But many Hebrew scholars regard this specific Greek rendering as an imprecise or debatable translation choice rather than a necessary or uniquely correct one; the alternative ancient Greek versions (Aquila, Theodotion, Symmachus) explicitly render the same word as 'possessed,' not 'created.' Second, and more fundamentally, most theologians hold that even setting the translation question aside, Proverbs 8's poetic personification of Wisdom is not a direct, literal statement about the origin of the second person of the Trinity in the first place. It is a poetic figure -- Wisdom as a personified quality or attribute of God, a common device in ancient Near Eastern wisdom literature -- whose later, typological application to Christ by New Testament writers and later theologians does not require reading every clause of the Old Testament poem as a literal, systematic statement of Christology. No more than any other image applied typologically to Christ elsewhere in the Old Testament requires a strictly literal one-to-one correspondence in every detail.

### 7. Does wisdom make people happy, or does increasing wisdom increase sorrow?

**References:** Proverbs 3:13; Ecclesiastes 1:18

**The claim:** Proverbs 3:13 declares, 'Happy is the man that findeth wisdom, and the man that getteth understanding.' Ecclesiastes 1:18, however, states, 'in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two books approach wisdom from different angles consistent with their different purposes: Proverbs describes wisdom's practical fruit in ordinary life -- the settled contentment, good judgment, and favorable outcomes that following wise counsel tends to produce, a genuinely happy state relative to the fool's self-destructive path. Ecclesiastes, written from the perspective of someone who has pursued wisdom to its furthest human limits (1:16, 'I have gotten more wisdom than all they that have been before me'), reflects on a different and more painful kind of increased understanding -- namely, a clearer, more painfully honest perception of the world's injustices, life's brevity, and the limits of human achievement 'under the sun,' the very difficulties Ecclesiastes spends its whole length wrestling with. Both observations are true simultaneously: wisdom brings the practical blessing Proverbs describes while also bringing the heavier, clearer-eyed awareness of hard realities that a less reflective, less wise person might simply not notice -- ignorance can feel more comfortable in the short term precisely because it does not see what wisdom sees, without this making ignorance actually preferable, a point Ecclesiastes itself never concludes despite voicing the difficulty candidly.

### 8. Is it true that no evil happens to the righteous, given the wisdom tradition's own acknowledgment of righteous suffering?

**References:** Proverbs 12:21

**The claim:** Proverbs 12:21 states plainly, 'There shall no evil happen to the just: but the wicked shall be filled with mischief' -- an unqualified-sounding claim of protection for the righteous.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is best read in light of the same wisdom-literature genre principle addressed at length elsewhere in this document (see the entries on Proverbs 26:4-5 and Proverbs 22:6): Proverbs consistently states general patterns and reliable tendencies rather than universal, exceptionless guarantees, a distinction the book's own compiler deliberately teaches through its structure. Read this way, 12:21 describes the general, long-term pattern that a life of integrity tends to avoid much of the self-inflicted disaster and ultimate ruin that characterizes persistent wickedness (the specific 'mischief' the verse contrasts it with), rather than promising the righteous complete immunity from every misfortune, illness, or hardship in a fallen world -- a promise this book's own companion volume, Job, exists specifically to explore in searching detail, and which the wisdom tradition elsewhere (Ecclesiastes 7:15) explicitly acknowledges does not hold without exception.

### 9. Can wealth serve as a ransom for a person's life, given that no amount of riches can redeem a soul from death?

**References:** Proverbs 13:8; Psalm 49:6-9

**The claim:** Proverbs 13:8 states, 'The ransom of a man's life are his riches,' suggesting wealth can function protectively, buying off threats or danger. Psalm 49:6-9, however, states that the wealthy 'cannot by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him... that he should still live for ever, and not see corruption.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two texts address ransom in different scopes and toward different threats: Proverbs 13:8 describes wealth's genuine, practical, temporal usefulness -- a rich person can pay off kidnappers, satisfy creditors, hire protection, or otherwise buy their way out of many worldly dangers that a poor person cannot escape by the same means, an observation about ordinary practical life. Psalm 49 addresses a categorically different and far weightier threat -- death itself and the need for ultimate redemption from it -- and denies that any amount of human wealth can purchase escape from that particular fate or from divine judgment, a limitation no worldly ransom can overcome. Wealth's real, limited usefulness against worldly threats (Proverbs) and its total uselessness against death and final judgment (the Psalm) are not competing claims about the same kind of threat.

### 10. Should we believe everything, or does true wisdom require scrutiny rather than simple belief?

**References:** Proverbs 14:15; Matthew 18:3; 1 Corinthians 13:7

**The claim:** Proverbs 14:15 warns, 'The simple believeth every word: but the prudent man looketh well to his going' -- commending scrutiny and caution over naive credulity. Jesus commends becoming 'as little children' to enter the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 18:3), and Paul describes love as believing 'all things' (1 Corinthians 13:7) -- both seemingly commending a simpler, more trusting posture.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These texts commend different things in different domains: Proverbs 14:15 warns specifically against gullibility in practical, worldly matters -- accepting claims, deals, or promises uncritically without exercising due diligence, a caution about discernment in everyday practical dealings and susceptibility to deception. Jesus' commendation of childlike faith in Matthew 18:3 concerns humble dependence and trust specifically toward God -- an unpretentious, teachable posture of receiving the kingdom as a gift rather than an achievement, not a general endorsement of uncritical credulity toward any and every human claim. Paul's statement that love 'believeth all things' in 1 Corinthians 13:7 is best read, in context, as describing a charitable disposition that gives others the benefit of the doubt and does not assume the worst about people, rather than an epistemological claim that a loving person should accept every factual assertion without discernment. None of these texts commends gullibility in the sense Proverbs 14:15 warns against, and Proverbs 14:15 does not discourage humble trust in God or charitable goodwill toward others.

### 11. Did God make the wicked specifically for the day of destruction?

**References:** Proverbs 16:4; 2 Peter 3:9; 1 Timothy 2:4

**The claim:** Proverbs 16:4 states, 'The LORD hath made all things for himself: yea, even the wicked for the day of evil' -- language some read as teaching that God specifically created certain people for the purpose of their own damnation. 2 Peter 3:9 states God is 'not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance,' and 1 Timothy 2:4 says God 'will have all men to be saved.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question touches the long-debated theological relationship between divine sovereignty and human destiny addressed at greater length in this project's Deuteronomy companion document (regarding free will and predestination), and Christian traditions differ in how they resolve it: some Reformed and Calvinist interpreters read Proverbs 16:4 as affirming that God, in his sovereign purposes, has ordained that the wicked's persistent rebellion will ultimately serve his own just purposes and glorify his justice on a day of reckoning, without this being read as God authoring or delighting in evil itself, holding this compatible with 2 Peter 3:9 and 1 Timothy 2:4 by distinguishing God's revealed, universal invitation to repentance (which these two texts express) from his sovereign, hidden ordering of history's ultimate outcomes. Other traditions read Proverbs 16:4 more narrowly as simply affirming that God governs and will hold accountable even the wicked, who will not escape a reckoning for their evil ('the day of evil' referring to their judgment, not their manufacture for that end), fully consistent with 2 Peter and 1 Timothy's affirmation that God genuinely desires and offers salvation to all. This document does not adjudicate between these positions, consistent with its treatment of the closely related free-will question elsewhere.

### 12. Does pleasing God guarantee that even one's enemies will be at peace, given the consistent biblical pattern of the righteous being persecuted?

**References:** Proverbs 16:7; John 15:18-20; 2 Timothy 3:12

**The claim:** Proverbs 16:7 states, 'When a man's ways please the LORD, he maketh even his enemies to be at peace with him' -- read by some as an unconditional guarantee. Yet Jesus tells his disciples the world will hate them as it hated him (John 15:18-20), and Paul states that 'all that will live godly in Christ Jesus shall suffer persecution' (2 Timothy 3:12) -- both describing faithful, God-pleasing people facing ongoing hostility rather than universal peace with their enemies.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the other proverbial 'guarantee' language addressed at length elsewhere in this document (see the entries on Proverbs 26:4-5 and 22:6, establishing the book's own genre of general, usually-true wisdom rather than universal law), Proverbs 16:7 describes a real and observable general tendency -- integrity, wisdom, and godly conduct often do defuse hostility and win over opponents, as illustrated in specific biblical narratives (such as Abimelech's peace-seeking toward Isaac after observing God's evident blessing on him, Genesis 26:26-31) -- rather than promising this outcome without exception in every case. Jesus' and Paul's statements address a different and specific category of hostility: opposition provoked precisely by faithful identification with Christ and his kingdom, a hostility Scripture presents as the world's rejection of God himself working through his people, which even perfect God-pleasing conduct does not eliminate (since Christ himself, perfectly pleasing to the Father, was rejected and crucified) -- the general social principle of Proverbs 16:7 and the specific promise of persecution for Christ's sake operate in different domains and are not offering competing universal guarantees.

### 13. Is happiness good, or is sorrow better than laughter?

**References:** Proverbs 17:22; Ecclesiastes 7:3

**The claim:** Proverbs 17:22 states, 'A merry heart doeth good like a medicine: but a broken spirit drieth the bones,' commending cheerfulness and warning against its opposite. Ecclesiastes 7:3 states, 'Sorrow is better than laughter: for by the sadness of the countenance the heart is made better.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the closely related tension addressed elsewhere in this document regarding wisdom and grief (Proverbs 3:13 against Ecclesiastes 1:18), the two books again approach the same subject from different angles and for different purposes: Proverbs 17:22 makes a general, practical observation about ordinary emotional health and physical well-being -- a cheerful disposition is generally beneficial to a person's body and daily functioning, while chronic despair ('a broken spirit') is harmful, a straightforwardly true observation about ordinary life. Ecclesiastes 7:3, within a chapter specifically commending the sober reflection that comes from confronting mortality and hardship honestly (7:2's 'it is better to go to the house of mourning, than to go to the house of feasting'), makes a narrower, more particular point about sorrow's capacity to produce serious reflection and genuine wisdom that shallow, escapist laughter cannot -- not a categorical claim that sorrow is generally preferable to joy in every respect, which would contradict the rest of Ecclesiastes' own repeated commendation of enjoying life's genuine pleasures as a gift from God (e.g., 2:24; 3:12-13; 9:7). Read in full context, both texts commend appropriate emotional responses suited to their respective occasions rather than offering a single, universal ranking of happiness against sorrow.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 14. 'He who finds a wife finds a good thing' -- vs. Ecclesiastes' bitter assessment of women

**References:** Proverbs 18:22; Ecclesiastes 7:26, 28

**The claim:** Proverbs 18:22 states warmly, 'He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD.' Ecclesiastes, by contrast, includes among its more startling and difficult statements: 'I find more bitter than death the woman whose heart is snares and nets, and whose hands are fetters... one man among a thousand I found, but a woman among all these I have not found' (7:26, 28) -- language that reads as a sweeping, negative generalization about women specifically.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Proverbs 18:22 and Ecclesiastes 7:26, 28 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading Ecclesiastes 7:26 as addressing a specific dangerous figure (also warned against elsewhere in Proverbs itself) and 7:28 within its own rhetorical and autobiographical context, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Ecclesiastes 7:26 in its immediate context as describing a specific kind of dangerous, manipulative woman -- the same figure Proverbs itself repeatedly and at length warns against under the heading of 'the adulteress' or 'the forbidden woman' (e.g. Proverbs 5, 7, and 9:13-18) -- rather than making a claim about women in general. The Preacher (Qoheleth) is engaged throughout Ecclesiastes 7 in a broader, difficult philosophical search for wisdom amid a fallen and disappointing world. 7:28's famously difficult statement about finding one man in a thousand but no corresponding woman is widely read by many commentators not as a literal claim about the moral quality of women as a class, but as a rueful, almost self-deprecating comment on how rare wisdom itself is to find in anyone. It is framed with a specific rhetorical structure common to ancient wisdom literature's use of exaggerated numerical proportions to make a point about universal human frailty, including, likely, an implied self-critical comment about the Preacher's own extensive experience with many wives and concubines (per Ecclesiastes 2:8) and his failure to find lasting wisdom or satisfaction there. This is a considerably narrower and more qualified statement than a blanket claim about women in general, which would sit in flat contradiction not only with Proverbs 18:22 but with Proverbs 31's own extended, warmly admiring portrait of an excellent wife.

## Ethical Tensions

### 15. Wine as 'a mocker' -- vs. gladdening the heart, Cana, and Paul's advice to Timothy

**References:** Proverbs 20:1; Proverbs 23:29-35; Psalm 104:15; John 2:1-11; 1 Timothy 5:23

**The claim:** Proverbs repeatedly and vividly warns against wine and strong drink: 'Wine is a mocker, strong drink a brawler, and whoever is led astray by it is not wise' (20:1), reinforced by an extended, graphic warning against drunkenness in 23:29-35. Yet Psalm 104:15 describes wine as one of God's good gifts that 'gladdens the heart of man,' Jesus' first recorded miracle involves producing an abundance of good wine for a wedding celebration (John 2:1-11), and Paul explicitly instructs Timothy to 'use a little wine for the sake of your stomach' (1 Timothy 5:23) -- a range of positive scriptural references to the same substance Proverbs warns against.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of these passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing wine's moderate, proper use from its abuse in drunkenness, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Proverbs' warnings, read carefully in their own context, as directed specifically against drunkenness and its consequences (23:29-35's extended description is explicitly about the physical and social effects of excessive drinking, not a categorical prohibition on wine's existence or moderate use) rather than against wine itself as inherently evil -- a distinction consistent with the wider canon's own pattern of treating wine as a genuinely good, God-given gift capable of serious misuse, exactly the same pattern Scripture applies to food, wealth, sexuality, and many other good things capable of becoming destructive when abused (a theme this project has addressed in other contexts, such as the ethics of wealth). Psalm 104:15, John 2, and 1 Timothy 5:23 all describe wine's proper, moderate, or medicinal use approvingly, while Proverbs 20:1 and 23:29-35 describe its abuse and resulting folly disapprovingly -- both stances describe the same substance without contradiction, since neither passage claims to be making an exhaustive, unqualified statement about every possible use of wine.

### 16. 'The rod' of discipline -- vs. the New Testament's gentler counsel to fathers

**References:** Proverbs 13:24; Proverbs 23:13-14; Ephesians 6:4

**The claim:** Proverbs repeatedly endorses physical discipline of children in strong terms: 'Whoever spares the rod hates his son, but he who loves him is diligent to discipline him' (13:24), and 'Do not withhold discipline from a child; if you strike him with a rod, he will not die... you will save his soul from Sheol' (23:13-14). The New Testament, by contrast, instructs fathers, 'do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord' (Ephesians 6:4), language some read as urging a gentler approach in some tension with Proverbs' more forceful endorsement of physical correction.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Proverbs' rod language and Ephesians 6:4 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading both texts as addressing complementary aspects (the necessity, and the manner, of discipline) of the same underlying principle, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not read these as contradictory but as complementary halves of a single, coherent approach to child-rearing: Proverbs' 'rod' language is generally read, in its ancient Near Eastern context, as endorsing appropriate, proportionate physical correction as one legitimate tool of discipline (not as license for abusive or excessive punishment, a distinction the same wisdom tradition elsewhere assumes through its broader emphasis on proportionate justice), while Ephesians 6:4's warning against 'provoking to anger' addresses the manner and spirit in which any form of discipline -- physical or otherwise -- is carried out, warning specifically against harsh, arbitrary, or embittering correction that damages rather than trains a child's character. Read together, the two texts are generally understood as jointly commending real, meaningful discipline (against a permissive extreme that 'spares the rod' entirely, per Proverbs) exercised without cruelty, favoritism, or provoking anger (against an abusive extreme, per Ephesians) -- both texts warning against different failure modes on either side of the same underlying principle of loving, purposeful correction, rather than offering competing philosophies of child-rearing.

### 17. Does the Bible condemn gambling, given its own approving use of casting lots?

**References:** Proverbs 10:4; Proverbs 16:33; Proverbs 13:11; Joshua 18:10; Acts 1:26

**The claim:** Proverbs 10:4 credits diligent, hardworking effort, not chance, as the reliable path to wealth ('he becometh poor that dealeth with a slack hand: but the hand of the diligent maketh rich'), and 13:11 similarly warns that 'wealth gotten by vanity shall be diminished.' Yet Scripture elsewhere describes the casting of lots -- a practice involving chance -- used approvingly for consequential decisions, including dividing the promised land (Joshua 18:10) and selecting Judas's apostolic replacement (Acts 1:26), with Proverbs 16:33 itself affirming that 'the lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These texts address two different practices that both involve an element of chance but serve entirely different purposes: casting lots in Scripture is consistently used as a method of seeking and discerning God's own guidance or verdict in a specific decision (dividing land fairly, identifying a guilty party, selecting a successor), understood as a means by which God himself, who governs even the fall of the lot (16:33), reveals his will -- not a means of acquiring personal wealth or advantage through risking one's own resources for potential gain. Proverbs' warnings against seeking wealth through 'vanity' or a 'slack hand' target exactly this second category -- the pursuit of unearned gain through chance, risk, or shortcuts as a wealth-building strategy, which the wisdom tradition consistently discourages in favor of diligent, productive labor. The Bible's approving use of lot-casting for decision-making and its consistent disapproval of chance-based wealth-seeking are addressing different activities, not offering conflicting verdicts on the same one.

## Literary Composition

### 18. Multiple named authors within a single book -- Solomon, Agur, and King Lemuel

**References:** Proverbs 1:1; Proverbs 10:1; Proverbs 25:1; Proverbs 30:1; Proverbs 31:1

**The claim:** Proverbs is repeatedly introduced and re-introduced as 'the proverbs of Solomon' (1:1, 10:1, and again at 25:1, 'these too are proverbs of Solomon'). Yet chapter 30 is explicitly headed 'the words of Agur son of Jakeh' (30:1), an otherwise unknown figure, and chapter 31 is headed 'the words of King Lemuel' (31:1), a king not identified anywhere else in Scripture -- raising a question about whether the book's overall attribution to Solomon is undermined by these two clearly distinct sections.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each section heading (1:1, 10:1, 25:1, 30:1, 31:1) exactly as given, preserving the book's own explicit, multi-author structure without harmonizing it into a single-author claim; there is no translation variance on this point.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction requiring reconciliation, but a straightforward feature of the book's own self-declared, composite structure, which the text itself makes entirely explicit at each point of transition rather than concealing or glossing over -- the book never claims Solomon personally authored every single word within it, but rather presents itself, through its own repeated internal headings, as a carefully assembled anthology combining Solomon's own extensive body of proverbial wisdom (the largest single component, and the material 1 Kings 4:32 explicitly credits him with producing in vast quantity) together with additional wisdom material from other named sages, compiled and arranged, according to Jewish tradition and the book's own internal evidence (25:1's note that Hezekiah's officials 'copied' additional Solomonic proverbs generations after Solomon's own lifetime), over an extended period. This composite, multi-author structure, openly signaled by the text's own section headings, is a feature of the book's self-understanding, not an inconsistency for a reader to resolve.

### 19. 'A way that seems right to a man' -- the same proverb, repeated twice in the book

**References:** Proverbs 14:12; Proverbs 16:25

**The claim:** Proverbs 14:12 and 16:25 are word-for-word identical (or nearly so, depending on translation): 'There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death.' The same warning appears twice within the same book, in two entirely different surrounding contexts, without comment or cross-reference.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses identically or nearly so, as given in the Hebrew, without commenting on or removing the repetition; this is a feature of the book's own compositional history, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as an error or a contradiction, but as an ordinary and well-attested feature of how Proverbs' individual sayings were collected and arranged -- as an anthology drawing on multiple originally separate collections of wisdom sayings (addressed in entry 10 above regarding the book's composite, multi-author structure), some memorable proverbs recur at more than one point where the compiler judged them relevant to the surrounding material, a practice with some parallel in the reuse of the same psalm material in two places within the Psalter (addressed in the companion Psalms document regarding Psalm 14 and Psalm 53). A proverb's repetition within its own book simply underscores its importance and wide applicability across different specific contexts, rather than constituting two competing or conflicting claims.

## Numbers and Text

### 20. Solomon's 3,000 proverbs -- yet this book contains roughly 915

**References:** 1 Kings 4:32; Proverbs (entire book)

**The claim:** 1 Kings 4:32 states that Solomon 'spoke 3,000 proverbs, and his songs were 1,005.' The book of Proverbs itself, however, contains roughly 915 individual proverbial sayings across its 31 chapters -- considerably fewer than the 3,000 attributed to Solomon in Kings, and the book itself, as addressed in entry 10 above, explicitly credits a meaningful portion of its content to authors other than Solomon (Agur and Lemuel).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Kings 4:32's '3,000 proverbs' consistently, and the book of Proverbs' own actual content is the same across all ten translations (differing only in how individual verses are divided or numbered in minor cases); the resolution is a matter of recognizing a curated anthology versus a complete lifetime output, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, since the book of Proverbs at no point claims to be an exhaustive, complete transcript of every proverb Solomon ever spoke -- 1 Kings 4:32 describes the full scope of Solomon's prolific output during his lifetime, while the book of Proverbs represents a later, deliberately curated anthology (addressed in entry 10 above, including the explicit note at 25:1 that some material was compiled generations after Solomon by Hezekiah's officials) selecting and preserving a substantial but partial portion of that output, alongside material from other sages, for its own specific didactic and liturgical purposes. A curated selection of roughly 915 sayings from a reported original output of 3,000, combined with additional material from other named contributors, is fully consistent with both figures being accurate descriptions of different things (Solomon's total lifetime output, versus this book's own more limited, selective anthology), not competing claims about the same quantity.
