# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Amos

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical
and textual questions involving the book of Amos, either entirely internal to the book or in
comparison with James, Leviticus, Acts, Zechariah, Deuteronomy, and Exodus. For each item, this
document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically
renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how
sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly
reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's most significant entry:** Entry 4 (Amos 9:11-12 and Acts 15:16-18)
documents one of the clearest and most consequential Hebrew/Greek textual variants addressed
anywhere in this project -- the entire argument for admitting Gentiles into the church without
circumcision at the Jerusalem Council rests specifically on the Septuagint's Greek wording of
this verse, which differs substantially from the Masoretic Hebrew text underlying every Old
Testament translation in this survey.

**Verification status:** Entries 4 and 5 (the Amos 9:11-12/Acts 15 variant, and the Amos
7:14 tense question) were both checked directly against multiple sources, including an
academic dissertation specifically addressing James's use of Amos at the Jerusalem Council.
The remaining entries are theological and historical questions where all ten translations
render the same underlying text and the harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of
wording, several of which cross-reference fuller treatments already published elsewhere in
this project.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'Does disaster come to a city, unless the LORD has done it?'

**References:** Amos 3:6; James 1:13

**The claim:** Amos 3:6 asks rhetorically, 'Does disaster come to a city, unless the LORD has done it?' -- a strong statement of divine sovereignty over calamity, connecting to the broader question addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (Isaiah 45:7, Lamentations 3:37-38, Ezekiel 20:25) regarding whether Scripture consistently distinguishes God's sovereignty over calamity and judgment from the claim, denied by James 1:13, that God directly tempts or authors moral evil.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Amos 3:6 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same distinction addressed at greater length in the companion Isaiah and Lamentations documents, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Isaiah and Lamentations documents, both addressing the closely parallel language of Isaiah 45:7 and Lamentations 3:37-38. As with those cases, the Hebrew term for 'disaster' here (ra'ah) covers calamity, misfortune, and judgment rather than exclusively moral evil, and the verse -- occurring in the specific context of Amos's opening argument that Israel's coming judgment is neither arbitrary nor avoidable, but the predictable consequence of a covenant relationship in which God has repeatedly warned and disciplined his people (3:1-8 as a whole builds a chain of cause-and-effect examples culminating in this rhetorical question) -- is best read as affirming God's comprehensive sovereignty over historical judgment and calamity, not a claim that God personally originates or tempts anyone toward moral wickedness, which is the specific claim James 1:13 denies.

### 2. 'I hate, I despise your feasts... let justice roll down like waters'

**References:** Amos 5:21-24; Leviticus 1-7

**The claim:** Amos 5:21-24 records one of the most forceful prophetic critiques of empty ritual in the Bible: 'I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies... take away from me the noise of your songs... but let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream' -- connecting to the same general tension addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (Isaiah 1:11-17, Hosea 6:6, 1 Samuel 15:22, Jeremiah 7:22-23) regarding whether such prophetic language is compatible with the Torah's own extensive sacrificial and festival legislation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Amos 5:21-24 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same emphatic-priority reading addressed at greater length in the companion Isaiah, Hosea, 1 Samuel, and Jeremiah documents, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Isaiah, Hosea, 1 Samuel, and Jeremiah documents, all pointing to the same resolution: this is best read as a recognized pattern of emphatic Hebrew relative-priority language directed against ritual observance divorced from genuine covenant faithfulness and social justice, rather than a considered rejection of festival worship as such. Amos's own specific context makes this especially clear -- the immediately preceding chapters (2:6-8, 4:1, 5:11-12) detail specific, concrete social injustices (exploitation of the poor, corrupt courts, oppressive taxation) that Amos identifies as the actual reason Israel's continued ritual observance has become detestable to God, not a claim that ritual worship itself, rightly practiced alongside genuine justice, would be equally condemned.

### 3. 'Are you not like the Cushites to me?' -- God's providential governance of other nations' histories, alongside Israel's unique election

**References:** Amos 9:7; Deuteronomy 7:6-8; Exodus 19:5-6

**The claim:** Amos 9:7 has God ask rhetorically, 'Are you not like the Cushites to me, O people of Israel?... Did I not bring up Israel from the land of Egypt, and the Philistines from Caphtor and the Syrians from Kir?' -- a remarkable statement attributing the historical migrations of Israel's own enemies (the Philistines and Syrians) to God's own providential governance, using language paralleling Israel's own celebrated Exodus. This raises a question about how this squares with Israel's own emphatically unique, chosen covenant status affirmed elsewhere (Deuteronomy 7:6-8, Exodus 19:5-6, 'you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Amos 9:7 and the Deuteronomy/Exodus election passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the verse's rhetorical function of reinforcing covenant accountability rather than denying unique election, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as a denial or diminishment of Israel's genuinely unique covenant election (which Amos's own book elsewhere clearly presupposes as the very basis for Israel's heightened accountability, 3:2, 'you only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities') but as a pointed corrective, in the same vein as 5:18-20 addressed in entry 7 above, against any presumption that election guarantees automatic favor regardless of covenant faithfulness. Amos 9:7 affirms that God's sovereign, providential governance extends over all nations' histories, not merely Israel's -- a truth entirely compatible with, and in the immediate context specifically employed to reinforce rather than undermine, Israel's own distinct covenant calling and its accompanying, heightened responsibility (3:2 makes explicit that unique election increases rather than removes accountability). The verse functions rhetorically to strip away any assumption that Israel's special relationship with God exempts it from judgment, precisely because that same God is shown to be sovereign over every nation's history, not a tribal deity uniquely and unconditionally partial to Israel regardless of its conduct.

## Numbers and Text

### 4. 'I will raise up the booth of David' -- and a major Hebrew/Greek textual variant with real theological consequences

**References:** Amos 9:11-12; Acts 15:16-18

**The claim:** Amos 9:11-12, in the Masoretic Hebrew text underlying all ten translations' Old Testament rendering, promises that when God restores 'the booth of David that is fallen,' Israel will 'possess the remnant of Edom and all the nations who are called by my name.' James, at the Jerusalem Council, quotes this same passage (Acts 15:16-17) to settle the debate over Gentile inclusion -- but his quotation, following the Greek Septuagint rather than the Hebrew, reads differently: 'that the remnant of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the Gentiles who are called by my name.' The differences are substantial: 'possess' (Hebrew yirshu) becomes 'may seek' (Greek ekzetesosin), and 'Edom' (Hebrew edom) becomes 'mankind' (Greek anthropoi) -- and James's entire argument for admitting Gentiles without circumcision depends specifically on the Greek reading, not the Hebrew one a modern reader consulting an Old Testament translation would actually find at Amos 9:12.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Amos 9:11-12 following the Masoretic Hebrew ('possess the remnant of Edom'), and Acts 15:16-18 following the New Testament's own Greek text, which itself follows the Septuagint's reading ('the remnant of mankind may seek the Lord') -- meaning all ten translations preserve, rather than resolve, this genuine Hebrew/Greek textual difference, since Old and New Testament translation committees do not harmonize wording across the testaments; this is one of the clearest cases in this project of an entire major New Testament theological argument (Gentile inclusion without circumcision) resting specifically on the Septuagint's wording of an Old Testament verse rather than the Hebrew text a reader would find in that same verse's own Old Testament translation.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most textually and theologically significant variants addressed anywhere in this project, and it is well documented rather than speculative. The Hebrew consonants behind 'Edom' (אדום) and 'mankind/humanity' (אדם) differ by only one letter, the final vav, and the Hebrew verbs for 'to possess/inherit' (yarash) and 'to seek' (darash) are similarly close in form. These are differences plausibly arising from either an ancient scribal variant in the Hebrew text underlying the Septuagint's translators, or from the Septuagint translators' own interpretive rendering of an admittedly difficult Hebrew original. Most commentators note that James's argument does not actually require treating the Septuagint's wording as a corrected or superior text superseding the Hebrew, a question this project does not adjudicate. Several instead read the two versions as complementary rather than strictly contradictory. 'Possessing' Edom and the nations, the Hebrew sense, can itself be understood -- in the specific context of God's stated purpose of blessing 'all the nations who are called by my name' at the end of both versions of the verse -- as being accomplished precisely through those nations coming to seek and know the Lord, the Septuagint's sense, rather than through military conquest. Edom itself is widely read elsewhere in the prophetic corpus (Obadiah, Ezekiel 35-36) as a recurring symbolic representative for the hostile nations generally, which some scholars hold supports reading 'possessing Edom' and 'the remnant of mankind seeking the Lord' as two compatible descriptions of the same ultimate outcome: the incorporation of the nations, rather than their military subjugation, into God's own people.

### 5. 'I was no prophet' -- past tense, or present? A real translation difference bearing on what Amos actually denies

**References:** Amos 7:14-15

**The claim:** When confronted by the priest Amaziah and told to stop prophesying at Bethel, Amos responds (in most translations) in the past tense: 'I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son, but I was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs... and the LORD took me from following the flock and said to me, Go, prophesy to my people Israel' (7:14-15). A minority of translations instead render this in the present tense: 'I am not a prophet, nor am I the son of a prophet' -- which, read in isolation, could suggest Amos denies being a prophet even in the very moment he is actively engaged in prophesying, an apparent self-contradiction.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders present tense: 'I am not a prophet [by profession], nor am I a prophet's son' -- joining NASB and LSB as a genuine exception to the majority past-tense rendering, rather than following the majority pattern (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders past tense: 'I was not a prophet or the son of a prophet' (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders past tense: 'I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son' (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders past tense: 'I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son' (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders past tense, matching the KJV (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB renders present tense: 'I am not a prophet, nor am I the son of a prophet' -- a notable departure from the past-tense rendering used by most other translations checked (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders present tense: 'I am not a prophet, nor am I the son of a prophet,' matching NASB's structure exactly -- a genuine exception to the majority past-tense rendering, not the majority pattern (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders past tense: 'I was neither a prophet nor the son of a prophet' (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders present tense: 'I'm not a professional prophet, and I was never trained to be one,' a hybrid rendering (present tense for the first clause, past tense for the second).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders past tense: 'I was no prophet, nor was I a son of a prophet, but I was a sheepbreeder and a tender of sycamore fruit' (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording, following the majority pattern.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators regard the past-tense rendering as correct and as resolving any apparent tension entirely. The underlying Hebrew here uses a construction without an explicit verb, a 'nominal sentence,' leaving the tense to be inferred from context. The great majority of Hebrew grammarians and commentators hold that the natural reading supplies a past tense, matching the following verb -- 'and the LORD took me,' clearly past tense, describing a specific prior calling. Amos's point, so read, is that he was not previously a professional or hereditary prophet, unlike the trained 'sons of the prophets' or court-affiliated prophetic guilds Amaziah may have assumed he belonged to, but a herdsman whom God specifically and directly called out of that ordinary occupation into the prophetic office. That office he was now, in the present moment of speaking, actively and legitimately exercising -- not a denial of his current prophetic status or authority at all, but an explanation of his unconventional path into it. The minority present-tense rendering, some commentators have noted, would create exactly the kind of self-contradiction Amos's own following statement (verse 15, describing his direct divine commissioning) makes clear he did not intend.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 6. 'Two years before the earthquake' -- an unusually specific historical anchor, with possible archaeological corroboration

**References:** Amos 1:1; Zechariah 14:5

**The claim:** Amos 1:1 dates the prophet's ministry unusually precisely: 'two years before the earthquake,' during the reigns of Uzziah of Judah and Jeroboam II of Israel. This specific earthquake is referenced again, generations later, in Zechariah 14:5, which describes people fleeing 'as you fled from the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah' -- treating it as a well-remembered historical event significant enough to still be a common point of reference more than two centuries afterward.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Amos 1:1 and Zechariah 14:5 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a matter of historical corroboration, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable case of a specific, datable historical detail with some independent archaeological support. Excavations at several sites in the region, including Hazor, have identified a significant destruction layer dated by archaeologists on independent grounds to approximately 760 BC, within the range scholars generally assign to Uzziah's reign -- some archaeologists and biblical historians have proposed this destruction layer as physical evidence of the same earthquake referenced in both Amos and Zechariah, though this identification, like most such archaeological correlations, remains a matter of scholarly judgment rather than absolute proof. This entry is included as an example of the kind of specific, checkable historical claim this project has addressed at several other points (Belshazzar's historicity in the companion Daniel document being the most extensively confirmed case), where the biblical text's own specificity provides a point of contact with independent historical and archaeological inquiry.

## Theological Interpretation

### 7. 'Why would you have the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light'

**References:** Amos 5:18-20

**The claim:** Amos 5:18-20 delivers a striking corrective to what appears to have been a popular expectation among Amos's audience: 'Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! Why would you have the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light... Is not the day of the LORD darkness, and not light, and gloom with no brightness in it?' -- directly reversing an apparently widespread assumption that this coming day would bring vindication and blessing specifically for Israel, rather than judgment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Amos 5:18-20 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses the prophet's own corrective to a popular theological assumption, not a contradiction between two texts, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction between two competing biblical claims, but as the prophet's own deliberate theological corrective to a popular misunderstanding current among his own audience -- Amos's contemporaries evidently anticipated 'the day of the LORD' (a concept already established in Israelite religious expectation as a time of God's decisive intervention) as an automatically favorable event for Israel specifically, simply by virtue of their covenant identity, regardless of their actual covenant faithfulness. Amos's corrective insists that the same day of divine intervention that unfaithful nations might reasonably fear will, for a covenant people who have themselves persistently violated that covenant through injustice and empty ritual (the burden of the book's preceding chapters), bring the same darkness and judgment rather than automatic vindication -- a pattern of prophetic correction to popular theological presumption addressed in comparable terms at several points elsewhere in the prophetic literature (compare Amos 9:7, addressed in entry 3 below, similarly challenging any assumption of automatic, unconditional favored status).
