# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 John

This document catalogs 4 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 1 John, either
entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Hebrews and 2 Timothy
documents, and 2 Thessalonians and Matthew. For each item, this document states the claim as
it is typically raised, notes how the tracked translations render the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim
appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entry:** Entry 1 addresses one of the most widely
discussed internal tensions in the entire New Testament -- whether a genuine believer sins
(1:8, 10) or does not, and cannot, sin (3:6, 9) -- presenting the standard "habitual sin"
resolution alongside a genuine scholarly complication some careful Greek grammarians raise
against that very resolution, rather than treating the question as fully and cleanly settled.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1 and 2 are presented with genuine scholarly diversity of
opinion, including, in entry 1's case, disagreement over whether the most commonly offered
resolution itself is linguistically sound.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 2 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing the full range of scholarly positions on each question, including specific named
grammarians and theologians on more than one side of each debate. Entry 3 was checked to
confirm the specific, limited scope of the term "antichrist" within the New Testament.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. 'If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves' -- vs. 'No one born of God makes a practice of sinning... he cannot sin'

*Entry ID: 1JOHN-001*

**References:** 1 John 1:8, 10; 1 John 3:6, 9

**The claim:** 1 John 1:8 states, 'If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us,' and 1:10 adds, 'If we say we have not sinned, we make him a liar' -- both explicitly affirming that genuine believers do, in fact, sin. Yet 1 John 3:6 states, 'No one who abides in him keeps on sinning,' and 3:9 goes further: 'No one born of God makes a practice of sinning, for God's seed abides in him; and he cannot keep on sinning, because he has been born of God' -- language that, read as an absolute claim, appears to directly contradict 1:8 and 10 by asserting that a true, born-again believer literally does not, or even cannot, sin at all. This is one of the most widely discussed internal tensions in the entire New Testament, with substantial implications for the doctrine of sanctification and Christian assurance.

**How the translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, NKJV, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** Several modern translations (NIV, NASB, ESV, and others) render 3:6 and 3:9 with explicit continuous or habitual-action language ('keeps on sinning,' 'practices sin'), directly reflecting the standard scholarly resolution in the translation itself, while KJV and a few others render the verses more simply, leaving the interpretive work more visibly to the reader; this reflects a genuine translation choice regarding a contested point of Greek grammar and interpretation, not a difference resolved uniformly across all translations.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 3:6 as 'keeps on sinning' but 3:9 as simply 'makes a practice of sinning,' both reflecting the habitual-sin reading in translation.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 3:6 and 3:9 more simply as 'sinneth not' and 'doth not commit sin,' without making the habitual/continuous distinction explicit in the wording itself, leaving the interpretive question more open to the English reader.
- **NASB:** NASB similarly renders 'practices sin' and 'no one who is born of God practices sin,' also reflecting this same interpretation directly in translation.
- **NIV:** NIV renders 3:6 and 3:9 with explicit continuous-action language ('No one who continues to sin... no one who is born of God will continue to sin'), reflecting the habitual-sin interpretation directly in its translation choice.
- **The "simple" camp, extended (matching KJV's approach of leaving the interpretive question open):** confirmed directly, **the AKJV** ("Whoever stays in him sins not"), **the ASV** ("sinneth not"), **the CPDV** ("does not sin"), **Darby** ("does not sin"), **the Douay-Rheims Bible** ("sinneth not," matching KJV almost exactly), **Smith's Literal Translation** ("sins not"), **Webster's Bible** ("sinneth not," matching KJV exactly), **the WEB** ("doesn't sin"), **the NET Bible** ("does not sin"), and **Young's Literal Translation** ("doth not sin") -- ten translations joining KJV's simpler rendering rather than the explicit habitual-action language. **RSV is also confirmed directly** in this same simple camp, checked against its own text: "No one who abides in him sins" (3:6) and "No one born of God commits sin... he cannot sin because he is born of God" (3:9), with no explicit habitual/continuous-action language.
- **The "habitual/continuous" camp, extended:** confirmed directly, **the BSB** ("No one who remains in Him keeps on sinning. No one who continues to sin...") and **the ERV** ("does not continue to sin. If they continue to sin...") -- both joining NIV's explicit continuous-action reading.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and actively debated passage, and this entry presents the standard resolution along with a genuine scholarly complication some raise against it, rather than a single, fully settled answer. The standard, most widely offered resolution distinguishes occasional, isolated acts of sin, which 1:8 and 10 affirm every believer, without exception, continues to commit, from habitual, unrepentant sin as a settled, defining pattern or direction of life, which 3:6 and 9 deny characterizes someone who is genuinely 'born of God' or 'abiding in him.' Many commentators, including John Stott, Sam Storms, Kenneth Wuest, and Gleason Archer, argue this distinction is supported by the Greek present tense verbs John uses in 3:6 and 9, which in this reading denote continuous, ongoing, or habitual action -- 'keeps on sinning,' 'makes a practice of sinning' -- rather than any single instance of sin. On this reading, John's own point is that a genuine believer's fundamental life-direction has decisively changed from a settled pattern of sin toward a new, godly direction, even while specific individual lapses, addressed in 1:8-10 and again at 2:1-2's provision for an 'advocate' when 'anyone does sin,' remain a real, ongoing part of the Christian life. However, this project also notes that this specific grammatical solution is itself genuinely disputed among careful Greek scholars. Some, including Zane Hodges and, in different terms, Stephen Smalley, argue the present tense alone cannot bear the weight of 'habitual' or 'continuous' action this reading requires, noting that 1 John itself uses the same present tense elsewhere (5:16) for a specific, individual act of sin rather than a habitual pattern -- which would undercut applying the 'habitual sin' reading consistently across the letter. An alternative resolution some scholars offer distinguishes the believer's fundamentally new, regenerate nature received from God, which 3:9, addressing 'God's seed' abiding within, describes as incapable of sin as such, from the believer's own remaining, ongoing struggle and experience, which 1:8-10 addresses. Both statements, on this view, are simultaneously true of the same person considered from these two different vantage points, rather than resolving the tension solely through Greek verb tense. This project does not adjudicate definitively between these approaches, noting that this remains a substantively discussed passage even among scholars who agree there is no genuine, unresolvable contradiction.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. 'Sin that leads to death' vs. 'sin not leading to death' -- a genuinely mysterious, actively debated distinction

*Entry ID: 1JOHN-002*

**References:** 1 John 5:16-17

**The claim:** 1 John 5:16-17 introduces a distinction its own author apparently expected his readers to understand without further explanation: 'If anyone sees his brother committing a sin not leading to death, he shall ask, and God will give him life... There is sin that leads to death; I do not say that one should pray for that. All wrongdoing is sin, but there is sin that does not lead to death.' John never specifies what this 'sin that leads to death' actually is, generating centuries of interpretive speculation.

**How the translations render it:** All 27 tracked translations render 1 John 5:16-17 plainly and consistently as given, preserving the same undefined distinction; the identity of the 'sin unto death' remains genuinely disputed regardless of translation, and is not resolved by any of the 27 tracked translations' specific wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and extensively debated passage, and this entry presents the leading proposals rather than a single confident answer, noting directly that no surviving manuscript omits or varies this passage despite its acknowledged difficulty. The most commonly proposed identification connects this 'sin unto death' to the specific, decisive apostasy this same letter addresses at length elsewhere -- a deliberate, final rejection of Jesus as the Christ come in the flesh (the specific doctrinal test this letter repeatedly applies to distinguish true believers from the false teachers who had departed from the community, 2:18-23, 4:1-3) -- understood as a conscious, complete renunciation of the faith rather than any specific category of especially severe moral sin (murder, sexual sin, or similar), a reading some connect to Jesus' own teaching on blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (Matthew 12:31-32) and to the severe warnings against apostasy addressed at length in the companion Hebrews chapter (6:4-6, 10:26-31). Other commentators, connecting the phrase to the Old Testament's own legal distinction between capital and non-capital offenses (compare Deuteronomy's own use of similar 'sin unto death' language for crimes carrying the death penalty), read the distinction more generally as separating especially serious sin from more ordinary transgression, without specifying a single, particular sin as the sole referent. A further reading holds that 'sin unto death' describes not a specific act at all, but an ongoing, settled state or condition of persistent, unrepentant sin characterizing someone who was never a genuine believer to begin with, connecting to and potentially informed by the tension addressed in entry 1 above regarding this letter's own treatment of sin and genuine spiritual birth. This project does not adjudicate between these proposals, noting this remains one of the most genuinely mysterious individual passages in the entire New Testament, a difficulty John's original readers, evidently already familiar with the specific reference, did not share.

### 3. 'Many antichrists' -- vs. later, popular expectation of a single, final 'the Antichrist'

*Entry ID: 1JOHN-003*

**References:** 1 John 2:18; 1 John 4:1-3; 2 Thessalonians 2:1-12

**The claim:** 1 John 2:18 states, 'Children, it is the last hour, and as you have heard that antichrist is coming, so now many antichrists have come. Therefore we know that it is the last hour' -- using the term in the plural, describing numerous figures already present in John's own historical moment (connected, in context, to false teachers who had departed from the church, 2:19). This differs from later, popular Christian expectation (though this specific term is not actually used in the book of Revelation, and is found nowhere else in the New Testament outside John's own letters) of a single, final, climactic 'the Antichrist' figure appearing at the very end of history, a popular expectation more directly connected to 2 Thessalonians 2's 'man of lawlessness' (addressed at length in its own companion chapter) and Revelation's own beast imagery.

**How the translations render it:** All 27 tracked translations render 1 John 2:18, 22, and 4:3 plainly and consistently as given, using the plural 'antichrists' and John's own present-tense framing; none of the 27 tracked translations introduces a singular, future 'the Antichrist' figure into these specific verses, since the text itself does not describe one. This entry addresses a matter of popular synthesis across different New Testament texts, not a contradiction, and is not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable case, worth clarifying directly, of popular usage combining and applying a specific term in a way that goes beyond its own actual, limited New Testament usage. The term 'antichrist' (Greek antichristos) appears only in John's own letters (1 John 2:18, 22; 4:3; 2 John 7) and nowhere else in the New Testament, including nowhere in the book of Revelation, which never uses this specific term for the beast or any other end-times figure it describes. John's own usage is explicitly and consistently plural and present-tense, describing numerous individuals already active in his own historical moment who denied Jesus' true identity (2:22, 'who is the liar but he who denies that Jesus is the Christ') or that Christ had come 'in the flesh' (4:2-3) -- a recurring pattern of opposition to Christ John explicitly connects to 'the last hour' in a way most commentators read as describing an ongoing feature of the entire period between Christ's first and second coming (addressed in a similar sense elsewhere in this project regarding 'the last days' language in the companion 2 Timothy chapter), not a single, uniquely climactic figure reserved for history's very end. The popular composite figure often called simply 'the Antichrist,' understood as a single, final, especially significant end-times opponent of Christ, more directly draws on and combines other New Testament material -- especially 2 Thessalonians 2's 'man of lawlessness' and Revelation's beast imagery -- with John's own specific term, a synthesis that developed in later Christian tradition and popular usage rather than being explicitly present as such within John's own letters themselves.

### 4. 'These three are one' -- the Comma Johanneum, the New Testament's most consequential Trinitarian textual variant

*Entry ID: 1JOHN-004*

**References:** 1 John 5:7-8

**The claim:** 1 John 5:7-8 contains one of the New Testament's most historically significant textual variants, traditionally called the Comma Johanneum ('the Johannine clause'). In its longer form, the passage contains explicit Trinitarian language naming the Father, Word (Son), and Holy Ghost as one; the overwhelming majority of Greek manuscripts, including all the earliest and most reliable ones, lack this clause entirely. The full manuscript history -- including the clause's documented path into the Textus Receptus via Erasmus's 1522 third edition -- and how each of the 27 tracked translations renders the passage, is addressed at length in the Manuscript and Translation Differences chapter (see its first entry on 1 John).

**How the translations render it:** See the Manuscript and Translation Differences chapter's entry on this passage for the full translation-by-translation breakdown, including a genuine surprise finding for the CPDV (a modern Catholic translation that, contrary to a widely repeated claim, still includes the clause).

**Reconciliation:** This is treated in this project not as a contradiction between translations but as the most significant confirmed manuscript-based textual variant addressed anywhere in this document, comparable in kind to Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11 addressed in their own chapters, though even more consequential doctrinally since it is the only variant in this entire project that directly supplies explicit Trinitarian language not otherwise present in that exact form anywhere else in Scripture. This project takes no position on which reading is original, noting only that the doctrine of the Trinity does not depend on this passage and is taught and defended throughout Scripture on other grounds even where this specific clause is absent.
