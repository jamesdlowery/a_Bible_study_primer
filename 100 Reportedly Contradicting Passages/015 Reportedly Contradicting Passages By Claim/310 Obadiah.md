# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Obadiah

This document catalogs 4 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical
and literary questions involving the book of Obadiah, either entirely internal to the book or
in comparison with Jeremiah, 2 Kings, 2 Chronicles, Psalms, Lamentations, Ezekiel, and 1
Kings. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the
**KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own
wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives
the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Obadiah is the shortest book in the entire Old Testament -- a single
chapter of 21 verses. This document has 4 entries, the fewest of any book addressed in this
series so far, reflecting the genuinely limited scope of material a book this short can
support without resorting to weak, manufactured entries. Entries 1 and 2 are directly
connected: the question of whether Obadiah or Jeremiah 49 has priority (entry 1) bears
directly on which historical setting Obadiah's oracle addresses (entry 2).

**A note on candor:** Both entries 1 and 2 are presented with genuine scholarly diversity of
opinion rather than a single forced resolution -- this book's date and literary relationship
to Jeremiah 49 are among the most actively debated questions surrounding any of the Minor
Prophets.

**Verification status:** Entries 1, 2, and 3 were checked against a wide range of sources for
this document, including Mark A. Hassler's peer-reviewed article devoted specifically to Obadiah's
historical setting ("The Setting of Obadiah: When Does the Oracle Concerning Edom
Transpire?" Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society 59, no. 2 (2016): 241-254). The remaining entry addresses a
pattern of corroboration across independent biblical texts rather than a contradiction, and is
not a matter of translation choice.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. A substantially overlapping oracle against Edom, also found in Jeremiah 49

**References:** Obadiah 1:1-9; Jeremiah 49:7-22

**The claim:** Obadiah 1:1-9, roughly the first half of this book's single chapter, closely parallels Jeremiah 49:7-22's own oracle against Edom -- in places sharing nearly identical wording and imagery (compare Obadiah's opening summons to the nations to rise against Edom with Jeremiah 49:14, and Obadiah's mockery of Edom's proverbial wisdom with Jeremiah 49:7) -- raising the same question addressed elsewhere in this project regarding shared prophetic material (the Isaiah 2/Micah 4 'swords into plowshares' oracle, and the Psalm 14/53 doublet): which prophet originated the material, and did one directly borrow from the other?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given, preserving the close verbal overlap without commenting on or resolving the question of literary priority in the text itself; this is a matter of literary and historical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and actively debated question among scholars, and this entry does not force a single resolution, since serious arguments exist on both sides. Those favoring Obadiah's priority (Jeremiah borrowing from Obadiah) note that Obadiah's version of the shared material consistently reads as more original and tightly integrated with its own surrounding verses, while Jeremiah's version shows signs of adaptation and expansion for its own new context (addressed to a different city, Bozrah, rather than the nation of Edom generally); this reading would place Obadiah considerably earlier than Jeremiah's own well-dated sixth-century ministry. Those favoring Jeremiah's priority (Obadiah borrowing from Jeremiah, or both drawing on a still earlier, now-lost common source) note that Jeremiah's oracle can be independently dated with more confidence to a specific point in his own well-attested ministry, and that a directly quoted or adapted oracle would not be unusual for either prophet given the ancient Near Eastern practice (also addressed in this project's companion Isaiah/Micah discussion) of reusing and adapting already-circulating prophetic material. A minority view holds both prophets independently drew on a common, no-longer-extant earlier oracle against Edom, which would explain the material's presence in both books without requiring either prophet to have directly copied the other.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 2. Obadiah's setting: the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem, or an earlier ninth-century conflict?

**References:** Obadiah 1:10-14; 2 Kings 8:20-22; 2 Chronicles 21:8-17; Psalm 137:7; Lamentations 4:21-22; Ezekiel 25:12-14

**The claim:** Obadiah 1:10-14 condemns Edom for standing aloof, gloating, looting, and handing over fugitives during a specific historical day of Judah's or Jerusalem's distress, when foreigners 'cast lots for Jerusalem.' The book itself never names the specific historical event or the reigning king, and this question is directly tied to the literary-priority question addressed in entry 1 above: if Obadiah is dependent on Jeremiah 49, the near-consensus reading identifies the event as Nebuchadnezzar's destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC (a reading strongly supported by Psalm 137:7, Lamentations 4:21-22, and Ezekiel 25:12-14, all independently condemning Edom's specific conduct at that exact event); if Obadiah predates and was used by Jeremiah, the event is more commonly identified with an earlier Philistine-Arab raid on Jerusalem during King Jehoram's reign (2 Kings 8:20-22, 2 Chronicles 21:8-17), in which Edom, having just revolted from Judah's control, is thought likely to have participated.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Obadiah 1:10-14 plainly and consistently as given, without dating the events described or identifying the specific historical occasion in the text itself; this is a matter of historical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question does not admit of a confident resolution independent of entry 1's literary-priority question, and this entry does not force one, consistent with how genuinely disputed dating questions have been handled at several points elsewhere in this project (regarding Joel, addressed in the companion Joel document, whose dating faces a comparably wide range of scholarly proposals). Proponents of the sixth-century setting note that Obadiah's description of the city's fall (lots cast over Jerusalem, extensive plunder, fugitives hunted down and handed over) matches the scale and specific details of the Babylonian destruction far more precisely than the more limited plundering of the royal palace 2 Chronicles 21:16-17 describes for Jehoram's reign, and that 2 Chronicles' own account does not explicitly name Edom as a participant in that earlier raid at all. Proponents of the ninth-century setting note that Obadiah's specific commands regarding future conduct (some read verses 12-14's grammar as prohibitions against not-yet-committed future acts, rather than condemnations of already-completed past ones) would be an odd way to address Edom if Jerusalem had already been reduced to rubble by the time of writing, and that Edom's known, documented revolt from Judah's control during precisely this period provides a plausible, independently attested occasion for exactly the kind of hostility Obadiah describes.

## Names and Identity

### 3. Which Obadiah? A common name shared by at least a dozen other biblical figures

**References:** Obadiah 1:1; 1 Kings 18:3-16

**The claim:** Obadiah 1:1 identifies this book's author simply as 'the vision of Obadiah,' with no father's name, hometown, or reference to any specific king's reign -- unusually sparse even by the standards of the Minor Prophets. 'Obadiah' (meaning 'servant of Yahweh') was itself a common Israelite name, shared by at least twelve other individuals named in the Old Testament, including most notably the righteous royal official who hid a hundred prophets from Queen Jezebel's persecution during Elijah's own ministry (1 Kings 18:3-16) -- raising a question about whether this book's Obadiah is the same person as any of these other figures.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Obadiah 1:1 exactly as given, supplying no further identifying information beyond the bare name; this is a matter of historical identification, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction, but an acknowledged case of genuine historical uncertainty regarding an otherwise entirely unknown figure. Ancient Jewish tradition (reflected in the Babylonian Talmud and other rabbinic sources) identified the prophet with the righteous official of 1 Kings 18, and further elaborated a tradition that he was himself a convert from Edom -- explaining, on this tradition's own terms, why he would be specifically chosen to prophesy Edom's downfall. Most modern scholars regard this identification as a later legendary tradition rather than an historically demonstrable fact, since the book itself supplies no information connecting its author to the earlier royal official, and 'none of [the other Obadiahs named in the Old Testament] seem obviously to be the author named in the book,' as one reference work states plainly. This remains an open question the text itself does not resolve, and readers should hold any specific identification (including the traditional one) as a matter of later tradition rather than a settled scriptural fact.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 4. Obadiah's specific charges against Edom, corroborated across several independent prophetic witnesses

**References:** Obadiah 1:10-14; Psalm 137:7; Lamentations 4:21-22; Ezekiel 25:12-14; Ezekiel 35:1-15

**The claim:** Obadiah's specific charges against Edom -- standing aloof while Jerusalem fell, gloating over Judah's ruin, looting the city, and handing captured fugitives over to their enemies -- are strikingly specific accusations for such a short book to level in isolation, raising a natural question of whether these charges are independently corroborated elsewhere in Scripture or stand alone.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Obadiah 1:10-14 and each of the corroborating passages plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a pattern of corroboration across independent texts, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry is included not because a contradiction exists, but to note, as this project has done at several other points regarding specific prophetic accusations -- the fulfillment of Deuteronomy 28's covenant curses in Lamentations' account of siege-cannibalism, addressed in the companion Lamentations document, is a comparable case -- that Obadiah's specific charges against Edom are independently and consistently corroborated across several other prophetic and poetic texts describing the same general period. Psalm 137:7 records the same accusation of Edomite gloating during Jerusalem's fall: 'Remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem, how they said, Lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations!' Lamentations 4:21-22 addresses Edom directly in terms very close to Obadiah's own. And Ezekiel 25:12-14 and 35:1-15 both deliver extended, independent oracles condemning Edom for taking vengeance against Judah 'at the time of their calamity.' This convergence of independent witnesses describing the same specific national conduct is generally read by scholars as strong internal corroboration for the underlying historical tradition Obadiah addresses, regardless of which specific historical occasion (addressed in entry 2 above) is ultimately in view.
