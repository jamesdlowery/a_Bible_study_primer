# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Habakkuk

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual and
theological questions involving the book of Habakkuk, either entirely internal to the book or
in comparison with Romans, Galatians, and Hebrews. For each item, this document states the
claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages
involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable
the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this book's central crisis:** Entry 3 (God's use of a more wicked nation, Babylon,
to judge a less wicked one, Judah) is the book's own central theological struggle, voiced
honestly through the prophet's two complaints rather than resolved with an easy answer -- this
document presents the book's own extended response rather than supplying an external one.

**Verification status:** Entry 5 (the ancient rabbinic tiqqune sopherim tradition regarding
Habakkuk 1:12) was checked in unusual depth for this document, including a direct check of
whether any of the ten translations in this survey adopt the tradition's proposed alternate
reading (none do) and whether external manuscript evidence (the Septuagint, and the Dead Sea
Scrolls' own Habakkuk commentary) supports the tradition's claim of an original, altered
reading (it does not, a nuance this entry presents candidly). Entry 1 (Habakkuk 2:4's three
New Testament citations) was also checked for translation-specific wording. The remaining
entries are theological and literary questions where all ten translations render the same
underlying text and the harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Interpretation

### 1. 'The righteous shall live by his faith' -- three different New Testament applications of one verse

**References:** Habakkuk 2:4; Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11; Hebrews 10:38-39

**The claim:** Habakkuk 2:4 -- 'the righteous shall live by his faith' -- is quoted directly in three separate New Testament books, each applying it to a somewhat different theological argument: Romans 1:17 uses it to establish justification by faith rather than works as the very theme of the letter; Galatians 3:11 uses it in a more specifically polemical argument that righteousness cannot come through observing the Mosaic Law; Hebrews 10:38-39 uses it in a context emphasizing faithful endurance and perseverance under persecution rather than the initial moment of justifying belief. In Habakkuk's own original context, the verse contrasts the arrogant, self-sufficient Babylonian invader with the righteous person's steady, trusting faithfulness that will sustain them through the coming Babylonian judgment -- a statement about persevering trust amid national crisis, not a formal doctrine of justification as such.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, ESV, NKJV, NLT, RSV2CE:** Most translations render Habakkuk 2:4 with 'faith,' matching the wording Paul draws on in Romans and Galatians, though NIV notably renders 'faithfulness' at this specific verse, closer to the sense of sustained fidelity Hebrews' own citation emphasizes; this reflects the genuine, dual semantic range of the underlying Hebrew term rather than an error in either direction.
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'the righteous one will live by his faith,' with a similar footnote.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'the just shall live by his faith,' the traditional and most historically influential English rendering (the wording that shaped Luther's own reading of Romans 1:17 and, through him, the Protestant Reformation's central doctrinal emphasis).
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'the righteous will live by his faith,' with a footnote noting the Hebrew can also be rendered 'faithfulness.'
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'But the righteous will live by his faith,' matching NASB exactly (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'the righteous person will live by his faithfulness,' explicitly choosing 'faithfulness' over 'faith' at Habakkuk 2:4 itself, closer to the sustained-fidelity sense, while its own citations of the verse in Romans and Galatians retain 'faith,' following Paul's own Greek wording there rather than harmonizing back to the Old Testament term.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as three competing or incompatible interpretations requiring a single correct answer, but as three legitimate facets of a single, richer underlying concept the Hebrew term for 'faith/faithfulness' (emunah) supports -- a term whose semantic range covers both the initial, trusting reliance on God that grounds a right standing before him (the aspect Paul draws out in Romans and Galatians) and the sustained, steady faithfulness or fidelity that perseveres through difficult circumstances (the aspect Hebrews draws out, appropriate to that letter's own concern with readers tempted to abandon their faith under persecution). Habakkuk's own original context already contains the seed of both ideas together -- the righteous person's faith is both the trusting reliance that distinguishes them from the arrogant Babylonian and the sustaining quality that will keep them steady through the coming crisis -- meaning each New Testament writer draws out and emphasizes a genuine dimension of the original Hebrew concept suited to their own letter's specific argument, rather than imposing a meaning foreign to the source text.

### 2. 'Though the fig tree should not blossom' -- affirmed joy amid total agricultural and economic collapse

**References:** Habakkuk 3:17-19

**The claim:** Habakkuk's closing verses affirm confident joy in God even while explicitly describing total agricultural and economic devastation: 'Though the fig tree should not blossom, nor fruit be on the vines, the produce of the olive fail and the fields yield no food, the flock be cut off from the fold and there be no herd in the stalls, yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation' (3:17-18) -- an affirmation of joy that some readers find in tension with the totality of loss being described.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Habakkuk 3:17-19 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing a pattern, addressed at several points elsewhere in this project, of faith and joy that does not depend on changed circumstances, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as one of the clearest examples in Scripture of a pattern addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (regarding the coexistence of genuine lament and genuine, settled hope in Lamentations 3:22-24, Job's own eventual restoration of trust, and several psalms of lament that similarly turn to praise before their circumstances have actually changed) -- a form of faith that does not depend on, or wait for, circumstances to improve before affirming trust in God's character and ultimate salvation. Habakkuk's closing affirmation follows directly from, and is generally read as the intended resolution to, the entire book's preceding struggle: having voiced his complaints honestly (chapters 1-2) and received God's own response, including the reminder of God's past faithfulness recounted in the theophany of 3:3-15, the prophet arrives at a settled trust that does not require every circumstance to be resolved first, modeling a pattern of faith consistent with, rather than contradicting, the book's own central verse addressed in entry 1 above ('the righteous shall live by his faith').

## Theological Tensions

### 3. Using a more wicked nation to judge a less wicked one -- the book's central crisis

**References:** Habakkuk 1:2-4; Habakkuk 1:5-11; Habakkuk 1:12-2:1

**The claim:** Habakkuk opens with the prophet's complaint that God is not acting against the injustice and violence within Judah itself (1:2-4). God's answer (1:5-11) is to announce that he is raising up the Babylonians (Chaldeans) -- described as 'that bitter and hasty nation,' guilty of their own extensive violence and self-deifying pride ('their justice and dignity go forth from themselves,' 1:7) -- as the very instrument of judgment against Judah. Habakkuk's second complaint (1:12-2:1) presses exactly this difficulty: how can God, who is 'of purer eyes than to see evil' (1:13), justify using a nation more violent and wicked than Judah itself as the means of Judah's punishment?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Habakkuk's two complaints and God's two responses plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing that Babylon's instrumental role in judging Judah does not exempt Babylon from separate accountability for its own excesses, addressed later in the same book, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is the book's own central theological crisis, and rather than glossing over it, Habakkuk's entire structure is built around voicing this difficulty honestly and then recording God's own further response to it (2:2-20), which does not deny the Babylonians' own guilt but explicitly promises their own subsequent judgment for exactly the excesses Habakkuk has identified -- an extended series of 'woes' pronounced specifically against Babylonian violence, plunder, and self-deification (2:6-19) -- meaning Babylon's role as an instrument of judgment against Judah does not exempt Babylon from its own accountability for its own excessive violence and pride, addressed in its own right and in its own time. This is the same pattern addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding God's use of imperfect or wicked instruments to accomplish judgment without thereby endorsing or excusing those instruments' own conduct (Assyria as 'the rod of my anger' in the companion Isaiah document, and Jehu's commendation for accomplishing a divinely mandated purpose while separately condemned for his own excessive methods in the companion 2 Kings and Hosea documents) -- God's sovereign use of a nation for a specific judicial purpose is treated throughout Scripture as fully compatible with that same nation's own subsequent accountability for its own independent wrongdoing.

### 4. 'Of purer eyes than to see evil' -- moral purity, or a claim about perceptual limitation?

**References:** Habakkuk 1:13

**The claim:** Habakkuk 1:13 addresses God as one who is 'of purer eyes than to see evil' and who 'cannot look at wrong' -- language that, read as a literal claim about perceptual capacity, would be in tension with God's comprehensive, ongoing knowledge and awareness of all human wrongdoing affirmed throughout the rest of Scripture (his omniscience being a settled and repeatedly stated attribute elsewhere).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Habakkuk 1:13 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing an idiomatic expression of moral revulsion rather than a literal claim about perceptual capacity, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not read as a claim about God's perceptual or cognitive limitations, but as an idiomatic Hebrew expression of moral revulsion and refusal to condone or delight in evil, comparable to an English speaker saying they 'can't bear to look at' something not because they are literally incapable of perceiving it, but because it is morally repugnant to them. Habakkuk's own immediate point, in context, is not a claim that God is unaware of Babylon's violence, but a complaint precisely because God does see it clearly and yet, in Habakkuk's perception at that moment, appears to tolerate it by using Babylon as his instrument -- the verse's rhetorical force depends entirely on God's full awareness of the evil in question, not on any limitation in perceiving it, making the idiomatic reading (moral intolerance of evil, not perceptual blindness to it) the only reading consistent with the passage's own argument.

## Numbers and Text

### 5. 'We shall not die' -- a much-discussed ancient scribal tradition, though not reflected in any of these ten translations

**References:** Habakkuk 1:12

**The claim:** Habakkuk 1:12 reads, in every translation surveyed in this project, 'Are you not from everlasting, O LORD my God, my Holy One? We shall not die.' Ancient rabbinic tradition, however, includes this verse among a small, specifically identified list of eighteen so-called tiqqune sopherim ('corrections of the scribes') -- passages Jewish tradition itself holds were deliberately altered by ancient scribes from an original wording considered too theologically uncomfortable to transmit unaltered. The tradition holds the original reading here was 'You [God] shall not die' (a natural continuation of the preceding question about God's own eternal nature), which scribes are said to have changed to 'we shall not die' (shifting the referent from God to Israel) specifically to avoid even a rhetorical question implying the possibility of God's own death.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Habakkuk 1:12 as 'we shall not die,' consistent with both the Masoretic Hebrew and the earlier Septuagint and Dead Sea Scrolls' independent witnesses to the same reading; none of the ten adopts the alternative reading proposed by the rabbinic tiqqune sopherim tradition, which the earliest available manuscript evidence does not clearly support.

**Reconciliation:** This entry is included as a genuinely fascinating case in the history of the Hebrew text's transmission, but with an important caveat this project treats candidly: the manuscript evidence available for evaluating this specific tradition does not clearly support it. Both the ancient Greek Septuagint translation (made centuries before the Masoretic tradition was fixed) and the Dead Sea Scrolls' own Habakkuk commentary (1QpHab, among the best-preserved and earliest witnesses to this specific book's text) agree with the 'we shall not die' reading found in the Masoretic Hebrew and in every modern translation, rather than supporting the proposed original 'you shall not die.' This external evidence leads a number of textual scholars to conclude that, whatever the traditional rabbinic explanation for this verse's inclusion on the tiqqune sopherim list, the 'we' reading is very likely the original text after all, and the list's own explanatory tradition (that scribes changed 'you' to 'we') may itself be a later, retrospective theological tradition rather than an accurate historical record of an actual textual alteration. None of the ten translations surveyed in this project adopts the proposed 'you shall not die' reading, consistent with the manuscript evidence's own support for 'we.'

## Literary Composition

### 6. A closing psalm in a different genre, complete with musical notations

**References:** Habakkuk 3:1-19

**The claim:** Habakkuk's first two chapters consist of a dialogue between the prophet and God (complaint and response), a format unique among the prophetic books. Chapter 3, however, shifts abruptly into a different literary genre entirely -- a formal psalm, complete with a musical superscription ('A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet, according to Shigionoth') and the recurring notation 'Selah' found otherwise almost exclusively in the book of Psalms, along with a closing musical direction ('To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments') -- raising a question about whether this chapter was originally composed as part of the same unified work as chapters 1-2, or added later from a separate liturgical source.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Habakkuk 3's musical notations and psalm-like structure exactly as given, without commenting on the chapter's compositional origin in the text itself; this is a matter of literary and compositional scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with comparable questions addressed elsewhere in this project regarding possible compositional layers within a single book (Job 28's 'hymn to wisdom,' and Ecclesiastes' epilogue), scholars are divided, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Many commentators read chapter 3 as an original and integral part of Habakkuk's own single, unified composition -- functioning as the dialogue's own fitting resolution, moving from the prophet's initial complaint and struggle (chapters 1-2) to a climactic theophany recounting God's past, decisive acts of deliverance (3:3-15) and a concluding affirmation of trust regardless of circumstances (3:17-19, addressed in entry 2 below), a natural and coherent movement for a single composition to make. Others, noting the chapter's musical notations and distinct genre, propose it was an independently composed liturgical psalm, possibly already in use in temple worship, that was incorporated into the book (whether by Habakkuk himself, adapting an existing psalm for this new context, or by a later editor) to supply a fitting climax to the preceding dialogue -- a reading that, as with the comparable cases in Job and Ecclesiastes, does not typically imply anything negative about the chapter's content or canonical authority, only about the specific stage at which it became part of the book's final, unified form.
