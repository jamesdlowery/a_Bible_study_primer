# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 Kings

This document catalogs 32 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of 1
Kings, either entirely internal to 1 Kings or between 1 Kings and a directly related passage
elsewhere (2 Chronicles, Deuteronomy, Hebrews, Numbers, Titus, Romans, Joshua, Judges, 2
Samuel). For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how
the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own
wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives
the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entry 5 (Baasha's death and the "36th year of Asa" chronology) is
presented without a single, confident resolution, since the leading proposed explanations
each face real objections and no one solution commands full scholarly consensus -- consistent
with this project's practice of flagging genuine open questions rather than manufacturing
false certainty.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where none commands real consensus,
an honest account of the live disagreement. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary
texts and the cross-references directly.

## Numbers and Text

### 1. Solomon's stalls for horses: 40,000, or 4,000?

**References:** 1 Kings 4:26; 2 Chronicles 9:25

**The claim:** 1 Kings 4:26 (Masoretic Hebrew) states Solomon had 40,000 stalls of horses for his chariots; 2 Chronicles 9:25 gives 4,000.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads '40,000' at 1 Kings 4:26, following the Masoretic Hebrew directly (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB reads '40,000' at 1 Kings 4:26, following the Masoretic Hebrew directly (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV reads '40,000' at 1 Kings 4:26, following the Masoretic Hebrew, with a footnote noting 2 Chronicles 9:25's '4,000' (verified against the published text; this entry was previously corrected in the general document set after an earlier draft mistakenly reversed ESV's reading).
- **KJV:** KJV reads '40,000' at 1 Kings 4:26, preserving the Masoretic figure without adjustment.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also reads '40,000,' often with a footnote pointing to the Chronicles parallel.
- **NASB:** NASB reads '40,000,' following the Masoretic Hebrew (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB reads '40,000' at 1 Kings 4:26, tracking NASB's Masoretic-following approach (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV reads '4,000' in the main text, harmonized with Chronicles, footnoting the Hebrew '40,000' (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT reads '4,000,' harmonizing directly (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV reads '40,000' at 1 Kings 4:26, following the Masoretic Hebrew directly (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** Given that 1 Kings 10:26 elsewhere states Solomon had 1,400 chariots, a ratio requiring 40,000 stalls (roughly 28 horses per chariot) is widely judged implausible, while 4,000 fits comfortably. Most textual scholars conclude 40,000 is a scribal error in the Masoretic transmission of this verse (a single-letter/digit slip in Hebrew numeral notation), and most modern translations either adopt or flag 4,000 as the more likely original figure.

### 2. Did the House of the Forest of Lebanon have four rows of pillars, or three?

**References:** 1 Kings 7:2-3

**The claim:** 1 Kings 7:2 states Solomon's House of the Forest of Lebanon was built 'upon four rows of cedar pillars.' The very next verse, 7:3, describes the cedar covering resting 'on forty five pillars, fifteen in a row' -- and forty-five divided by fifteen yields three rows, not four, an apparent internal inconsistency within the same two-verse description.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely difficult textual point within the Masoretic Hebrew's architectural description, and this document does not claim a single, fully certain resolution, consistent with its practice of candor where the evidence does not clearly settle a question. Several explanations have been proposed: some textual scholars suspect a numeral corruption in one of the two verses (either 'four' should read 'three' in 7:2, or the pillar arrangement in 7:3 originally specified a different count per row); others propose that 7:2's 'four rows' describes the overall structural layout of pillars supporting the roof beams across the building's full width (which may have included pillars along the building's ends not part of the 45-pillar count of 7:3), while 7:3's 45-pillar, three-row figure describes only a specific interior colonnade or one particular level of the multi-story structure. Given the complexity and fragmentary nature of the architectural details in this chapter generally (compare the similarly debated pi-ratio question addressed elsewhere in this document), this entry is presented as a genuine, unresolved textual and architectural puzzle rather than a confidently harmonized one.

### 3. How tall were the temple's two bronze pillars, Jachin and Boaz -- eighteen cubits each, or thirty-five cubits combined?

**References:** 1 Kings 7:15; 2 Chronicles 3:15

**The claim:** 1 Kings 7:15 states each of the two great bronze pillars at the Temple's entrance was 'eighteen cubits high.' 2 Chronicles 3:15 instead gives 'thirty-five cubits' for what appears to be the same pair of pillars.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard and widely accepted harmonization holds that 2 Chronicles 3:15's figure describes the combined height of both pillars together (35 cubits, closely approximating but not identical to two times 1 Kings' 18-cubit figure, which would total 36) -- ancient architectural records not infrequently give both a per-unit measurement and a combined total depending on the specific point being made, and the small remaining one-cubit discrepancy between 36 (2 x 18) and Chronicles' 35 is generally attributed to ordinary measurement rounding, or possibly to the differing treatment of a decorative capital or joint at the point where the two figures were reckoned, rather than to two irreconcilable systems of measurement for the same object.

### 4. What was the molten sea's stated capacity -- two thousand baths, or three thousand?

**References:** 1 Kings 7:26; 2 Chronicles 4:5

**The claim:** 1 Kings 7:26 states the great bronze 'sea' in Solomon's Temple 'contained two thousand baths.' 2 Chronicles 4:5, describing the identical object, instead states 'it received and held three thousand baths.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization distinguishes two different senses of capacity using the specific Hebrew verbs each text employs: 1 Kings 7:26 uses a term generally understood to describe the vessel's normal, everyday working capacity (how much water it typically held in ordinary use, likely somewhat below its physical brim to allow for priestly use without overflow), while 2 Chronicles 4:5's wording ('received and held,' a fuller expression) is read by many commentators as describing the vessel's maximum, full-to-the-brim physical capacity -- two different, both accurate figures for the same object's operational versus maximum volume, rather than two competing claims about a single fixed measurement.

### 5. How many chief officers oversaw Solomon's building projects -- 550, or 250?

**References:** 1 Kings 9:23; 2 Chronicles 8:10

**The claim:** 1 Kings 9:23 states that Solomon had '550' chief officers who ruled over the people engaged in his building work. 2 Chronicles 8:10, describing what appears to be the same body of officers, instead gives '250.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the related question addressed elsewhere in this document regarding Solomon's distinct categories of Israelite and non-Israelite labor, the most commonly offered harmonization notes that 1 Kings 5:16 separately records 3,300 lower-level foremen overseeing the (largely non-Israelite) labor force directly, while 9:23's 550 and 2 Chronicles 8:10's 250 both describe a smaller, higher tier of chief or principal officers specifically -- with the numerical difference between 550 and 250 most plausibly reflecting two different, overlapping administrative categories being counted (for instance, officers over Israelite supervisory staff specifically, which 2 Chronicles 8:9-10 explicitly distinguishes from the non-Israelite labor force in the immediately surrounding verses, versus a fuller combined total across both categories in 1 Kings), consistent with the same kind of differing administrative scope already documented for the parallel forced-labor question addressed elsewhere in this book.

### 6. How many talents of gold did Hiram send Solomon from Ophir -- 420, or 450?

**References:** 1 Kings 9:28; 2 Chronicles 8:18

**The claim:** 1 Kings 9:28 states that Hiram's ships brought Solomon '420 talents' of gold from Ophir. 2 Chronicles 8:18, describing the same shipment, instead gives '450 talents.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated as a further instance of the same kind of minor numeral variation already well documented between these two books' parallel accounts of Solomon's reign (compare the pillar-height and molten-sea capacity questions addressed elsewhere in this document), most likely arising from an ordinary scribal transmission difference affecting one or both figures in the course of copying -- the two numbers are close enough in magnitude, and the underlying Hebrew numeral notation similar enough in form, that most textual scholars treat this as a transmission variant of the same general kind rather than a substantive historical disagreement about the scale of Solomon's wealth from this specific expedition, which both figures otherwise corroborate.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. 'Solomon made no slaves of the people of Israel' -- yet Israel later complains of Solomon's 'heavy yoke'

**References:** 1 Kings 9:20-22; 1 Kings 5:13-18; 1 Kings 12:4

**The claim:** 1 Kings 9:20-22 states that Solomon conscripted forced labor from the remaining Canaanite peoples, but explicitly adds, 'of the people of Israel, Solomon made no slaves.' Yet 1 Kings 5:13-18 describes Solomon raising a labor force 'out of all Israel' of 30,000 men for the Lebanon timber-cutting rotation, and after Solomon's death, the assembled tribes complain to his son Rehoboam about 'the heavy yoke that your father put on us' (12:4), asking for relief -- language that sounds very much like the burden of Israelite forced labor, not merely conscripted foreign labor.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing categories of unfree labor described in the text itself, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read 9:20-22's specific denial ('no slaves') as addressing a particular category of unfree, permanent, hereditary bondage-labor of the kind imposed on the conquered Canaanite population, distinguished from the rotational, temporary conscript levy of native Israelites described in 5:13-18 (which explicitly rotates men in and out, 'a month in Lebanon and two months at home,' 5:14, a materially different arrangement from permanent slavery). On this reading, Israelites were subject to real, burdensome, and evidently deeply resented conscripted labor and taxation (which 12:4's complaint targets), but not to the specific institution of chattel slavery the author distinguishes them from in 9:22 -- a genuine distinction in ancient labor categories, though one that would have offered little comfort to those actually bearing the burden, which the narrative itself treats sympathetically enough to have it trigger the kingdom's division in the very next chapter.

### 8. Cities exchanged between Solomon and Hiram: did Solomon give cities to Hiram, or Hiram to Solomon?

**References:** 1 Kings 9:10-14; 2 Chronicles 8:1-2

**The claim:** 1 Kings 9:11-13 states that Solomon gave King Hiram of Tyre twenty cities in Galilee as compensation for the cedar, cypress, and gold Hiram had supplied for the Temple and palace -- but when Hiram came to inspect them, 'they did not please him,' and he named the region 'Cabul' (roughly, 'good for nothing'). 2 Chronicles 8:1-2 instead describes Solomon building up 'the cities that Huram had given to him,' and settling Israelites there -- reversing the direction of the gift.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Kings 9:11-13's account of Solomon giving cities to Hiram, and 2 Chronicles 8:1-2's account of Solomon building cities Huram had given him, exactly as given; the harmonization rests on reading the two texts as sequential stages of one transaction, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators overwhelmingly read these as two stages of a single continuous transaction rather than as contradictory claims about who originally gave what to whom: Solomon first gave the twenty cities to Hiram as payment (1 Kings 9:11), Hiram inspected and rejected them as unsatisfactory compensation (9:12-13), and -- most naturally reading the two accounts together -- Hiram then returned the unwanted cities to Solomon, who subsequently rebuilt, fortified, and resettled them with Israelites, which is precisely the stage of the process 2 Chronicles 8:1-2 describes. On this reading, 'the cities that Huram had given to him' in Chronicles refers to Hiram giving the cities back after rejecting them, not to an original, independent gift from Hiram to Solomon -- the two accounts describe sequential, not competing, stages of the same exchange.

### 9. Jericho 'rebuilt' under a curse -- yet referenced as an inhabited place centuries earlier

**References:** 1 Kings 16:34; Joshua 6:26; Judges 3:12-13; 2 Samuel 10:5

**The claim:** 1 Kings 16:34 records that in Ahab's day, 'Hiel of Bethel built Jericho,' losing his firstborn and youngest sons in the process 'according to the word of the LORD, which he spoke by Joshua' -- fulfilling Joshua's curse (Joshua 6:26) on whoever would rebuild the city after its destruction. Yet Jericho appears as a functioning, named location in the centuries between Joshua's curse and Hiel's 'rebuilding': Judges 3:12-13 describes the Moabite king Eglon capturing 'the city of palms' (a standard designation for Jericho) as a base of operations, and 2 Samuel 10:5 has David instruct his humiliated servants to 'remain at Jericho' until their beards grow back -- both apparently describing an inhabited town, well before Hiel's supposedly first 'rebuilding.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the relevant passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing fortified city-rebuilding from lesser habitation of the surrounding area, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators resolve this by distinguishing Joshua's specific curse -- which targeted the rebuilding of Jericho as a fortified city with gates and walls ('he shall lay its foundation... and set up its gates,' Joshua 6:26, language 1 Kings 16:34 repeats nearly verbatim) -- from a lower-level, unfortified, or seasonal habitation of the general area and its palm-grove oasis, which the intervening references in Judges and 2 Samuel are read as describing. On this view, the site was never entirely uninhabited or unused in the centuries between Joshua and Hiel (people evidently continued to live in or pass through the vicinity, and the name persisted for the region, much as several other sites in this project have been shown to carry a name forward through resettlement), but it remained an unfortified settlement until Hiel specifically undertook the fortified rebuilding project Joshua's curse addressed -- the specific act, and the specific curse, both concern city fortification, not bare habitation of the area.

### 10. Was Solomon alone at Gibeon when he offered a thousand burnt offerings, or accompanied by the assembled leaders of Israel?

**References:** 1 Kings 3:4; 2 Chronicles 1:2-6

**The claim:** 1 Kings 3:4 simply states, 'the king went to Gibeon to sacrifice there... a thousand burnt offerings did Solomon offer,' without mentioning any companions. 2 Chronicles 1:2-6, recounting the same event, has Solomon first assemble 'all Israel, the captains of thousands and of hundreds, the judges, and every governor,' who go up to Gibeon together with him.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Kings 3:4's silence about companions is not itself a claim that Solomon went alone; ancient narrative accounts routinely compress or omit surrounding detail a parallel account supplies, and 1 Kings' focus in this passage is specifically on Solomon's own personal request for wisdom in the dream that follows (3:5-14), for which the presence or absence of an accompanying assembly is simply not relevant to the point being made. 2 Chronicles' fuller account, emphasizing the national, corporate character of the occasion (consistent with Chronicles' broader interest throughout in the assembled nation's corporate worship), supplies additional detail 1 Kings does not include, rather than contradicting it.

### 11. What was Solomon's gift to Hiram of Tyre -- annual provisions of food, or twenty cities?

**References:** 1 Kings 5:10-11; 1 Kings 9:10-14

**The claim:** 1 Kings 5:10-11 describes Solomon supplying Hiram with wheat and pure oil annually, in exchange for the cedar and cypress timber Hiram provided for the Temple's construction. Later, 1 Kings 9:10-14 describes an entirely different compensation -- twenty cities in Galilee -- given to Hiram at the end of the twenty-year building project.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These describe two different components of the same long trading relationship rather than competing accounts of a single payment: the wheat and oil of 5:10-11 are explicitly described as an ongoing, annual supply arrangement sustained throughout the building years, functioning as Hiram's court's regular provisions in exchange for continued timber and labor, while the twenty cities of 9:10-14 are a separate, one-time settlement payment specifically covering the gold Hiram had also supplied (9:14 mentions 120 talents of gold from Hiram, a category the wheat-and-oil arrangement does not address) at the conclusion of the entire twenty-year project (Temple and palace combined, 9:10). The city transaction and its own further complications are addressed at greater length elsewhere in this document (see the entry on whether Solomon gave cities to Hiram or Hiram to Solomon).

### 12. How did Jeroboam die -- naturally, or struck down by the LORD?

**References:** 1 Kings 14:20; 2 Chronicles 13:20

**The claim:** 1 Kings 14:20 records Jeroboam's death with the standard, formulaic language used throughout Kings for a king's passing: 'Jeroboam slept with his fathers... and Nadab his son reigned in his stead.' 2 Chronicles 13:20, however, states more specifically that after his defeat by King Abijah of Judah, 'the LORD struck him, and he died' -- language suggesting a specific, violent act of divine judgment rather than an ordinary, peaceful death.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Slept with his fathers' is a standard formulaic notice of death and succession used throughout Kings and Chronicles for virtually every king, righteous and wicked alike, and does not itself specify or imply the manner or cause of death (compare its use for kings who died in battle, by assassination, or of illness, none of which the phrase distinguishes) -- it functions as a genealogical and chronological marker of succession, not a claim about peaceful circumstances. 2 Chronicles 13:20 supplies the specific, additional detail of how that death actually came about (a divine judgment following Jeroboam's defeat), consistent with, rather than contradicting, 1 Kings' more general formulaic notice, in the same way a brief obituary noting only that someone 'passed away' is not contradicted by a fuller account elsewhere specifying the particular cause.

### 13. Did Elisha receive Elijah's mantle at his initial calling, or only after Elijah was taken up into heaven?

**References:** 1 Kings 19:19-21; 2 Kings 2:9-14

**The claim:** 1 Kings 19:19-21 has Elijah cast his mantle upon Elisha at the time of Elisha's initial calling, long before Elijah's departure, and Elisha leaves his oxen to follow Elijah as his attendant. Yet 2 Kings 2:9-14 has Elisha receive Elijah's mantle again -- this time the mantle that 'fell from him' as Elijah was taken up into heaven -- which Elisha then uses to part the Jordan, marking his succession to Elijah's prophetic ministry.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two episodes describe two distinct, sequential stages of Elisha's calling and commissioning rather than two competing accounts of a single event: the initial mantle-casting in 1 Kings 19:19 marks the beginning of Elisha's discipleship and personal attendance on Elijah (a period the text describes as Elisha 'ministering unto' Elijah, 1 Kings 19:21, spanning several years of the narrative that follows), while the second mantle event in 2 Kings 2, occurring at Elijah's actual departure, marks the formal, final transfer of Elijah's prophetic office and empowerment to Elisha as his sole successor -- symbolized by Elisha's request for 'a double portion' of Elijah's spirit (2 Kings 2:9) and confirmed by the assembled prophets' recognition that 'the spirit of Elijah doth rest on Elisha' (2:15) immediately afterward. A gradual process of discipleship followed by a final, formal commissioning is a coherent and unremarkable pattern for succession to an office, not a duplicated account of one moment.

### 14. Did King Jehoshaphat remove the high places, or leave them standing?

**References:** 1 Kings 22:43; 2 Chronicles 17:6

**The claim:** 1 Kings 22:43 states that Jehoshaphat 'did that which was right in the eyes of the LORD,' but adds, 'nevertheless the high places were not taken away.' 2 Chronicles 17:6, describing the same king, instead states that 'he took away the high places and groves out of Judah.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization, consistent with how several other 'good' kings' partial religious reforms are described across Kings and Chronicles, distinguishes two different categories of 'high place' the two accounts have in view: the specifically idolatrous high places, associated with foreign gods and cultic objects ('groves,' i.e., Asherah poles, explicitly mentioned in 2 Chronicles 17:6), which Jehoshaphat did remove, consistent with Chronicles' account, versus the more general, longstanding local high places used for sacrificing to the LORD himself outside the Jerusalem Temple -- a widespread, popular practice that even otherwise faithful kings routinely failed to fully suppress (the same qualifying formula, 'nevertheless the high places were not taken away,' is applied to several other approved kings throughout Kings) -- which 1 Kings 22:43 specifically notes remained. On this reading, Jehoshaphat undertook a real, partial reform (removing specifically idolatrous worship sites, as Chronicles emphasizes) without achieving the fuller, more difficult suppression of all local high-place worship generally, which 1 Kings' formula acknowledges remained an ongoing, unresolved problem.

## Chronology

### 15. When did Baasha attack Judah -- before, or after, his own recorded death?

**References:** 1 Kings 15:33; 1 Kings 16:6-8; 2 Chronicles 16:1

**The claim:** 1 Kings 15:33 and 16:8 together establish that Baasha began to reign in Asa's third year and reigned 24 years, meaning his death (and his son Elah's accession, explicitly dated to 'the twenty-sixth year of Asa,' 16:8) fell in Asa's 26th year. Yet 2 Chronicles 16:1, describing a still-later event, states, 'In the thirty-sixth year of the reign of Asa, Baasha king of Israel came up against Judah' -- ten years after 1 Kings' own numbers place Baasha's death.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '24 years' (1 Kings 15:33), '26th year' (1 Kings 16:8), and '36th year' (2 Chronicles 16:1) exactly as given in the Masoretic Hebrew, without adjusting any of the figures to resolve the tension; no translation differences are involved in this discrepancy.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely difficult chronological puzzle, and unlike some of the numeral disputes addressed elsewhere in this project, no single explanation commands full scholarly consensus, though several are seriously proposed. The majority explanation among conservative harmonists, following an observation credited to the Jewish historian Josephus, holds that 2 Chronicles 16:1's '36' is a scribal transmission error for '26' -- a plausible confusion in the Hebrew numeral system, and one that would need to apply equally to the closely related figure at 2 Chronicles 15:19. A minority position proposes instead that '36' is reckoned not from Asa's own accession but from the division of the kingdom under Rehoboam, which would place the event at roughly Asa's 16th regnal year. This solution is favored by some older commentators, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown among them, but criticized by others, including the conservative scholar Gleason Archer, as lacking any clear parallel elsewhere in Chronicles' own consistent practice of dating events to the reigning king's own accession year. This entry is presented candidly as an unresolved question among the possible explanations, consistent with this project's practice of not manufacturing false certainty where none exists among serious scholars.

### 16. 480 years from the Exodus to Solomon's Temple -- does the math add up?

**References:** 1 Kings 6:1; Judges (cumulative totals); Acts 13:19-22

**The claim:** 1 Kings 6:1 states the Temple's construction began 480 years after the Exodus. Adding up the individual reign and judgeship lengths recorded through the books of Judges, Samuel, and the early monarchy produces a total noticeably higher than 480 years by many reckonings.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Kings 6:1's '480 years' exactly as given, without adjusting the figure; the resolution is a matter of historical-chronological interpretation, addressed at greater length in the companion Judges document, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Judges document, which addresses the cumulative judges-period arithmetic directly. In brief, the most widely offered harmonization treats 480 (12 x 40) as a stylized, generation-counting figure common in ancient Near Eastern chronological writing rather than a strict sum of sequential periods, and holds that several of the judgeships recorded in Judges were regional rather than nationwide, and therefore overlapped in time rather than following one another in a single unbroken sequence -- which would make a straightforward addition of every period's length an overstatement of the actual elapsed time even on the text's own terms.

## Theological Tensions

### 17. Elijah says 'I, even I only, am left' -- yet God says 7,000 have not bowed to Baal

**References:** 1 Kings 19:9-10; 1 Kings 19:14; 1 Kings 19:18; Romans 11:2-4

**The claim:** Fleeing Jezebel's threat and in deep despair at Mount Horeb, Elijah twice tells the LORD, 'I, even I only, am left' among the prophets of the LORD, and the people seek to kill him too (19:10, 14). God's response, however, reveals, 'Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto Baal' (19:18) -- directly contradicting, in a literal sense, Elijah's repeated claim to be entirely alone.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both Elijah's claim and God's response plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing Elijah's statement as emotionally honest but factually mistaken personal perception, a reading Paul's own use of the passage in Romans 11 supports, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This tension is addressed directly within the passage itself and is generally read not as an error but as the narrative's own deliberate point: Elijah's claim reflects his genuine, despairing emotional and psychological state (following an exhausting flight, deep fear, and profound discouragement after the apparent failure of Baal's prophets' defeat on Mount Carmel to produce lasting national repentance, rather than a claim Elijah had personally verified through any actual national census) rather than a literal, considered assertion of fact -- God's gentle correction (revealing 7,000 faithful Elijah had no way of knowing about) is presented as part of God's compassionate response to Elijah in his depression, giving him needed perspective and companionship (Elisha) rather than leaving him in isolation. Paul cites this very episode in Romans 11:2-4 explicitly to make the same point: God's faithful remnant is frequently larger and less visible than even a faithful believer, in a moment of despair, may perceive.

### 18. Unconditional dynasty, or conditional -- both promised to Solomon in the same speech

**References:** 1 Kings 9:3-5; 1 Kings 9:6-9; 2 Samuel 7:12-16

**The claim:** In the same nighttime revelation to Solomon following the Temple's dedication, God first reaffirms the Davidic covenant in strongly unconditional terms echoing 2 Samuel 7 -- 'I will establish your royal throne over Israel forever' (9:5) -- and then, only a sentence later, states a sharply conditional warning: 'But if you turn aside from following me... then I will cut off Israel from the land... and this house... I will cast out of my sight' (9:6-7).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both statements plainly and consistently as given, within the same speech; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the unconditional dynastic promise from the conditional enjoyment of its immediate blessings, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this as reflecting a real and consistent distinction maintained throughout the Old Testament's treatment of the Davidic covenant between two distinct promises: an unconditional guarantee that David's dynastic line would never be permanently extinguished and that a descendant of David would ultimately and finally reign forever (the promise 2 Samuel 7 and 1 Kings 9:5 both affirm, and which the New Testament understands as fulfilled in Christ), and a conditional promise regarding any individual king's, or generation's, immediate enjoyment of the throne, the Temple, and the land -- which could be, and eventually was, forfeited through persistent unfaithfulness (culminating in the exile), without this cancelling the ultimate, unconditional dynastic promise itself. The same chapter holding both together, rather than contradicting itself, models a pattern already implicit in 2 Samuel 7:14-15's own language ('when he commits iniquity, I will discipline him... but my steadfast love will not depart from him').

### 19. Micaiah's vision: did the LORD send a 'lying spirit' to deceive Ahab's prophets?

**References:** 1 Kings 22:19-23; Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2; Hebrews 6:18

**The claim:** The prophet Micaiah describes a vision of the LORD's heavenly council in which the LORD asks for a volunteer to 'entice Ahab' to his death at Ramoth-gilead; a spirit volunteers, saying, 'I will go out and be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets,' and the LORD approves the plan (22:19-23) -- language that appears to have God directly authorizing and commissioning deception, in apparent tension with statements elsewhere that God 'never lies' (Titus 1:2, Hebrews 6:18) and 'is not a man, that he should lie' (Numbers 23:19).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Micaiah's vision plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing God's own truthfulness from God's sovereign permission of a deceiving spirit's activity as an act of judgment, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish between God himself directly speaking a falsehood (which the cited passages deny is possible, and which does not occur anywhere in this account -- God does not personally tell Ahab's prophets anything false) and God sovereignly permitting or commissioning a created spiritual being to carry out deception as an act of judgment against a king already determined, through his own persistent, willful rejection of God's genuine prophetic word (illustrated throughout 1 Kings by Ahab's repeated hostility toward Elijah and, in this very chapter, his imprisonment of Micaiah for telling the truth, 22:26-27), to meet the fate he had earned. This is read as consistent with a broader biblical pattern (compare the 'lying spirit' language with God 'giving people over' to a delusion already invited by their own persistent unbelief, 2 Thessalonians 2:11-12, and Satan's own activity operating only within God's ultimate permission, Job 1-2) -- an act of judicial judgment executed through a deceiving agent, not God himself lying, and not a case where an innocent party is deceived apart from their own prior, culpable rejection of the truth already available to them.

### 20. Was Solomon the wisest who would ever live, or is 'a greater than Solomon' present in Christ?

**References:** 1 Kings 3:12; Matthew 12:42

**The claim:** 1 Kings 3:12 has God tell Solomon, 'I have given thee a wise and an understanding heart; so that there was none like thee before thee, neither after thee shall any arise like unto thee' -- an emphatic superlative. Jesus later says, regarding himself, 'a greater than Solomon is here' (Matthew 12:42).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Kings 3:12's superlative is explicitly and narrowly scoped to the specific kind of wisdom given to Solomon as a human king ruling Israel -- administrative, judicial, and practical wisdom of the sort the immediately following narrative illustrates (the famous judgment between the two women, 3:16-28) -- not a claim ranking Solomon above every person who would ever exist in every respect, including the incarnate Son of God, a categorically different kind of being than any human king the promise to Solomon has in view. Jesus' statement in Matthew 12:42 draws precisely this distinction, contrasting his own unique identity and authority (as the one greater than the Temple, the Sabbath, and Jonah in the same discourse) with Solomon's human wisdom, without either text treating itself as a rival ranking of merely human wise men.

### 21. Does God dwell in thick darkness, or in unapproachable light?

**References:** 1 Kings 8:12; 1 Timothy 6:16

**The claim:** At the Temple's dedication, Solomon declares that the LORD 'said that he would dwell in the thick darkness' (1 Kings 8:12). Paul later describes God as 'dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto' (1 Timothy 6:16).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Kings 8:12's 'thick darkness' echoes the specific imagery of God's manifest presence at Sinai -- Exodus 20:21, Deuteronomy 4:11 and 5:22 all describe the LORD's presence in 'thick darkness' at the giving of the Law -- and the cloud that had just filled the Temple in the immediately preceding verses (8:10-11). This is a recurring biblical image of God's presence being so overwhelming and holy that it appears as impenetrable darkness or cloud to human observers unable to bear direct exposure to it, not a literal claim that God's own nature is dark. 1 Timothy 6:16's 'unapproachable light' makes essentially the same underlying point from the opposite sensory direction: God's glory is so intense that, whether described as blinding light or impenetrable cloud, it cannot be directly beheld by human beings. Both images convey the identical truth of God's transcendent, unapproachable holiness, rather than offering two literal, competing descriptions of his dwelling's visual appearance.

### 22. Does God actually dwell in temples built by human hands?

**References:** 1 Kings 8:13, 27; Acts 7:48

**The claim:** Solomon prays, 'I have surely built thee an house to dwell in' (1 Kings 8:13), seemingly claiming the Temple would literally house God. Only fourteen verses later, in the same prayer, Solomon himself asks, 'will God indeed dwell on the earth? behold, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee; how much less this house that I have builded?' (8:27) -- and Stephen later states plainly, 'the most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands' (Acts 7:48).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Solomon's own prayer supplies its own qualification within the very same speech, making this one of the clearest cases in Scripture of a text anticipating and directly answering the apparent tension it raises: 8:13's 'house to dwell in' uses the ordinary, functional language of a sanctuary as the designated place of God's special, localized manifestation of presence and the focus of Israel's worship and prayer (the sense in which the Temple was genuinely 'God's house'), while 8:27 immediately clarifies that this in no way implies God's infinite being is literally contained or housed the way a human dwelling contains its occupant. Stephen's statement in Acts 7:48 makes the same underlying point Solomon's own prayer already established -- that no physical structure can literally contain or house the transcendent, omnipresent God -- rather than contradicting the Temple's real, God-ordained function as recorded throughout 1 Kings 8.

### 23. Is there anyone who does not sin?

**References:** 1 Kings 8:46; 1 John 3:9

**The claim:** Solomon's dedicatory prayer states plainly, 'there is no man that sinneth not' (1 Kings 8:46), affirming universal human sinfulness. 1 John 3:9 states, 'Whosoever is born of God doth not commit sin; for his seed remaineth in him: and he cannot sin, because he is born of God' -- language some read as claiming believers achieve sinless perfection.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 John itself, in the same short letter, explicitly denies the reading that would create a real conflict here: 1 John 1:8, 10 states plainly, 'If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us... If we say that we have not sinned, we make him a liar' -- the same author cannot be understood, only one chapter later, to claim believers are entirely sinless without contradicting his own immediately preceding statement. Most commentators accordingly read 1 John 3:9's stronger language as describing either a settled, characteristic pattern or direction of life (the one born of God does not practice sin as an habitual, unrepentant lifestyle, using a Greek present tense elsewhere argued to carry a continuous, habitual sense) rather than an absolute, unbroken freedom from every individual act of sin, or as describing the believer's new nature itself (which, as it truly is 'of God,' cannot sin) in tension with the ongoing presence of the old nature's continuing capacity to sin that 1:8-10 addresses -- either reading leaves 1 Kings 8:46's universal claim about actual human conduct fully intact.

### 24. Is God the one who creates and brings evil, or does evil have no place in him at all?

**References:** 1 Kings 14:10; Isaiah 45:7; James 1:13; 1 John 1:5

**The claim:** 1 Kings 14:10 has God declare, through the prophet Ahijah, 'I will bring evil upon the house of Jeroboam.' Isaiah 45:7 similarly has God say, 'I make peace, and create evil.' Yet James 1:13 states God 'tempteth no man' toward evil, and 1 John 1:5 declares 'God is light, and in him is no darkness at all.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew word rendered 'evil' in passages like 1 Kings 14:10 and Isaiah 45:7 (ra) has a considerably broader semantic range than the specifically moral sense the English word most often carries today, regularly covering calamity, disaster, harm, or judgment brought about as a consequence of sin, rather than moral wickedness or sin itself -- the same word describes famine, military defeat, and physical suffering elsewhere in the Old Testament without any implication that God is the author of moral corruption. Read this way, 1 Kings 14:10 and Isaiah 45:7 both affirm God's sovereign authority to bring calamity and judgment upon those who persist in wrongdoing (the immediate context of both verses), a claim about God's judicial and providential governance of history, while James 1:13 and 1 John 1:5 address a different question entirely -- whether God's own moral character contains or produces sin/temptation to sin, which both texts deny -- so that 'evil' in the sense of calamity/judgment (which God does bring) and 'evil' in the sense of moral corruption or temptation to sin (which James and John both deny of God) are simply not the same category the apparent contradiction requires them to be.

### 25. Did God bring evil upon Ahab as a specific, deserved judgment?

**References:** 1 Kings 20:42

**The claim:** A prophet tells Ahab, after Ahab released the defeated King Ben-hadad of Syria contrary to God's command, 'thy life shall go for his life, and thy people for his people' -- a pronouncement of coming disaster ('evil') upon Ahab as direct divine judgment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry raises the same broader word-study question addressed at greater length elsewhere in this document (see the entry on whether God is the creator of evil, discussing 1 Kings 14:10 and Isaiah 45:7); in brief, 'evil' here again functions in its judicial/calamity sense -- a specific, deserved consequence pronounced against a specific king for a specific act of disobedience (releasing a king God had appointed for destruction, in direct violation of the herem/devotion-to-destruction principle governing this particular conflict) -- rather than a claim that God authors moral wickedness. The pronouncement is presented throughout 1 Kings as just retribution for Ahab's own specific choice, not an arbitrary or causeless affliction.

## Numbers and Objects

### 26. What was inside the Ark of the Covenant at the Temple's dedication?

**References:** 1 Kings 8:9; Hebrews 9:4

**The claim:** 1 Kings 8:9 states that at the time the Temple was dedicated, the Ark contained only the two stone tablets. Hebrews 9:4 states the Ark contained the tablets, a golden urn of manna, and Aaron's rod that budded.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this passage as given; the harmonization concerns different points in a long history, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These describe different points in time: Numbers 17:10 and Exodus 16:33-34 record that the manna and Aaron's rod were originally placed 'before the testimony' / near the Ark during the wilderness period, while this verse describes the Ark's contents centuries later, at Solomon's Temple dedication -- by which time the manna and rod may have been removed, lost, or (per some Jewish tradition) kept alongside the Ark rather than inside it even earlier. Hebrews reflects the fuller wilderness-era tradition of what was associated with the Ark; this verse reports the situation at one specific later date.

### 27. The bronze 'sea' and the value of pi

**References:** 1 Kings 7:23; 2 Chronicles 4:2

**The claim:** Both texts describe the Temple's large bronze basin as 10 cubits in diameter and 30 cubits in circumference -- a ratio implying pi equals exactly 3, whereas the true value is approximately 3.14159.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both figures (10 cubits, 30 cubits) as given, without adjusting them; the harmonization concerns interpreting which part of the vessel each measurement describes, not translation.

**Reconciliation:** Several explanations are commonly offered: the measurements are most likely rounded figures appropriate to the practical, non-scientific purposes of the text; some point to the specific Hebrew wording describing a rim or brim that flared outward 'a handbreadth thick, like the brim of a cup' (7:26), meaning the 10-cubit figure may describe the outer rim's diameter while the 30-cubit figure describes the smaller circumference of the vessel's body beneath the flared rim -- two different measurements at two different points on the same object, which would closely match the correct ratio for pi rather than requiring a value of exactly 3.

## Law and Practice

### 28. The law for Israel's king (Deuteronomy 17:14-17) vs. Solomon's actual practice

**References:** Deuteronomy 17:14-17; 1 Kings 10:14-29; 1 Kings 11:1-8

**The claim:** Deuteronomy's law governing a future Israelite king explicitly prohibits the king from acquiring many horses, many wives ('lest his heart turn away'), or excessive silver and gold. This book describes Solomon doing all three at an extraordinary scale -- vast horse-trading operations (10:26-29), 700 wives and 300 concubines explicitly said to have turned his heart away after his foreign wives led him into idolatry (11:1-8), and enormous quantities of gold (10:14-25) -- largely without an immediate prophetic rebuke recorded at the moment each accumulation is first described, though the text does record God's displeasure and its consequences shortly afterward, in 11:9-13.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the connection between them is a matter of recognizing this book's own narrative structure and echoing vocabulary, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not generally treated as Scripture contradicting itself but as Scripture directly illustrating, in narrative form, the very failure Deuteronomy's law was written to prevent -- the author narrates Solomon's accumulations largely without comment through chapter 10, then explicitly ties the consequence (God's anger, the announced division of the kingdom) directly back to the specific violation of exactly this pattern in 11:1-13, using language ('his wives turned away his heart,' 11:3) that directly echoes Deuteronomy 17:17's warning. Far from an oversight, most commentators read 1 Kings 10-11 as a deliberate, structured demonstration of Deuteronomy's king-law being violated in each of its particulars, with consequences following exactly as that law implied they would.

### 29. Divided-kingdom regnal chronology: do the years of Israel and Judah's kings add up consistently?

**References:** 1 Kings 15-16; 2 Kings 1-18; 2 Chronicles 16-32

**The claim:** 1 and 2 Kings (with parallel material in 2 Chronicles) provide the regnal length of every king of the divided kingdoms of Israel and Judah, along with dozens of cross-references synchronizing a given king's accession to a specific year of his counterpart on the other throne. Added straightforwardly, however, these regnal-year totals and cross-synchronisms do not consistently agree with each other -- for example, summing the reigns of Israel's kings from Jehu to Pekahiah yields roughly 114 years and 7 months, while the same period in Judah, from Athaliah to Azariah (Uzziah), totals roughly 128 years, a 14-year gap for what should be the same span of real time. This is one of the most technically complex chronological questions raised about the Old Testament's historical narrative.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the individual regnal-year figures throughout 1-2 Kings and 2 Chronicles plainly and consistently as given; the apparent inconsistency arises only when these figures are added and cross-checked against each other and against external Assyrian and Babylonian chronological records, not from any variation in how a given number is translated.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely complex area of biblical chronology, not resolved by any single simple harmonization, though it has been the subject of sustained, serious scholarly work rather than being an unaddressed or unaddressable difficulty. The standard modern reference point is Edwin R. Thiele's 1951 study *The Mysterious Numbers of the Hebrew Kings*, which proposed that the apparent discrepancies arise from several distinct, historically attested scribal conventions operating differently in the two kingdoms: Judah and Israel used different calendar start-months (Tishri versus Nisan) at different points; the two kingdoms sometimes used different systems for counting a king's first partial year (the 'accession-year' system, which does not count a king's first partial year as year one, versus a 'non-accession-year' system, which does); and, most significantly, at least nine reigns across both kingdoms appear to have involved co-regencies -- periods when a king reigned jointly with his father or son -- which, if not accounted for, would cause the same real years to be counted twice when summing consecutive 'reigns' as though they were sequential rather than overlapping. Applying these principles, Thiele and later scholars who refined his work (including Siegfried Horn, Gershon Galil, and Rodger Young) were able to construct a chronology that reconciles the great majority of the biblical regnal figures both internally and against fixed points in Assyrian records (notably a solar eclipse in 763 BC recorded in the Assyrian Eponym Canon). This project does not treat any single reconstruction (Thiele's or a competitor's) as definitively established in every particular, since real scholarly disagreement remains over specific details -- but the existence of a plausible, textually-grounded, and externally-correlated solution using conventions independently attested in the ancient Near East (dual dating, co-regency, and variable new-year reckoning) means the underlying figures need not be treated as simple arithmetic errors.

## Names and Identity

### 30. Who was Heman's father -- Mahol, or Zerah?

**References:** 1 Kings 4:31; 1 Chronicles 2:6

**The claim:** 1 Kings 4:31 lists Heman, along with Chalcol and Darda, as 'the sons of Mahol,' wise men to whom Solomon's own wisdom is favorably compared. 1 Chronicles 2:6 lists Heman, Chalcol, and Darda (spelled Dara) again together, but as sons of Zerah, of the tribe of Judah -- a different father's name entirely.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Many Hebrew scholars read 'Mahol' in 1 Kings 4:31 not as a personal name at all but as a common noun meaning 'dance' or 'choir,' so that 'the sons of Mahol' (bene ha-mahol) most likely functions as a guild or professional designation -- 'members of the choir/dance company' or a similar title for a recognized class of temple musicians and sages -- rather than a literal patronymic competing with 1 Chronicles' genealogical record of the same men as literal descendants of Zerah. On this reading, the two texts are not in conflict about the men's actual father at all: 1 Chronicles supplies their genealogical father (Zerah), while 1 Kings identifies them by their professional guild or class, much as a person today might be identified either by their surname or by their guild/professional title depending on context.

### 31. Was Hiram the craftsman's mother from the tribe of Naphtali, or Dan?

**References:** 1 Kings 7:13-14; 2 Chronicles 2:14

**The claim:** 1 Kings 7:14 identifies the skilled bronze craftsman Hiram (distinct from King Hiram of Tyre) as the son of 'a widow woman of the tribe of Naphtali,' with a father who was a man of Tyre. 2 Chronicles 2:14, describing the same craftsman, instead calls his mother 'a woman of the daughters of Dan.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Several harmonizations are commonly offered for this minor genealogical variant: since the tribal territories of Naphtali and Dan bordered one another in the northern part of Israel, Hiram's mother may have belonged to Dan by birth but resided in, or been closely associated with, Naphtali's territory by marriage or residence at the time relevant to each account (comparable to similar geographic-versus-tribal identifications addressed elsewhere in this project, such as Samuel's father being called both a Levite and an 'Ephrathite'); alternatively, some commentators propose she may have been widowed twice, first to a man of Dan and then to the Tyrian craftsman who fathered Hiram, making both tribal identifications independently accurate depending on which marriage each text has in view. Either explanation is consistent with both texts' agreement on every other detail of the account (the craftsman's mixed Israelite-Tyrian parentage and his exceptional skill), with only the specific tribal identification of the mother differing.

### 32. Was Jehu the son of Nimshi, or his grandson?

**References:** 1 Kings 19:16; 2 Kings 9:2, 14

**The claim:** 1 Kings 19:16 has the LORD instruct Elijah to anoint 'Jehu the son of Nimshi' as king over Israel. 2 Kings 9:2 and 9:14, however, identify Jehu more fully as 'the son of Jehoshaphat the son of Nimshi' -- making Nimshi Jehu's grandfather, with Jehoshaphat as his immediate father.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with several similar genealogical questions addressed elsewhere in this project (regarding Kish's father, and Laban's relationship to Nahor, among others), 'son of' in these formulas regularly denotes any male descendant rather than exclusively a direct, one-generation parent, and identifying a person by a more prominent grandfather or ancestor rather than an immediate, less well-known father is a well-documented pattern throughout these genealogies. 1 Kings 19:16's briefer formula names Jehu by his more prominent grandfather Nimshi, while 2 Kings 9:2, 14 supply the fuller, more precise three-generation genealogy (Jehu, son of Jehoshaphat, son of Nimshi) -- both consistent with each other once the flexible use of 'son of' in this kind of ancestral reference is recognized.
