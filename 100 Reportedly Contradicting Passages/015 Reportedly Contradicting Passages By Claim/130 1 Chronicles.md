# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 Chronicles

This document catalogs 18 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of 1
Chronicles, either entirely internal to 1 Chronicles (a significant share of this book's
alleged tensions arise from its extensive genealogies, several of which are addressed here for
the first time in this series) or between 1 Chronicles and its parallel material in 1-2
Samuel and Genesis. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised,
notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the
translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in
English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's character:** Five entries here (9, 10, 11, 12, and part of 2) address
textual variants between this book and 2 Samuel that were already researched and verified
earlier in this project, where the parallel 2 Samuel verse was treated as primary. Since this
book's own wording at each of those points is not itself in dispute among the ten
translations, those entries are kept brief and cross-reference the fuller treatment already
published, rather than repeating it. The remaining seven entries are freshly researched
genealogical and literary questions substantially unique to this book.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine uncertainty remains
among scholars (as with the Eliam/Ahithophel identification in entry 2), an honest account of
what is and is not established. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary texts and the
cross-references directly.

## Genealogy

### 1. Jesse's sons: eight, or seven?

**References:** 1 Samuel 17:12; 1 Chronicles 2:13-15

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 17:12 states Jesse had eight sons; 1 Chronicles 2:13-15's genealogical list names only seven sons of Jesse, with David as the seventh (the youngest named).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both texts exactly as given (eight sons in 1 Samuel 17:12, seven named in 1 Chronicles 2:13-15); the harmonization concerns ancient genealogical conventions of selective listing, not translation.

**Reconciliation:** Genealogical lists in the ancient Near East, including biblical ones, are well documented as sometimes selectively recording only surviving or prominent descendants relevant to the compiler's purpose, rather than functioning as an exhaustive census. A common explanation here is that one of Jesse's eight sons is presumed to have died without issue, or without further narrative relevance, before this genealogy was compiled generations later, and was accordingly omitted from this specific list, without this being treated as an error by ancient standards of genealogical record-keeping -- a pattern also seen elsewhere in this book's own genealogies (see the Saul genealogy entry below, and the seven/nine sons of David entry, both of which show similar selective listing conventions within 1 Chronicles itself).

### 2. Bathsheba's name and her father's name: two sets of variants in the same verse

**References:** 1 Chronicles 3:5; 2 Samuel 11:3; 2 Samuel 23:34

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 11:3 identifies David's future wife as 'Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam.' 1 Chronicles 3:5, listing David's children born in Jerusalem, instead calls her 'Bath-shua, the daughter of Ammiel' -- two names changed in the very same verse. Separately, 2 Samuel 23:34 lists 'Eliam the son of Ahithophel the Gilonite' among David's mighty men -- a name some identify with Bathsheba's father, which would make her the granddaughter of Ahithophel, David's trusted counselor who later defects to Absalom's rebellion -- yet the parallel list of mighty men in this book (1 Chronicles 11:36) names 'Ahijah the Pelonite' at the corresponding position, not Eliam or any reference to Ahithophel at all.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads 'Bath-shua (Bathsheba) daughter of Ammiel (Eliam),' explicitly supplying both alternate names in the text itself via parenthetical amplification.
- **CSB:** CSB reads 'Bathshua daughter of Ammiel,' following the Masoretic Hebrew, typically with a footnote.
- **ESV:** ESV reads 'Bath-shua, the daughter of Ammiel,' with a footnote noting some Hebrew manuscripts, along with the Vulgate, Septuagint, and 2 Samuel 11:3, read 'Bathsheba.'
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'Bathshua the daughter of Ammiel' at 1 Chronicles 3:5, preserving both name variants exactly as the Masoretic Hebrew has them.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also reads 'Bath-shua...Ammiel,' matching the KJV.
- **NASB:** NASB reads 'Bath-shua the daughter of Ammiel,' following the Masoretic Hebrew as received.
- **LSB:** LSB reads 'Bath-shua the daughter of Ammiel,' matching NASB exactly (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV reads 'Bathsheba daughter of Ammiel' -- harmonizing the first name to match 2 Samuel while retaining the Chronicles-specific 'Ammiel' for the father, a footnote noting the Hebrew reads 'Bathshua.'
- **NLT:** NLT reads 'Bathsheba, daughter of Ammiel,' harmonizing the first name in the main text.
- **RSV2CE:** The RSV tradition renders this 'Bath-shua, the daughter of Ammiel' (confirmed via the closely related NRSV text, 'Bath-shua, daughter of Ammiel'), following the Masoretic Hebrew; RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** Both name-pairs are well-documented cases of transposition or letter-confusion rather than genuinely different identities, and are treated this way by the wide majority of Hebrew scholars: 'Ammiel' and 'Eliam' are composed of the identical two Hebrew elements (el, 'God,' and am, 'people/kinsman') simply reversed in order -- a documented pattern elsewhere in Hebrew names (compare Jehoiachin/Jeconiah); 'Bathshua' and 'Bathsheba' differ by a single easily-confused Hebrew letter (a vav for a bet, letters with a similar sound). Most commentators treat these as legitimate alternate forms of the same two names rather than scribal errors requiring correction. The mighty-men list discrepancy (Eliam/Ahithophel in 2 Samuel vs. Ahijah the Pelonite in 1 Chronicles) is treated separately and is more genuinely uncertain -- some textual scholars view 1 Chronicles' 'Ahijah the Pelonite' at this position as reflecting textual corruption in one list or the other during transmission, given how closely the two mighty-men rosters otherwise track each other name-for-name in the same sequence; if the identification with Bathsheba's father were secure, it would supply a specific historical motive (David's earlier sin against Bathsheba and her husband) for Ahithophel's otherwise unexplained defection to Absalom -- a widely discussed but ultimately unproven scholarly conjecture, since establishing Ahithophel's age at the time and ruling out a second, unrelated Eliam both remain open questions.

### 3. David's second son: Chileab, or Daniel?

**References:** 2 Samuel 3:2-3; 1 Chronicles 3:1

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 3:3, listing David's sons born in Hebron, names his second son (by Abigail) as 'Chileab.' 1 Chronicles 3:1, listing the same sons, names David's second son (also by Abigail) as 'Daniel.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'Chileab' at 2 Samuel 3:3 and 'Daniel' at 1 Chronicles 3:1 exactly as given, without harmonizing the name in either direction; the explanation (one individual, two names) applies regardless of translation.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators treat this simply as a case of an individual known by two names -- a well-attested and unremarkable practice throughout the historical books of the Old Testament (compare Solomon/Jedidiah, 2 Samuel 12:24-25, and Jehoiachin/Jeconiah/Coniah elsewhere), rather than as competing or erroneous records. No narrative material about this particular son survives in either book to compare beyond the bare genealogical listing, so there is no further internal evidence bearing on the question beyond the plausibility of dual-naming already well documented for other biblical figures.

### 4. Seven sons born in Jerusalem, or nine?

**References:** 2 Samuel 5:14-16; 1 Chronicles 3:5-8

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 5:14-16 lists the sons born to David in Jerusalem: Shammua, Shobab, Nathan, Solomon, Ibhar, Elishua, Nepheg, Japhia, Elishama, Eliada, and Eliphelet -- eleven names, though some names recur with variant spellings across manuscripts, and various countings of the unique names yield different totals in different sources. 1 Chronicles 3:5-8 gives an overlapping but not identical list, including two names ('Eliphelet' and 'Nogah') not found in the 2 Samuel list at all, and 'Elishama' appears where 2 Samuel has 'Elishua' at the corresponding position.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both lists exactly as given in their respective books, without harmonizing the total count or reconciling the specific names in the text; the explanation rests on recognizing differing scope and selectivity between the two accounts, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators generally treat the two lists as complementary rather than conflicting: some names likely represent the same individual under a variant spelling (Elishua/Elishama, a plausible scribal or dialectal variation of a similar name), while other names present only in Chronicles (Eliphelet as it first appears, and Nogah) are generally explained as sons who died in infancy or early childhood without further narrative significance, and were consequently omitted from the shorter list in Samuel -- Chronicles, writing centuries later with access to full royal genealogical records for its comprehensive purpose, includes names 2 Samuel's more narrative-focused account did not find necessary to record. This is treated as a difference in scope and selectivity between the two accounts, not a genuine conflict about which sons actually existed.

### 5. Saul's genealogy, given twice within this book itself

**References:** 1 Chronicles 8:29-38; 1 Chronicles 9:35-44

**The claim:** This book records Saul's family genealogy in full twice, within the span of its own first nine chapters -- once at 8:29-38 and again, in nearly identical form, at 9:35-44 -- with only minor variations in a few names between the two lists, raising the question of why the same information is repeated, and whether the two lists actually agree in every detail.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both genealogies exactly as given, without merging or cross-correcting the minor name variations between them in the text; the resolution concerns recognizing the literary purpose of the repetition and the ordinary scope of ancient genealogical copying variation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read the repetition as a deliberate literary technique (a 'resumptive repetition,' a recognized device in ancient Near Eastern and biblical narrative for returning to a topic after a digression) rather than an accidental duplication: chapter 8 closes the broader genealogical survey of the tribes with Saul's line specifically because it leads into the narrative of his death, which begins in chapter 10, while chapter 9's repetition serves a different, immediate purpose -- introducing the roster of families who resettled Jerusalem after the exile (9:1-34), before pivoting back to Saul's line specifically as the direct segue into the historical narrative that follows. On the minor name variations between the two lists (a small number of names differ or are spelled differently at corresponding positions), most textual scholars attribute these to ordinary copying variation across what was, in either case, likely drawn from a shared underlying genealogical source -- consistent with the kind of minor variation seen in ancient genealogical lists generally, and not indicating two fundamentally different or competing family records.

### 6. Was Timna a son of Eliphaz, or the mother of Amalek?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 1:36; Genesis 36:12

**The claim:** Genesis 36:12 explicitly and unambiguously identifies Timna as a concubine of Eliphaz (Esau's son) and the mother of Amalek by him -- a woman, not a son. 1 Chronicles 1:36, however, lists 'Timna' as one of several names in an undifferentiated list headed 'the sons of Eliphaz,' immediately alongside 'Amalek' -- reading, on its face, as though Timna herself were one of Eliphaz's sons.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Chronicles 1:36's list in the same undifferentiated form as the Masoretic Hebrew, without adding a clarifying word or phrase to distinguish Timna's actual role from the surrounding sons' names; this is a case where every translation surveyed preserves the same compressed, potentially ambiguous phrasing, and the resolution depends on reading 1 Chronicles 1:36 alongside Genesis 36:12 rather than on any translation's own wording.

**Reconciliation:** Hebrew scholars, including the influential nineteenth-century commentator C. F. Keil, note that the name Timna itself, by its grammatical form, is a feminine name -- a detail an ancient Hebrew-literate reader would have recognized immediately, guarding against exactly the misreading a modern English reader unfamiliar with Hebrew grammatical gender might otherwise make. The verse is best understood as an intentionally compressed summary: 1 Chronicles 1:36 lists five actual sons of Eliphaz (Teman, Omar, Zepho, Gatam, Kenaz), then appends 'Timna, and Amalek' as a shorthand way of also including Amalek's line, expecting the reader to already know from Genesis 36 (which 1 Chronicles' genealogies elsewhere consistently assume as background knowledge) that Timna was Amalek's mother, not another son -- an economical style of listing rather than a claim that Timna was male or a direct son of Eliphaz.

### 7. Does Matthew's genealogy skip generations that this book's fuller record of the kings from David to the exile includes?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 3:10-16; Matthew 1:8-11

**The claim:** 1 Chronicles 3:10-16 gives an unbroken, generation-by-generation list of the kings of Judah descended from Solomon down to the Babylonian exile, including Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah in sequence between Joram and Uzziah (Azariah). Matthew 1:8, however, moves directly from 'Joram begat Ozias [Uzziah],' omitting Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah entirely from its genealogy of Christ.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a well-recognized and, among conservative scholars, uncontroversial feature of Matthew's genealogy rather than an error: Matthew's own introduction explicitly organizes his genealogy into three stylized groups of fourteen generations (Matthew 1:17), a structure requiring selective compression consistent with a well-documented ancient Near Eastern and Jewish genealogical convention (the Hebrew word for 'begat' regularly covers 'was the ancestor of' across any number of intervening generations, not exclusively an immediate parent-child link, a pattern already addressed elsewhere in this project regarding several Old Testament genealogies). Matthew's three omitted kings -- Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah -- are additionally the direct descendants of the notoriously wicked Athaliah (herself descended from Ahab and Jezebel's line via her marriage into David's house, 2 Kings 8:18, 26), and some commentators note this may have given Matthew an additional, theologically motivated reason for omitting this specific three-generation branch from his symbolic count, alongside the simple stylistic need to reach fourteen generations per group -- 1 Chronicles' own fuller, unbroken record is not disputed or contradicted by Matthew's selective, patterned genealogy, which does not claim to list every intervening generation.

### 8. Was Jeconiah the son of Josiah, or his grandson?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 3:15-16; Matthew 1:11

**The claim:** 1 Chronicles 3:15-16 lists Josiah's sons (including Jehoiakim) and then, in the next verse, lists 'the sons of Jehoiakim: Jeconiah his son' -- making Jeconiah (Jechoniah) the son of Jehoiakim and the grandson of Josiah. Matthew 1:11, in its compressed genealogy, states 'Josias begat Jechonias,' appearing to skip Jehoiakim and present Jeconiah as Josiah's direct son.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the closely related question addressed elsewhere in this document regarding Matthew's omission of three kings between Joram and Uzziah, Matthew's genealogy again uses 'begat' in the broader, well-attested Hebrew and Greek sense of 'was the ancestor of' rather than requiring an unbroken, single-generation link at every step -- Matthew's own genealogy already demonstrates this convention throughout (compressing many generations across its three fourteen-generation groups), and 1 Chronicles' own fuller, more detailed record of the intervening generation (Jehoiakim) is not disputed by Matthew's more compressed, patterned listing, which does not itself claim to record every generation without omission.

### 9. Was Zerubbabel's father Pedaiah, or Shealtiel?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 3:17-19; Ezra 3:2; Haggai 1:1; Matthew 1:12; Luke 3:27

**The claim:** 1 Chronicles 3:19 states, 'the sons of Pedaiah; Zerubbabel, and Shimei' -- naming Pedaiah as Zerubbabel's father, with Shealtiel listed as Pedaiah's brother in the previous verse. Yet Ezra 3:2, Haggai 1:1, Matthew 1:12, and Luke 3:27 all consistently identify Zerubbabel as 'the son of Shealtiel' instead.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that Shealtiel, Zerubbabel's legal father per the overwhelming weight of biblical testimony, most likely died without a son of his own, and that his brother Pedaiah fathered Zerubbabel on Shealtiel's behalf under the ancient institution of levirate marriage (compare Deuteronomy 25:5-6's provision for a brother to raise up an heir for a deceased, childless brother) -- meaning Zerubbabel was Pedaiah's biological son but Shealtiel's legal son and heir, entitled to be called 'son of Shealtiel' in every genealogical and royal-succession context, which is exactly how every other biblical text besides this single verse in 1 Chronicles consistently identifies him. On this reading, 1 Chronicles 3:19 alone preserves the additional detail of Zerubbabel's biological paternity, a detail of interest to the Chronicler's comprehensive genealogical project, without contradicting the legal paternity every other text (including this same book's own broader genealogical convention of following legal/covenantal descent lines) consistently records.

### 10. How many sons did Benjamin have -- three, five, or ten?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 7:6; 1 Chronicles 8:1-2; Genesis 46:21; Numbers 26:38-41

**The claim:** This book itself gives two different lists of Benjamin's sons: 1 Chronicles 7:6 names three (Bela, Becher, Jediael), while 1 Chronicles 8:1-2 names five (Bela, Ashbel, Aharah, Nohah, Rapha). Genesis 46:21, still earlier, lists ten sons of Benjamin, and Numbers 26:38-41 gives yet another list of five clan-names, only partially overlapping with 1 Chronicles 8's five names.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This document treats this as a genuine, recognized complexity in the Benjaminite genealogical tradition rather than claiming a single, fully certain resolution for every name across all four lists, consistent with this project's practice of candor where the evidence does not clearly settle every detail. The most commonly offered explanations, which are not mutually exclusive, include the following. First, the lists represent different generational scopes. Genesis 46:21's ten names most likely include grandsons alongside direct sons -- several of the ten are elsewhere identified as sons of Bela rather than of Benjamin directly; compare 1 Chronicles 8:3-5's list of Bela's own sons, several of which reappear among Genesis 46:21's ten. Numbers 26 and 1 Chronicles 8 may instead reflect the surviving major clans some generations later, after some lines had died out or been absorbed into others, explaining the smaller, partially different name-sets. Second, this book's own two internal lists (7:6 and 8:1-2) are widely read as serving different purposes. 7:6's brief three-name list appears within a broader summary of several tribes' military registries, while 8:1-2's fuller list opens a dedicated, detailed Benjaminite genealogy leading to Saul's line, and may draw on a different underlying source or emphasize a different genealogical branch than 7:6's more compressed entry. Genuine uncertainty remains among scholars about reconciling every specific name across all four lists in full detail.

## Law and Practice

### 11. Levitical service age lowered to 20 -- does this conflict with Numbers, or does the text explain the change itself?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 23:24-27; Numbers 4:3; Numbers 8:24

**The claim:** Numbers 4 sets the age for Levites beginning their tabernacle transport service at 30 (a figure addressed at greater length in the companion Numbers document, alongside Numbers 8:24's age 25 for a related aspect of service). This book instead states Levites were numbered 'from twenty years old' for service in David's reorganized system (23:24, 27) -- a third, lower figure.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '20' at 1 Chronicles 23:24, 27 and the accompanying explanatory rationale exactly as given, without adjusting the figure to match Numbers; the harmonization is drawn directly from the text's own stated reasoning, not from translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Unlike the Numbers 4/Numbers 8 tension (which the text does not explain directly), this book supplies its own explicit rationale for the change to age 20, within the very same passage: 'for the last words of David were, The LORD, the God of Israel, has given rest to his people, and he dwells in Jerusalem forever; and so the Levites no longer need to carry the tabernacle or any of the things for its service' (23:25-26). The wilderness-era ages (30, and the related 25) were specifically tied to the physically demanding work of dismantling, transporting, and reassembling the portable tabernacle -- labor that became unnecessary once the Ark had a permanent, fixed home in Jerusalem, allowing David to reassign Levites to lighter, more numerous administrative, musical, and gatekeeping duties (23:28-32) starting at a younger age. This is a rare case in this project where the text explicitly states its own reason for changing an earlier figure, rather than leaving readers to infer one.

## Literary Composition

### 12. David's song of thanksgiving in this book closely matches portions of three separate psalms

**References:** 1 Chronicles 16:8-36; Psalm 105:1-15; Psalm 96:1-13; Psalm 106:1, 47-48

**The claim:** When the Ark is brought to Jerusalem, this book records David delivering a song of thanksgiving (16:8-36) that corresponds closely, in overlapping sections, to material also found independently as Psalm 105:1-15, Psalm 96 (nearly in its entirety), and the opening and closing verses of Psalm 106 -- raising a question about whether this represents one single, original composition later split and reused across three separate psalms, or evidence that the Chronicler assembled a composite from already-existing psalms rather than recording David's own words on this specific occasion.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Chronicles 16:8-36 and the three corresponding psalms independently and consistently, without cross-referencing or merging them in the text; the question addressed here concerns compositional history and liturgical practice, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both explanations have serious defenders among conservative scholars, and this entry does not adjudicate between them, since the underlying, uncontested fact -- ancient Israelite worship regularly reused, adapted, and recombined existing liturgical material across different occasions, exactly as modern hymnody does -- makes either reading fully compatible with a high view of the text's reliability: some hold David composed this song as a unified whole on this specific occasion, with portions of it later excerpted and adapted (with minor variations) into the three separate psalms that now appear independently in the Psalter; others hold the Chronicler, writing generations later and drawing on the Psalter as it existed in his own time, assembled this scene using already-established liturgical texts appropriate to the occasion being described, a common and unremarkable compositional practice for historical narrative of this kind, rather than a verbatim transcript of David's own extemporaneous words. Either way, the material itself is not presented as being in conflict -- the same words appearing in more than one context is a feature of how ancient Israelite worship music functioned, not two competing claims about what was actually said or written.

### 13. Are all fourteen 'sons of Heman' actually personal names, or does part of the list form a Hebrew prayer?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 25:4-5

**The claim:** 1 Chronicles 25:4 lists fourteen names as 'the sons of Heman': Bukkiah, Mattaniah, Uzziel, Shebuel, Jerimoth, Hananiah, Hanani, Eliathah, Giddalti, Romamtiezer, Joshbekashah, Mallothi, Hothir, and Mahazioth -- appointed, per verse 5, 'according to the words of God to lift up the horn.' Several of the latter names in this list, when read together in sequence, closely resemble a coherent Hebrew liturgical phrase or prayer rather than a typical string of personal names.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** A number of Hebrew scholars, including several respected conservative commentators, have long observed that the second half of this list reads remarkably like a fragment of liturgical Hebrew when translated as a continuous phrase rather than as isolated names -- roughly, 'Have mercy upon me, O LORD, have mercy upon me; thou art my God; I have magnified and exalted [my] help; sitting in adversity, I have spoken abundance of visions.' Some propose that at least part of what the text presents as personal names may originally have been, or were also intended as, a liturgical composition associated with Heman's musical office. This could be incorporated into the genealogical list either because these were genuinely used as personal names drawn from an existing prayer -- a documented ancient Near Eastern naming practice, giving children names that are themselves short prayers or phrases, comparable to several other theologically-loaded Hebrew names elsewhere in Scripture -- or because the text deliberately preserves a double meaning. This document does not treat this observation as displacing the straightforward reading that these were the names of fourteen actual sons appointed to musical service, which the surrounding verses' administrative, list-making context supports and which the vast majority of translations and commentaries follow. It notes the coincidence as a recognized literary curiosity, not a claim requiring full resolution here.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 14. Who incited David to number Israel -- the LORD, or Satan?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 21:1; 2 Samuel 24:1

**The claim:** This book states Satan incited David to take a census of Israel (21:1); 2 Samuel 24:1, describing the same event, states the LORD incited David to do it.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses as given, without softening either statement; the harmonization is theological/interpretive rather than a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 2 Samuel document, which addresses 2 Samuel 24:1 as its primary text. In brief, the standard reading holds both true at different levels of causation: God, in judgment for an unstated prior sin (2 Samuel 24:1 ties the census to God's anger against Israel), permitted or directed Satan to tempt David, exactly as God later permits Satan to test Job. This book names the proximate tempter; 2 Samuel names the ultimate, permitting cause -- a pattern, not a contradiction, that appears elsewhere in Scripture.

### 15. Who killed Goliath -- David, or Elhanan's brother-of-Goliath, correctly clarified in this book?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 20:5; 2 Samuel 21:19; 1 Samuel 17:49-51

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 17 credits David with killing Goliath the Gittite. 2 Samuel 21:19 (Masoretic Hebrew, read literally) states Elhanan killed Goliath the Gittite. This book, describing the same later battle, states Elhanan killed 'Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite' (20:5) -- widely regarded by textual scholars as preserving the original, correct sense that 2 Samuel 21:19's transmission garbled.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Chronicles 20:5's 'the brother of Goliath' consistently and without dispute; the translation variance in this discrepancy occurs entirely at 2 Samuel 21:19, addressed in the companion 2 Samuel document and the general document set.

**Reconciliation:** Comparing the Hebrew consonants involved across the two parallel verses, most textual scholars conclude 2 Samuel 21:19 suffered a scribal mix-up in transmission (the word for 'the brother of' and the accusative particle before a name closely resemble each other in Hebrew, and a nearby word for 'Bethlehemite' appears to have absorbed what should have been the name 'Lahmi'), while this book's version of the same verse escaped the same corruption and preserves what most scholars regard as the more original reading -- making this book's account the clarifying text for the whole discrepancy, rather than a third, additional puzzle. This is treated at greater length, including full translation-by-translation wording, in this project's general document set and the companion 2 Samuel document.

## Numbers and Text

### 16. Cost of the threshing floor: 600 shekels of gold, or 50 shekels of silver?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 21:25; 2 Samuel 24:24

**The claim:** This book has David pay 600 shekels of gold for 'the place' where the Temple would be built (21:25); 2 Samuel 24:24 has him pay only 50 shekels of silver for the threshing floor and oxen.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both figures as given, without harmonizing them in the text; the resolution is interpretive (different purchases), not a matter of translation.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 2 Samuel document. In brief, the most widely held harmonization treats these as two separate purchases at different scopes: the 600 shekels of gold in this book covers the entire surrounding plot of land on which the Temple would later be built (per 2 Chronicles 3:1), described from a broader, Temple-history perspective, while the 50 shekels of silver in 2 Samuel covers only the threshing floor and oxen used for the immediate sacrifice.

### 17. Years of famine offered to David: three, or seven?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 21:11-12; 2 Samuel 24:13

**The claim:** This book offers David three years of famine as one of three punishment options (21:11-12); 2 Samuel 24:13 (Masoretic Hebrew) offers seven years for the same event.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this book's '3 years' consistently and without dispute; the translation variance in this discrepancy occurs entirely at 2 Samuel 24:13, addressed in the companion 2 Samuel document with full translation-by-translation detail.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 2 Samuel document. In brief, the Septuagint text of 2 Samuel 24:13 itself reads 'three years,' matching this book, and many textual scholars judge the Masoretic Hebrew's 'seven' at 2 Samuel 24:13 to be a copying error, with this book's 'three' widely regarded as preserving the more original figure.

### 18. Do only four listed generations from Levi to Moses fit the scale of Israel's population growth in Egypt?

**References:** 1 Chronicles 23:6, 12-13; Exodus 6:16-20

**The claim:** 1 Chronicles 23:6-13, cataloguing the Levitical divisions, lists only Levi, Kohath, Amram, and then Moses and Aaron -- the same brief, four-generation genealogy already given in Exodus 6:16-20 -- spanning the entire period from Jacob's family entering Egypt to the Exodus, a period Exodus 12:40 elsewhere states lasted 430 years.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question -- whether so few listed generations can accommodate both the stated length of the Egyptian sojourn and Israel's population growth from seventy persons to several hundred thousand by the Exodus -- is addressed at length in this project's Exodus companion document (see its entry on the genealogy from Levi to Moses and the scale of Israel's population growth), which discusses both the well-documented pattern of ancient genealogies naming only prominent ancestors while skipping intervening generations, and the demographic plausibility of substantial natural population growth over four centuries. The same reasoning applies without modification to this book's repetition of the identical brief genealogy for a different, administrative purpose (organizing the Levitical divisions for tabernacle and Temple service) rather than a new instance of the underlying chronological question.
