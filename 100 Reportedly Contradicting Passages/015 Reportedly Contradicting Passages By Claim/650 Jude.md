# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Jude

This document catalogs 3 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Jude, either
entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Titus and 2 Peter
documents, Acts, 1 Corinthians, and Deuteronomy. For each item, this document states the claim
as it is typically raised and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entry:** Entry 1 addresses one of the most
substantively debated questions regarding New Testament citation practice anywhere in this
series -- Jude's direct quotation of the non-canonical book of 1 Enoch, introduced with the
explicit claim that Enoch himself "prophesied" these words. This entry presents the genuine
range of scholarly positions in detail, including a documented distinction between valuing a
source and canonizing it (independently attested at Qumran), a comparison to Paul's own
citations of pagan poets, and a minority position (reportedly held by the theologian Wayne
Grudem) regarding the historical Enoch's own original prophecy.

**A note on candor:** Entry 1 is presented with genuine scholarly diversity of opinion rather
than a single forced resolution, reflecting this question's real significance for how
biblical inspiration and citation are understood.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 2 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing the range of scholarly positions on Jude's use of non-canonical Jewish
literature, including citation to Richard Bauckham, Thomas Schreiner, and a documented
Qumran-based distinction between valuing and canonizing a text.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary text and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. 'Enoch... prophesied' -- Jude directly quotes the non-canonical book of 1 Enoch

**References:** Jude 1:14-15

**The claim:** Jude 14-15 states, 'It was also about these that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, "Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all..."' -- a direct quotation, introduced with the specific claim that Enoch himself 'prophesied' these words, matching 1 Enoch 1:9, part of a well-known Jewish pseudepigraphal work (the 'Book of Watchers') composed centuries after the biblical Enoch's own lifetime, and accepted as canonical Scripture only within the Ethiopian Orthodox and Eritrean Orthodox churches among all Christian traditions. This raises a genuinely significant question: does a canonical New Testament author's explicit claim that Enoch himself 'prophesied' these specific words imply an endorsement of 1 Enoch's own claimed authorship and prophetic authority, and if so, what does this mean for the doctrine of biblical inspiration and the boundaries of the canon?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jude 1:14-15 plainly and consistently as given, preserving Jude's own claim that Enoch 'prophesied' these words; this entry addresses a matter of biblical citation practice and the doctrine of inspiration, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most substantively debated questions regarding New Testament citation practice, and this entry presents the genuine range of scholarly positions in detail rather than a single confident answer. Most commentators (a position explicitly attributed to the biblical scholar Richard Bauckham, and reflected in Thomas Schreiner's own commentary) hold that Jude's introductory language ('prophesied') indicates he regarded this specific saying as a genuine, divinely inspired prophecy -- but that this does not require regarding the entire book of 1 Enoch as canonical Scripture, since ancient Jewish communities (including the community at Qumran, where multiple copies of Enochic literature have been discovered) are independently documented as having valued and preserved certain texts as containing genuine spiritual insight or inspired material without including them in their own recognized canon of Scripture, a documented ancient distinction between valuing a source and canonizing it. On this reading, Jude's own use is comparable in kind, if considerably more direct and extensive, to Paul's own citations of pagan Greek poets elsewhere (Epimenides in Acts 17:28 and Titus 1:12, the latter introduced by Paul with the identical term 'prophet,' addressed in the companion Titus document; Aratus in Acts 17:28; Menander in 1 Corinthians 15:33) -- ancient authors, including biblical ones, could recognize and cite a specific true statement as valuable or even inspired without thereby endorsing the entirety of the work or author it came from, or resolving the question of that work's own broader authorship or canonical status. Some commentators note a genuine complicating factor distinguishing Jude's case from Paul's more incidental citations: Jude's engagement with Enochic tradition and imagery (the 'Watchers,' fallen angels 'kept in eternal chains,' addressed also at Jude 6) runs more deeply and structurally through the letter's own argument than Paul's brief, single-line citations, making the case for mere incidental or rhetorical use somewhat harder to sustain than for Paul's citations. A minority position (reported as personally held by the theologian Wayne Grudem in a recounted conversation) holds that Jude's citation indicates the historical Enoch, prior to the flood, genuinely spoke these specific words as an authentic ancient prophecy, which the much later book of 1 Enoch then preserved (however imperfectly elsewhere) as a 'custodian' of this specific authentic tradition -- meaning Jude cites the original, ancient prophecy accurately, without this requiring any specific view about 1 Enoch's own later, larger composition as a whole. This project does not adjudicate between these positions, noting this remains a genuinely significant and actively discussed question with real implications for how biblical inspiration and citation practice are understood.

### 2. Michael's dispute with the devil over Moses' body -- another apparent citation of non-canonical material

**References:** Jude 1:9; Deuteronomy 34:5-6

**The claim:** Jude 1:9 references a specific episode not found anywhere in the canonical Old Testament: 'the archangel Michael, contending with the devil, disputed about the body of Moses.' Deuteronomy 34:5-6, the only canonical account of Moses' death, states simply that Moses died and that 'he buried him in a valley in the land of Moab... but no one knows the place of his burial to this day,' without any mention of a dispute between Michael and the devil over his body. This specific episode is widely believed by scholars to derive from another Jewish text outside the canon, commonly called the Testament of Moses or Assumption of Moses, of which only a fragmentary, incomplete copy survives (missing precisely the section that would have contained this specific episode, known instead through early patristic references to the lost portion).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jude 1:9 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a further instance of the same citation-practice question addressed in entry 1, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated similarly to entry 1 above, as a further instance of Jude's demonstrated willingness to draw on non-canonical Jewish tradition and literature to illustrate and support his own argument, without this necessarily implying a claim that the source text itself is canonical Scripture. Most commentators read this brief reference the same way they read Jude's Enoch citation -- as drawing on a specific, apparently well-known narrative tradition circulating among Jude's original readers (whether from the Testament/Assumption of Moses directly, or from broader oral tradition this text itself may have also drawn upon) to make a specific rhetorical point (Michael's own restraint, even in dispute with the devil himself, refusing to 'pronounce a blasphemous judgment' and instead simply saying 'The Lord rebuke you,' 1:9b, serving as a model of restraint Jude commends in contrast to the false teachers he condemns for 'blaspheming' spiritual powers they do not understand, 1:8, 10) -- without this citation requiring the entire source document's own broader content or claimed status to be treated as authoritative Scripture. This is generally read as consistent with the broader pattern of citation practice addressed in entry 1 above, rather than as a separate or additional difficulty.

### 3. Jude's close literary relationship with 2 Peter -- addressed at length in the companion 2 Peter document

**References:** Jude 1:4-19; 2 Peter 2:1-22

**The claim:** Jude's own extended condemnation of false teachers (1:4-19) overlaps so substantially in content, structure, and specific illustrations with 2 Peter 2 that some scholars have described the relationship as close enough that 2 Peter's second chapter could accurately be called '2 Jude' -- a literary relationship addressed at length in the companion 2 Peter document regarding that letter's own contested authorship.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jude 1:4-19 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion 2 Peter document for the fuller discussion of this literary relationship and its bearing on questions of authorship.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion 2 Peter document, which addresses the scholarly consensus that 2 Peter drew on Jude as a source (rather than the reverse, or both drawing on a common source), and discusses this literary relationship as one of several significant factors bearing on 2 Peter's own disputed authorship -- the single most contested authorship question in the entire New Testament canon, per that document's own fuller treatment.
