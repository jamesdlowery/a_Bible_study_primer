# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Esther

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual
questions involving the book of Esther, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison
with Daniel and, in one entry, the extra-biblical historian Josephus's chronology. For each
item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV**
specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording
materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the
standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Esther is a short, unified narrative. This document has 7 entries rather
than the 12 used for longer books in this series, consistent with the approach taken for
Ruth, Ezra, and Nehemiah.

**A note on entry 1:** This is a distinctive entry for this project -- since RSV2CE, one of
the ten translations surveyed throughout this series, is a Catholic edition, it is the only
one of the ten to include the deuterocanonical "Additions to Esther," which add extensive,
explicit references to God absent from the other nine translations' Hebrew-based text. This
is a genuine, substantial content difference among the ten translations covered by this
project, not merely a wording variance.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 2 (the Additions to Esther, and Mordecai's age) were
freshly researched and checked against multiple sources for this document, including a
direct confirmation that RSV2CE includes the Additions to Esther in their traditional
narrative position. The remaining entries are chronological, legal, and theological questions
where all ten translations render the same underlying Hebrew text and the harmonization is
exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts directly.

## Textual History

### 1. Nine of the ten translations in this survey never mention God -- one does, extensively

**References:** Esther (entire book); RSV2CE Additions to Esther

**The claim:** The Hebrew book of Esther, translated in nine of the ten versions surveyed in this project, is famous for never once mentioning God, prayer, or any explicitly religious act -- providence is implied throughout (most famously in Mordecai's words at 4:14) but never named. RSV2CE, however, being a Catholic edition, includes the 'Additions to Esther' -- six substantial passages found in the ancient Greek (Septuagint) text of Esther but absent from the Hebrew text underlying the other nine translations -- which add dozens of explicit references to God, including extended prayers by both Mordecai and Esther, not found anywhere in the other nine translations of this book.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** Renders only the Hebrew Masoretic text of Esther, in which God is never once mentioned by name or explicit title; this is the standard Protestant and Jewish canonical text of the book.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the Additions to Esther, restored to their original narrative position within the story (rather than appended at the end, as in the ancient Vulgate and some earlier Catholic Bible editions) -- adding an opening dream-vision for Mordecai, the full text of the royal edicts against and later on behalf of the Jews, extended prayers by Mordecai and Esther explicitly invoking God before her approach to the king, an expanded account of Esther's audience with the king, and a closing interpretation of Mordecai's dream, none of which appear in the other nine translations surveyed here.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction between two claims about the same events, but a genuine, well-documented difference in which underlying ancient text-form each translation is rendering: the Hebrew Masoretic text of Esther (translated by KJV, NKJV, ESV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, AMP, and CSB) contains no divine references at all, while the Greek Septuagint's expanded version of Esther -- accepted as canonical scripture by the Catholic Church (and, in a related but not identical form, by Orthodox churches) and included in RSV2CE as the deuterocanonical portions of Esther -- adds substantial material explicitly supplying the religious framework many ancient readers felt implicit in the Hebrew story. This reflects a real difference in the canon of Scripture recognized by different Christian traditions (addressed in Catholic teaching as the deuterocanonical books, and by Protestant traditions as the non-canonical Apocrypha) rather than a textual error in either version -- both the unadorned Hebrew narrative and the expanded Greek version have been read, for many centuries, as authoritative Scripture by different, longstanding Christian communities.

## Chronology

### 2. Was Mordecai himself exiled in 597 BC -- which would make him well over a century old?

**References:** Esther 2:5-6

**The claim:** Esther 2:5 introduces Mordecai as 'the son of Jair, the son of Shimei, the son of Kish, a Benjamite.' The next verse states, 'who had been carried away from Jerusalem with the captives who had been carried away with Jeconiah king of Judah' (2:6) -- read at face value in English, this relative clause appears to describe Mordecai himself as having been personally exiled in 597 BC, alongside King Jeconiah (Jehoiachin). Since the events of Esther take place during the reign of a Persian king usually identified with Xerxes I (486-465 BC), a literal reading would make Mordecai well over 120 years old at the time of the story -- and Esther, his young cousin whom he raised as a daughter and who is later selected as one of many beautiful young virgins for the king's harem, correspondingly elderly as well.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, LSB, RSV2CE:** Most translations preserve the Hebrew's surface ambiguity in English (leaving 'who' able to refer to either Mordecai or his great-grandfather Kish), while NLT resolves it explicitly toward the ancestral reading; nearly all commentators, regardless of which translation is used, favor understanding the clause as describing Kish rather than Mordecai, given the chronological impossibility of the alternative.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'who had been carried away from Jerusalem,' preserving the ambiguity similarly to the KJV.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'Who had been carried away from Jerusalem,' preserving the same surface ambiguity as the Hebrew, without specifying whether 'who' refers to Mordecai or Kish.
- **NKJV:** NKJV similarly renders 'who had been carried away from Jerusalem,' preserving the same ambiguity.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'who had been taken into exile from Jerusalem,' also preserving the ambiguity.
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'who had been carried into exile from Jerusalem,' with the surrounding context ('Mordecai... who had been carried into exile') leaning toward attaching the clause to Mordecai himself in the English, though NIV study editions commonly footnote the Kish-as-referent resolution.
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'His family had been among those who... had been exiled,' resolving the ambiguity explicitly toward an ancestral, rather than personal, exile.

**Reconciliation:** Nearly all commentators, including several cited in classic and modern commentary traditions, resolve this by noting that the relative clause in verse 6 most naturally attaches, in the underlying Hebrew, to the nearest preceding referent that makes chronological sense -- Kish, Mordecai's great-grandfather named at the end of verse 5 -- rather than to Mordecai himself, several names and one full generation-list removed from the clause. On this reading, it was Kish, not Mordecai, who was carried into exile with Jeconiah in 597 BC, making Mordecai a fourth-generation descendant of the original exile roughly a century later -- a chronologically unremarkable age for the story's events. Some English translations preserve the same surface ambiguity present in the Hebrew (leaving 'who' free to attach to either antecedent), while others resolve it explicitly in one direction or the other.

### 3. Esther's three-day fast, and approaching the king 'on the third day'

**References:** Esther 4:16; Esther 5:1

**The claim:** Esther instructs Mordecai to have the Jewish community in Susa fast on her behalf 'for three days, night or day' before she will risk approaching the king uninvited (4:16). The very next verse of the narrative states, 'on the third day Esther put on her royal robes and went' to the king (5:1) -- read as a strict count, this would mean Esther approached the king before the three full days of fasting were actually completed.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both 'three days' (4:16) and 'on the third day' (5:1) plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing ancient inclusive day-counting convention, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is standardly resolved the same way as several other 'third day' timing questions addressed elsewhere in this project (most notably regarding the resurrection and Jonah): ancient Jewish reckoning commonly counted any part of a day as a full day (inclusive counting), so a fast beginning at some point on day one, continuing through all of day two, and concluding on any part of day three would properly be described both as 'three days' (4:16) and as action occurring 'on the third day' (5:1) without any actual shortfall in the fast's intended duration -- the two statements describe the same inclusive three-day span from two different, entirely compatible angles (the fast's stated length, and the specific day on which it concluded and Esther acted).

## Numbers and Text

### 4. 127 provinces, or 120 satraps -- does Esther's empire match Daniel's?

**References:** Esther 1:1; Daniel 6:1

**The claim:** Esther 1:1 describes Ahasuerus's Persian empire as encompassing '127 provinces, from India to Ethiopia.' Daniel 6:1, describing what is generally understood to be the same Persian imperial administrative structure only a couple of generations earlier, states that Darius 'appointed 120 satraps to be throughout the whole kingdom.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '127 provinces' at Esther 1:1 and '120 satraps' at Daniel 6:1 exactly as given, without adjusting either figure or terminology; the harmonization concerns recognizing a two-tiered administrative structure, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not read these as competing counts of the identical administrative unit, but as two different levels or kinds of administrative division within the same empire: a 'satrap' (Daniel 6:1) was a senior regional governor overseeing a larger administrative satrapy, while a 'province' (medinah, Esther 1:1) was a smaller administrative subdivision, with multiple provinces falling under a single satrap's broader authority -- a two-tiered Persian administrative structure independently attested in extra-biblical historical sources describing the Achaemenid Empire's government. On this reading, 120 satraps overseeing a total of 127 (or more, in different periods) provinces is not only compatible but expected, since the two figures describe different administrative tiers rather than the same tier at two different times.

## Law and Practice

### 5. 'The law of the Medes and Persians, which cannot be revoked' -- a shared legal principle across two books

**References:** Esther 1:19; Esther 8:8; Daniel 6:8, 12, 15

**The claim:** Esther twice invokes a specific legal principle: that a decree issued under the king's seal, once given, cannot be revoked or altered, even by the king himself (1:19, 8:8). Daniel 6:8, 12, and 15 invoke the identical principle in a different narrative context (the plot against Daniel), describing it explicitly as 'the law of the Medes and Persians, which cannot be revoked.' Since both books describe the same specific, seemingly unusual legal doctrine, some ask whether this reflects a genuine, externally attested feature of Persian law, or a shared literary convention between the two books.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this legal principle consistently in both books; the question addressed here concerns historical attestation of Persian law, not a matter of internal biblical inconsistency or translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, since both books describe the same principle consistently and to the same narrative effect (an irrevocable royal decree driving the plot to require a second, counteracting decree rather than a simple reversal) -- the question some raise concerns historical attestation rather than internal biblical consistency. Whether or not this specific 'irrevocability' doctrine is independently and precisely attested in surviving extra-biblical Persian administrative records (a matter of ongoing historical discussion, with the concept of an absolute, self-binding royal decree at least broadly consistent with Persian kings' general practice of ruling through an extensive, often rigid administrative and legal bureaucracy), both biblical books treat it as an established feature of Persian governance well known to their respective narratives' audiences, using it for the same basic narrative purpose in each case.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 6. Identifying Ahasuerus, and the multi-year gap between chapters 1 and 2

**References:** Esther 1:3; Esther 2:16; Esther 3:7

**The claim:** Esther 1:3 dates the king's great feast to 'the third year of his reign.' Esther 2:16 dates Esther's own elevation to queen to 'the seventh year' of the same king's reign -- a gap of roughly four years, during which the narrative records only the search for Vashti's replacement. Ahasuerus is generally identified by scholars with the historically attested Persian king Xerxes I (486-465 BC), whose own extensively documented, and ultimately disastrous, military campaign against Greece occupied much of this same multi-year window -- raising a question, discussed by historians rather than by the biblical text itself, of how the narrative's timeline relates to this independently attested historical gap.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'the third year' (1:3) and 'the seventh year' (2:16) exactly as given; the historical identification of Ahasuerus and the significance of the intervening gap is a matter of historical correlation, not translation choice or internal biblical tension.

**Reconciliation:** This entry, like the wall-completion-time comparison addressed in the companion Nehemiah document, touches on a question of historical correlation rather than internal biblical contradiction, and is included with the same caveat: comparing Esther's internal chronology to reconstructed secular Persian history is a matter of historical identification, not a tension between two biblical texts. On the substance, most scholars who accept the Xerxes identification read the gap between chapters 1 and 2 as entirely compatible with, and in fact illuminated by, Xerxes' own attested absence from Susa during his Greek campaign (480-479 BC), which falls within the several-year window the book itself leaves unaccounted for between the two dated events -- the king's return from a costly, failed campaign providing a plausible occasion for seeking the comfort and diversion Esther 2:1-4 describes him pursuing. This is offered as a supporting historical correlation rather than a claim the biblical text itself makes or requires.

## Theological Tensions

### 7. A book that never names God -- an anomaly among the historical books?

**References:** Esther (entire Hebrew text)

**The claim:** Every other historical book in the Old Testament explicitly and repeatedly names and credits the LORD as the active agent behind Israel's history. Esther's Hebrew text, by contrast, never once mentions God, prayer, sacrifice, or any other explicitly religious practice -- even at moments (Esther's fast, 4:16; the timing of Haman's plot falling on Passover, 3:12) where religious observance would be historically expected -- raising a question about whether this represents a genuine theological anomaly within the canon, addressed in this project's general treatment of the book's textual history (entry 1 above) but worth noting as a distinct literary-theological question in its own right.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** All ten translations of the Hebrew text preserve the same total absence of any direct reference to God throughout the book; this is a matter of literary and theological interpretation, not translation choice.
- **RSV2CE:** Unlike the other nine translations surveyed, RSV2CE is the exception noted in entry 1 above: it includes the Additions to Esther, which supply extensive explicit references to God (including prayers by both Mordecai and Esther) absent from the Hebrew text underlying the other nine translations. The literary-theological question addressed in this entry therefore applies specifically to the Hebrew text as rendered by the other nine translations; RSV2CE's canonical form of the book does not share the same total absence of God-language.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Esther's silence about God as a deliberate literary and theological choice rather than an oversight or theological deficiency: the book repeatedly gestures toward providence through pointed narrative coincidence (the king's sleeplessness leading him to have the royal chronicles read at exactly the right moment, 6:1; Haman's gallows being repurposed for his own execution; Mordecai's own words to Esther, 'who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this,' 4:14, strongly implying an unnamed guiding hand) without ever naming the actor directly -- a technique some scholars connect to the book's setting entirely within the Persian diaspora, outside the land and Temple, where God's people must discern his hidden hand at work in ordinary political and social circumstances rather than through direct prophetic address or miraculous intervention, a fitting narrative strategy for a story about faithful life in exile. This distinctive style is generally read as making a positive theological point about providence operating quietly through ordinary events, rather than as evidence of a gap in the book's underlying theology -- though, as entry 1 above notes, the ancient Greek expansion of the book found in ancient Jewish, Catholic, and Orthodox tradition took the different approach of supplying the explicit religious language many readers across the centuries have felt drawn to add.
