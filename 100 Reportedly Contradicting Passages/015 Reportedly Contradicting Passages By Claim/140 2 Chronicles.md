# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 Chronicles

This document catalogs 13 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of 2
Chronicles, either entirely internal to 2 Chronicles or between 2 Chronicles and its parallel
material in 1-2 Kings, Genesis, Leviticus, and Matthew. For each item, this document states the
claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages
involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable
the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this book's character:** Two entries (1 and 2, the Jehoiachin and Ahaziah regnal
ages) involve disputed verses that live in this book itself, so they are given full,
translation-by-translation treatment here with previously verified wording. Four entries
(10, 11, and part of 8, 9, 12) address the Chronicles side of textual variants and
supplementary narratives already treated with 1-2 Kings as the primary or shorter text; those
are kept brief and cross-referenced rather than repeated. The remaining six entries are
substantially unique to this book, including two genuine, actively debated genealogical
cruxes (Abijah's mother's name and Zechariah's father's name) presented candidly as open
questions where serious scholars have not reached consensus.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly disagreement
remains (entries 4, 5, and 12), an honest account of what is and is not established. Readers
are encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Numbers and Text

### 1. Jehoiachin's age at accession: 18, or 8?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 36:9; 2 Kings 24:8

**The claim:** 2 Chronicles 36:9 (Masoretic Hebrew) states Jehoiachin was 8 years old when he began to reign; 2 Kings 24:8 states 18.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads '18' in the main text (rendered 'eight[teen]' in some print editions to show the harmonizing insertion), with a note on the Masoretic '8.'
- **CSB:** CSB reads '18' in the main text, following the Septuagint/Syriac reading, with a footnote on the Masoretic '8' (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV reads '18' directly in the main text, following the Septuagint/Syriac and 2 Kings 24:8, with a footnote noting the Masoretic '8' (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV follows the Masoretic Text's '8,' preserving the apparent discrepancy with 2 Kings 24:8 in the text itself.
- **NKJV:** NKJV likewise reads '8,' following the Masoretic tradition.
- **NASB:** The current (2020) NASB text reads '18' in the main text; the earlier NASB1995 edition read '8.' If using an NASB1995 copy, expect '8'; 2020 and later printings read '18' (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB, like NASB1995, reads '8' following the Masoretic Text, typically with a footnote on the '18' variant.
- **NIV:** NIV renders '18' in the main text with a footnote noting most Hebrew manuscripts read '8.'
- **NLT:** NLT reads '18' directly in the main text, harmonizing with 2 Kings.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads '8,' following its Masoretic base text, without a harmonizing footnote.

**Reconciliation:** Nearly all modern critical editions and translations follow the Septuagint, several Hebrew manuscripts, and the Syriac Peshitta at this verse, which read '18,' agreeing with 2 Kings -- treating the Masoretic '8' as a scribal error (likely a dropped digit or a corrupted numeral) rather than adopting it as original.

### 2. Ahaziah's age at accession: 22, or 42?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 22:2; 2 Kings 8:26

**The claim:** 2 Chronicles 22:2 (Masoretic Hebrew) states Ahaziah was 42 when he began to reign -- which would make him two years older than his own father Jehoram, who died at 40 (2 Chronicles 21:20). 2 Kings 8:26 states Ahaziah was 22.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads '22,' harmonized with 2 Kings.
- **CSB:** CSB reads '22' in the main text.
- **ESV:** ESV reads '22' directly in the main text, following the Septuagint/Syriac reading and 2 Kings 8:26, with a footnote on the Masoretic '42.'
- **KJV:** KJV reads '42' at 2 Chronicles 22:2, following the Masoretic Text as received.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also reads '42.'
- **NASB:** NASB (both the 1995 and 2020 editions) reads '22' directly in the main text, harmonized with 2 Kings.
- **LSB:** LSB most likely reads '22' as well, given its close tracking of the current NASB text at this type of variant.
- **NIV:** NIV reads '22' in the main text, harmonized with 2 Kings, footnoting the Masoretic '42.'
- **NLT:** NLT reads '22,' following the harmonized reading.
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV reads 'Ahazi'ah was forty-two years old when he began to reign' (verified against the published RSV text), following the Masoretic Hebrew directly rather than harmonizing with 2 Kings 8:26 -- consistent with RSV's general pattern elsewhere of retaining the harder Masoretic reading (e.g. its '8' at Jehoiachin's age and '40,000' at Solomon's stalls); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** The Septuagint, Syriac, and several other ancient witnesses read '22' at this verse as well, agreeing with 2 Kings; the Masoretic '42' is almost universally treated by textual critics as a copying error (likely influenced by the number in an adjacent verse), not as two independently intended figures.

### 3. Solomon's stalls for horses: 4,000 here -- or 40,000, per the parallel in Kings?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 9:25; 1 Kings 4:26

**The claim:** This book states Solomon had 4,000 stalls of horses for his chariots (9:25); the parallel at 1 Kings 4:26 (Masoretic Hebrew) states 40,000.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this book's '4,000' consistently and without dispute; the translation variance in this discrepancy occurs entirely at 1 Kings 4:26, addressed in the companion 1 Kings document with full translation-by-translation detail.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 1 Kings document, which addresses 1 Kings 4:26 as the disputed text. In brief, given that 1 Kings 10:26 elsewhere states Solomon had 1,400 chariots, a ratio requiring 40,000 stalls is widely judged implausible, while 4,000 fits comfortably; most textual scholars conclude 40,000 is a scribal error in the Masoretic transmission of the Kings parallel, with this book's 4,000 widely regarded as the more likely original figure.

## Genealogy

### 4. Abijah's mother: Maacah daughter of Absalom, or Micaiah daughter of Uriel of Gibeah?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 11:20-22; 2 Chronicles 13:2; 1 Kings 15:2; 2 Samuel 14:27

**The claim:** This book itself gives two different identifications for the mother of King Abijah, within the span of only two chapters: 2 Chronicles 11:20-22 names Rehoboam's favorite wife, mother of Abijah, as 'Maacah the daughter of Absalom.' 2 Chronicles 13:2, introducing Abijah's own reign, instead gives his mother's name as 'Micaiah the daughter of Uriel of Gibeah.' Complicating matters further, 2 Samuel 14:27 states Absalom had only one daughter, named Tamar -- not Maacah -- raising a question about how Maacah could be Absalom's literal daughter at all.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, NKJV, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** Most translations retain the two distinct name forms ('Maacah'/'Maachah' at 11:20 and 'Micaiah'/'Michaiah' at 13:2) exactly as the Hebrew text has them in each place, without harmonizing between the two chapters; NIV is a partial exception, harmonizing the name itself to 'Maakah' in both places while still retaining the two different fathers named in each verse. No translation resolves the full puzzle (name, father, and the daughter/granddaughter question) through wording alone -- the harmonization proposed above requires reading the passages together with 2 Samuel 14:27 and 1 Kings 15:10, not adopting any single translation's specific choices.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'Maacah the daughter of Absalom' (11:20) and 'Micaiah the daughter of Uriel of Gibeah' (13:2), retaining both distinct name forms as given in the Hebrew.
- **KJV:** KJV renders both 'Maachah the daughter of Absalom' (11:20) and 'Michaiah the daughter of Uriel of Gibeah' (13:2), preserving both name forms exactly as the Masoretic Hebrew has them.
- **NASB:** NASB likewise retains both distinct forms, 'Maacah the daughter of Absalom' and 'Micaiah the daughter of Uriel of Gibeah.'
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'Maakah daughter of Absalom' (11:20) and 'Maakah daughter of Uriel' (13:2) -- harmonizing the name itself to 'Maakah' in both places while retaining the different fathers ('Absalom' and 'Uriel') as given in each respective verse, a partial harmonization not adopted by most other translations.

**Reconciliation:** The majority scholarly resolution, held since antiquity (reflected in the Jewish Targum's own explanatory tradition on this passage) and adopted by most conservative commentators, treats 'Maacah' and 'Micaiah' as variant forms of the same name (a documented pattern of vowel/consonant shift in Hebrew names), and understands 'daughter of Absalom' in the broader ancient Near Eastern sense of 'descendant of,' commonly used for granddaughters and more distant female descendants without a separate word (a usage this project has documented elsewhere, e.g., regarding Ahaziah's parentage). On this reading, Tamar (Absalom's only named daughter, per 2 Samuel 14:27) married a man named Uriel of Gibeah, and their daughter -- Maacah/Micaiah -- was thus Absalom's granddaughter, correctly called 'daughter of Absalom' in the loose sense while also correctly identified as 'daughter of Uriel' in the direct, literal sense at 2 Chronicles 13:2. This reading is also invoked to resolve a related tension: the same woman is elsewhere named as the mother of both Abijah (2 Chronicles 11:22) and Asa, Abijah's own son and successor (1 Kings 15:10, 13) -- most commentators conclude 'mother' at 1 Kings 15:10 in fact means grandmother (the queen mother/dowager who held that court position under her grandson, a role 1 Kings 15:13 shows her occupying and later being removed from), consistent with the broader pattern of loose generational terminology already established. A minority of scholars instead treat 2 Chronicles 13:2 as reflecting a genuine, independent textual tradition (possibly the more original reading, later harmonized elsewhere) rather than a resolved case of grandmother/granddaughter terminology, and this entry does not claim the matter is settled beyond all scholarly dispute, only that a coherent, non-contradictory reading is available and widely adopted.

### 5. Zechariah's father: Jehoiada, or Berechiah?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 24:20-22; Matthew 23:35; Zechariah 1:1

**The claim:** This book records the priest Zechariah, son of Jehoiada, being stoned to death 'in the court of the house of the LORD' at King Joash's command for rebuking the nation's apostasy (24:20-22). Centuries later, Jesus condemns the scribes and Pharisees for the cumulative guilt of 'all the righteous blood shed on earth, from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah the son of Berechiah, whom you murdered between the temple and the altar' (Matthew 23:35) -- a death matching this book's description of Zechariah son of Jehoiada's murder in vivid, specific detail (the location 'between the temple and the altar' aligns closely with 'the court of the house of the LORD'), yet naming a different father, 'Berechiah' -- which is instead the name given for the father of the very different, much later prophet Zechariah in Zechariah 1:1.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both 'Jehoiada' at 2 Chronicles 24:20-22 and 'Berechiah' at Matthew 23:35 plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the names in either text; this is a live exegetical question, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated question among careful, conservative scholars, without a single fully settled answer, and is presented candidly as such rather than given a manufactured resolution. First, Jehoiada, who the text states lived to 130 years old (24:15), may have had a son named Berechiah who died before Jehoiada himself. 'Zechariah son of Jehoiada' in this book would then refer to a grandson raised and identified by his famous grandfather's name -- a documented ancient practice, comparable to Zechariah the prophet himself being called 'son of Iddo' in Ezra 5:1, skipping his immediate father Berechiah to name his more prominent grandfather. On this reading, the same individual could accurately be called both 'son of Jehoiada' (this book) and 'son of Berechiah' (Matthew), if 'Berechiah' was in fact his immediate, literal father, distinct from the 'Iddo' association with the prophet. Second, Jesus may be referring to an entirely different Zechariah -- most plausibly the minor prophet, explicitly named 'son of Berechiah' in Zechariah 1:1. His own violent death, while not directly narrated anywhere in the Old Testament text that survives, may have been known through a tradition available to Jesus' original audience but since lost to us; some ancient Jewish and early Christian sources associate other, later violent deaths with a temple-related 'Zechariah,' though none can be matched with full confidence to either figure. Third, a minority of scholars, including some who otherwise hold a high view of the text's reliability, consider 'Berechiah' at Matthew 23:35 a very early copying or transmission variation, possibly influenced by a scribe's familiarity with the more famous prophet's patronymic. Some serious commentators reject this as unnecessary given the first two options, while others find it the most straightforward reading of the evidence. No single explanation commands full consensus, and this entry is intentionally presented as an open question.

## Chronology

### 6. Did Josiah's reforms precede, or follow, the discovery of the Book of the Law?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 34:3-8; 2 Kings 22:3-13

**The claim:** This book states that Josiah 'began to seek the God of David his father' in his eighth year (age 16) and began actively purging Judah and Jerusalem of idolatry -- destroying high places, altars, and idols across the land -- in his twelfth year (age 20), fully six years before the Book of the Law is discovered during Temple repairs in his eighteenth year (34:3-8). 2 Kings 22, by contrast, opens its account of Josiah's reforms with the Temple repairs and the book's discovery in his eighteenth year, with no earlier mention of reform activity, reading as though the book's discovery was what first prompted Josiah's reforming zeal.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both accounts plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing each book's differing scope and selective emphasis, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These are best read as complementary rather than conflicting accounts, reflecting each book's own selective emphasis: 2 Kings, more narrowly focused on the specific covenant-renewal crisis triggered by the book's discovery and its immediate national consequences, begins its account of Josiah's reign at that pivotal moment without necessarily implying no prior reform activity had occurred (2 Kings nowhere states Josiah did nothing religiously significant before his eighteenth year); this book, with its characteristic interest in supplying fuller detail on the personal piety and gradual moral development of its kings, traces Josiah's reforming zeal back to its earlier roots, showing a young king's faith and reforming energy building for over a decade before the book's dramatic discovery served as a capstone confirmation and intensification of a reform already well underway, rather than its sole trigger. This is treated as a straightforward case of one account supplementing rather than contradicting a shorter parallel, consistent with a pattern already documented for Manasseh's and Josiah's own death narratives in the companion 2 Kings document.

### 7. The seventy-year exile and the land's unobserved Sabbath years

**References:** 2 Chronicles 36:20-21; Leviticus 25:1-7; Jeremiah 25:11-12

**The claim:** Leviticus 25:1-7 commands a Sabbath year of rest for the land every seventh year. This book states that the seventy-year Babylonian exile corresponded specifically to 'the days that it lay desolate it kept sabbath, to fulfil threescore and ten years' (36:21) -- explaining the seventy-year figure as compensation for the accumulated Sabbath years Israel had failed to observe throughout the preceding centuries, in fulfillment of Jeremiah's prophecy (Jeremiah 25:11-12).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Chronicles 36:21's explanatory statement identically; no translation differences are involved.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction but a case where this book supplies its own explicit theological explanation for a chronological figure (the seventy years) that Jeremiah's prophecy states but does not itself explain -- 2 Chronicles 36:21 directly connects the exile's length to the land's accumulated, unobserved Sabbath-year rest, implying roughly 490 years (seventy sabbatical cycles of seven years each) of prior non-observance across the monarchy period, a figure broadly consistent with standard chronological reckonings of Israel's time in the land from the conquest to the exile. This entry is included as an example, alongside a few others in this project, of an apparent 'unexplained number' in one biblical text (Jeremiah's seventy years) receiving its own explicit, internally-supplied explanation elsewhere in Scripture, rather than being left to speculation.

### 8. Baasha's campaign in the 'thirty-sixth year of Asa' -- after his own recorded death

**References:** 2 Chronicles 16:1; 1 Kings 15:33; 1 Kings 16:6-8

**The claim:** This book states, 'In the thirty-sixth year of the reign of Asa, Baasha king of Israel came up against Judah' (16:1). Yet 1 Kings 15:33 and 16:8 together establish that Baasha began to reign in Asa's third year and reigned 24 years, meaning his death fell in Asa's 26th year -- ten years before this book's stated date for his campaign against Judah.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '36' at this verse and '24 years'/'26th year' at the 1 Kings parallel exactly as given in the Masoretic Hebrew, without adjusting any of the figures; see the companion 1 Kings document for the fuller, unresolved discussion.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 1 Kings document, which presents it candidly as a genuinely unresolved chronological puzzle rather than manufacturing a confident answer: the leading proposed solutions (a scribal transmission error reading '36' for an original '26,' following an observation credited to Josephus; or reckoning the '36th year' from the division of the kingdom under Rehoboam rather than from Asa's own accession) each face real objections from serious conservative scholars, and no single explanation commands full consensus.

### 9. Did Jehoiakim die in Babylon, or in or near Jerusalem?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 36:5-6; 2 Kings 24:1, 6; Jeremiah 22:18-19

**The claim:** 2 Chronicles 36:6 states that Nebuchadnezzar 'came up against' Jehoiakim and 'bound him in fetters, to carry him to Babylon' -- language some read as implying Jehoiakim died in Babylonian captivity. 2 Kings 24:6, by contrast, records his death with the standard formula used for kings who died in Judah ('Jehoiakim slept with his fathers'), and Jeremiah 22:18-19 specifically prophesies that Jehoiakim would receive no formal royal mourning and would be given 'the burial of an ass,' his body 'cast forth beyond the gates of Jerusalem' -- an undignified but distinctly local, Jerusalem-area disposal, not a death and burial in a foreign land.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 2 Chronicles 36:6 states Nebuchadnezzar's intent and action to bind Jehoiakim for removal to Babylon, but -- unlike the parallel account of his son Jehoiachin's actual, completed deportation a few years later (2 Kings 24:15, which explicitly states 'he carried away' the king to Babylon) -- it does not itself state that Jehoiakim's removal was completed or that he died there. The most common harmonization among conservative historians holds that Jehoiakim was bound and perhaps briefly held as a vassal-subject after Nebuchadnezzar's campaign, but died in Jerusalem before an actual deportation to Babylon took place (2 Kings 24:1's account of Jehoiakim serving Nebuchadnezzar for three years as a subject king, then rebelling, fits a scenario of continued residence in Judah rather than exile), with Jeremiah 22:18-19's specific prophecy of a dishonorable, local burial near Jerusalem's gates being the detail 2 Kings' brief formulaic notice of his death is consistent with, even though 2 Kings does not itself narrate the specific manner of burial. On this reading, 2 Chronicles 36:6 describes an action taken against Jehoiakim (being bound for intended removal), not a completed relocation and death in Babylon, leaving 2 Kings' and Jeremiah's accounts of a death and disgraceful burial in the vicinity of Jerusalem undisturbed.

## Geography and Typology

### 10. Was the Temple built on the same 'Mount Moriah' where Abraham nearly sacrificed Isaac?

**References:** 2 Chronicles 3:1; Genesis 22:2

**The claim:** This book identifies the site of Solomon's Temple as 'Mount Moriah... at the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite' (3:1). Genesis 22:2 identifies the location of Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac as 'the land of Moriah,' on 'one of the mountains' God would show him -- raising the question of whether 2 Chronicles is claiming these are the same specific site (many centuries and narrative developments apart), a connection Genesis itself never makes explicit.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Chronicles 3:1's 'Mount Moriah' and Genesis 22:2's 'the land of Moriah' plainly and consistently as given, without adding or removing any explicit cross-reference between them; the connection is a matter of theological and geographical inference from this book's own unique naming, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction (nothing in either text asserts the identification is false) but as a claim of typological and geographical continuity that rests on this book's own explicit naming, not on any statement within Genesis itself -- 2 Chronicles 3:1 is, in fact, the only verse in the Old Testament to name the Temple's location as 'Moriah,' making the connection to Genesis 22 a matter of the Chronicler's own theological interpretation of Jerusalem's significance (linking the site of Israel's central sacrificial worship to Abraham's earlier, paradigmatic act of sacrificial obedience) rather than an independently, externally verifiable historical claim. Most commentators, including those who affirm the connection, note that 'the land of Moriah' in Genesis 22:2 describes a wider region, not a single point, so identifying the Temple mount as a specific peak within that same region is a reasonable and traditional inference rather than a strict logical necessity -- and the connection, whether or not the same literal hilltop is in view, is widely read as carrying real theological weight regardless of the precise geographical question, given how substantially Israelite sacrificial worship is thematically anticipated in the Genesis 22 narrative.

## Literary Composition

### 11. Manasseh's evil reign -- and his otherwise-unrecorded captivity and repentance

**References:** 2 Chronicles 33:1-20; 2 Kings 21:1-18

**The claim:** This book narrates Manasseh's exceptionally wicked, 55-year reign, but uniquely adds an account entirely absent from the parallel record in 2 Kings 21: Manasseh being taken captive to Babylon in bonds, humbling himself and praying to the LORD there, being restored to his throne, and undertaking reforms afterward (33:10-16).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this book's fuller account plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing each book's distinct selective purpose, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 2 Kings document, which addresses 2 Kings 21 as the shorter parallel account. In brief, this is not a contradiction (2 Kings never states or implies Manasseh did not repent, only that it does not mention it) but a difference in selection consistent with each book's distinct purpose: 2 Kings, tracing Judah's cumulative covenant unfaithfulness toward the coming exile, has a clear thematic reason to emphasize Manasseh's guilt and its lasting consequences (even after his own repentance, per 2 Kings 23:26-27, 24:3-4); this book, written after the exile for a returning community needing a message of hope and the possibility of restoration through repentance, has an equally clear thematic reason to include Manasseh's captivity and repentance as an encouraging pattern for its own audience.

### 12. Josiah's death: a fuller, more circumstantial account than the parallel in Kings

**References:** 2 Chronicles 35:20-24; 2 Kings 23:29-30

**The claim:** This book gives a considerably fuller account of Josiah's death than 2 Kings 23:29-30's brief notice: Josiah disguising himself before the battle against Pharaoh Neco, being fatally wounded by archers, telling his servants to remove him from the battle because he was severely wounded, being moved to a second chariot, and dying en route to Jerusalem, followed by a description of Judah's national mourning and Jeremiah's lament for him (35:20-24).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this book's fuller account plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is a matter of recognizing supplementation rather than contradiction, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 2 Kings document. In brief, this is a standard case of one account (this book) narrating at greater length and in more circumstantial detail what a shorter parallel (2 Kings) summarizes briefly, rather than two conflicting versions of the same event -- the core facts agree entirely (Josiah is fatally wounded in battle against Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo and dies before or shortly after reaching Jerusalem), and this book's additional detail supplements rather than contradicts the shorter account in Kings.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 13. Cities exchanged between Solomon and Hiram: received here, given in Kings

**References:** 2 Chronicles 8:1-2; 1 Kings 9:10-14

**The claim:** This book describes Solomon building up 'the cities that Huram had given to him,' settling Israelites there (8:1-2). 1 Kings 9:11-13 instead states that Solomon gave King Hiram twenty cities in Galilee, which Hiram, upon inspection, rejected as unsatisfactory.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this book's account of Solomon building cities Huram had given him, and 1 Kings 9:11-13's account of Solomon giving cities to Hiram, exactly as given; the harmonization rests on reading the two texts as sequential stages of one transaction, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 1 Kings document. In brief, commentators overwhelmingly read these as two stages of a single continuous transaction: Solomon first gave the twenty cities to Hiram as payment (1 Kings 9:11), Hiram inspected and rejected them as unsatisfactory (9:12-13), and Hiram then returned the unwanted cities to Solomon, who subsequently rebuilt, fortified, and resettled them with Israelites -- precisely the stage of the process this book describes.
