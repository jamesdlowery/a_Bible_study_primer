# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Zechariah

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual,
historical, and theological questions involving the book of Zechariah, either entirely
internal to the book or in comparison with Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Jeremiah, Acts, and
Revelation. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes
how the tracked translations render the passages involved (only where the translation's
own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and
gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 2, 3, and 6 are all presented with genuine scholarly
diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution -- Zechariah's authorship across
its two markedly different halves, whether Matthew's account of the triumphal entry involves
one donkey or two, the attribution of a Zechariah citation to "Jeremiah" in Matthew 27:9, and
the crowning of Joshua the high priest are all live questions among serious scholars.

**Verification status:** Entries 2 and 3 were both checked in unusual depth against a wide
range of sources, including a two-part peer-reviewed study (Stephen C. Carlson, Catholic
Biblical Quarterly 81 (2019), cited in full below) specifically devoted to the
"two donkeys" question. Entry 4 and entry 7 both involve genuine, verified translation
variance with real interpretive consequences. The remaining entries are theological and
historical questions where all 27 tracked translations render the same underlying text and the
harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. A book of two halves: chapters 1-8 and 9-14, and the question of common authorship

**References:** Zechariah 1:1-8:23; Zechariah 9:1-14:21

**The claim:** Zechariah's first eight chapters are precisely dated (down to the day, similar to the companion book of Haggai), consist largely of a series of numbered night visions with an interpreting angel, and focus closely on the immediate concerns of the returned exilic community -- Zerubbabel, Joshua the high priest, and the Temple's reconstruction. Chapters 9-14 are markedly different: entirely undated, written in a different literary style (extended oracles rather than visions), never mentioning Zerubbabel, Joshua, or the Temple project by name, and addressing more distant, explicitly eschatological and messianic material -- differences substantial enough that a broad scholarly consensus, dating back centuries, proposes the two sections may derive from different authors and different historical periods, a question directly comparable to the Isaiah 1-39/40-66 authorship debate addressed in the companion Isaiah document.

**How the translations render it:** All 27 tracked translations render the entire 14-chapter book as a single continuous text attributed to Zechariah, without dividing it by proposed date or authorship; the question addressed here is a matter of historical-critical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and long debated compositional question, and this entry does not force a single resolution. The traditional view holds the entire book, both sections, was composed by the single prophet Zechariah, understanding the stylistic and thematic shift between the two halves as reflecting a later stage in the same prophet's ministry, perhaps decades after the Temple's completion (520-516 BC), when his focus naturally shifted from the immediate concerns of Temple reconstruction toward more distant, eschatological hope -- a shift in emphasis and genre within a single career, not evidence of different authorship. The majority critical view, following the same kind of internal evidence addressed regarding Isaiah, holds that chapters 9-14 (sometimes further subdivided into 9-11 and 12-14 as possibly separate units) were composed by one or more later, anonymous prophets working after Zechariah's own lifetime, whose material was eventually appended to and transmitted alongside the original Zechariah's own visions -- a reading some scholars connect to specific historical allusions within chapters 9-14 some believe fit a later period (including proposed references to Greek influence in the region, which would suggest a date after Alexander's conquests, considerably later than Zechariah's own attested sixth-century ministry). As with Isaiah, this project takes no position on which reading is correct, noting only that both the traditional and critical positions have been defended at length by serious scholars.

## Numbers and Text

### 2. 'Mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey' -- did Matthew depict Jesus riding two animals at once?

**References:** Zechariah 9:9; Matthew 21:2-7; Mark 11:1-7; Luke 19:29-35; John 12:14-15

**The claim:** Zechariah 9:9 describes the coming king as 'mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey' -- a construction most Hebrew scholars read as synonymous poetic parallelism describing a single animal in two successive phrases, a common feature of Hebrew poetry. Matthew's account of Jesus' triumphal entry, however, has the disciples bring both 'the donkey and the colt' (21:2, 7), and states the disciples 'put on them their cloaks, and he sat on them' (21:7) -- language some read as depicting Jesus straddling two animals simultaneously, in apparent contrast to Mark 11:1-7, Luke 19:29-35, and John 12:14-15, each of which mentions only a single animal (a colt).

**How the translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, NKJV, LSB, NIV, RSV2CE:** Most translations preserve the Greek text's own grammatical ambiguity regarding what 'them' refers to in Matthew 21:7 (the cloaks, or the two animals), while NASB and NLT each resolve the ambiguity explicitly in the English text -- NASB toward the cloaks, NLT toward Jesus riding only the single colt; this reflects a genuine translation choice at a point of real, actively-studied scholarly debate, not a matter of one translation being simply correct.
- **ESV:** ESV similarly renders 'on which they had put their cloaks, and he sat on them,' preserving the same ambiguity present in the Greek.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'and put on them their clothes, and they set him thereon,' preserving the Greek's own ambiguous plural pronouns without resolving which antecedent (the cloaks or the animals) 'them' refers to.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'and put their coats on them, and He sat on the coats,' resolving the ambiguity toward the cloaks specifically (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT resolves the ambiguity in Matthew 21:7 explicitly, rendering 'They brought the donkey and the colt to him and threw their garments over the colt, and he sat on it' -- clarifying in the English text itself that Jesus rode only the colt (verified against the published text).
- **The remaining 12 tracked translations, all confirmed directly:** **the AKJV** ("put on them their clothes, and they set him thereon"), **the ASV** ("put on them their garments; and he sat thereon"), **the BSB** ("laid their cloaks on them, and Jesus sat on them"), **the CPDV** ("laid their garments on them, and they helped him sit upon them"), **Darby** ("put their garments upon them, and he sat on them"), **the Douay-Rheims Bible** ("laid their garments upon them, and made him sit thereon"), **the ERV** ("covered the donkeys with their coats, and Jesus sat on them"), **the NET Bible** ("placed their cloaks on them, and he sat on them"), **Smith's Literal Translation** ("put on them their garments, and he sat over them"), **Webster's Bible** ("put on them their clothes, and they set him thereon," matching KJV exactly), **the WEB** ("laid their clothes on them; and he sat on them"), and **Young's Literal Translation** ("did put on them their garments, and set him upon them") -- all twelve preserve the same underlying grammatical ambiguity as KJV and ESV, with none resolving explicitly toward either the cloaks or the animals the way NASB and NLT each do.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and actively debated question among careful scholars, addressed at length in Stephen C. Carlson, "'The Jenny and the Colt' in Matthew's Messianic Entry," Catholic Biblical Quarterly 81, no. 1 (2019): 62-84, and no. 2 (2019): 235-251, and this entry does not force a single resolution. The majority position, held by most commentators, reads Matthew as historically accurate but simply more detailed than the other three Gospel accounts -- Matthew alone mentions that a mother donkey accompanied her colt (a natural detail, since a young, unbroken colt would be more readily and calmly led alongside its own mother), with Jesus riding only the colt while the mother walked alongside, and 'them' in 21:7 most naturally read as referring back to the disciples' 'cloaks' (which were placed on both animals) rather than to Jesus physically straddling both simultaneously -- consistent with Mark, Luke, and John's own narrower focus on the one animal Jesus actually rode. A minority of scholars, however, hold that Matthew, writing in Greek for an audience without direct access to the Hebrew Zechariah 9:9 or unfamiliar with Hebrew poetic convention, read the prophecy's parallelism overly literally as describing two separate animals, and constructed his account of the two donkeys' presence specifically to make the prophecy's fulfillment appear more precisely, verbally exact -- a reading some scholars hold reflects a known feature of Matthew's citation technique elsewhere (a tendency toward heightened verbal correspondence with Old Testament wording, sometimes at the cost of a fully smooth narrative), without this being considered by these same scholars to constitute a strict factual error, since the underlying historical event (Jesus riding into Jerusalem on a young donkey in fulfillment of Zechariah's prophecy) remains unaffected either way.

### 3. 'Thirty pieces of silver... thrown to the potter' -- attributed to 'Jeremiah,' not Zechariah

**References:** Zechariah 11:12-13; Matthew 27:9-10; Jeremiah 18:1-4; Jeremiah 19:1-13; Jeremiah 32:6-9

**The claim:** Matthew 27:9-10 states, 'Then was fulfilled what was spoken by the prophet Jeremiah: They took the thirty pieces of silver... and gave them for the potter's field, as the Lord directed me' -- yet no passage in the book of Jeremiah contains this specific wording. The material corresponds much more closely to Zechariah 11:12-13, which has the prophet paid 'thirty pieces of silver' and told to 'throw it to the potter,' which he then throws 'to the potter in the house of the LORD.'

**How the translations render it:** All 27 tracked translations render Matthew 27:9's attribution to 'Jeremiah' exactly as it appears in the underlying Greek manuscripts, without emending it to 'Zechariah' or omitting the prophet's name; no manuscript variant reading 'Zechariah' is attested at this verse in any of the 27 tracked translations' textual basis, meaning this is a feature of the New Testament text itself rather than a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a long-recognized textual puzzle with several seriously proposed resolutions, and this entry does not assert a single definitive answer. The most commonly offered explanation notes that the traditional Hebrew ordering of the 'Latter Prophets' placed Jeremiah first among the collected prophetic scrolls, meaning the entire collection (including Zechariah, near its end) could sometimes be referred to by the name of its first book, much as a modern reader might refer to an entire anthology by its lead-off piece -- on this reading, Matthew's citation of 'Jeremiah' functions similarly to citing 'the Jeremiah scroll' as a way of referencing material found within the broader prophetic collection, not a claim that Jeremiah personally wrote these specific words. A second, widely held explanation holds that Matthew is deliberately weaving together language and imagery from both prophets: Zechariah supplies the specific 'thirty pieces of silver' and 'thrown to the potter' details, while the 'potter's field' itself (which Zechariah's own text never mentions, since Zechariah's potter operates 'in the house of the LORD,' not a field) most closely echoes Jeremiah's own extended, distinctive potter and field imagery and vocabulary (the potter's house of Jeremiah 18:1-4, the purchase of a field in Jeremiah 32:6-9, and the symbolic breaking of a potter's flask in the valley outside Jerusalem in Jeremiah 19:1-13) -- on this reading, Matthew names the prophet whose imagery supplies the passage's most distinctive and unusual element (the field itself), attributing the composite citation to its most substantial single source even though the specific numerical detail comes from Zechariah. A minority of scholars propose a scribal transmission error (an early copyist mistakenly writing 'Jeremiah' for 'Zechariah,' perhaps influenced by a marginal note), though this explanation is generally considered less likely given that 'Jeremiah' appears in every existing Greek manuscript of this verse without a documented textual variant reading 'Zechariah' instead.

## Theological Interpretation

### 4. 'They will look on me, on him whom they have pierced' -- God's own first-person identification with a pierced figure

**References:** Zechariah 12:10; John 19:37; Revelation 1:7

**The claim:** Zechariah 12:10 has God speak in the first person, promising that when a spirit of grace is poured out on Jerusalem, the people 'will look on me, on him whom they have pierced' -- an unusual, almost paradoxical construction in which the speaker (God, using 'me') and the one who was pierced (referred to immediately afterward as 'him') appear to be identified as the same figure, yet described with a shift in grammatical person within a single clause. John 19:37 and Revelation 1:7 both apply this verse directly to Jesus' crucifixion.

**How the translations render it:** This is a genuine and well-documented textual variant with real theological stakes, and extending the survey to 27 translations reveals a richer picture than a single KJV data point could show.
- **"Me" (following the Masoretic Hebrew's majority reading, preserving the direct divine first-person identification):** KJV (already established), and confirmed directly, **the Douay-Rheims Bible, the ASV,** and **YLT** ("they have looked unto Me whom they pierced"). **NASB and CSB** are also reported to render "me" at this verse, preserving the same reading -- and confirmed directly, **the CJB** ("they will look to me, whom they pierced"), **the BSB** ("they will look on Me, the One they have pierced"), **Darby** ("they shall look on me whom they pierced"), **LSB** ("they will look on Me whom they have pierced"), **NKJV** ("they will look on Me whom they pierced"), **AMP** ("they will look at Me whom they have pierced"), **NLT** ("They will look on me whom they have pierced"), **Webster's Bible** ("they shall look upon me whom they have pierced"), **the NET Bible** ("they will look to me, the one they have pierced"), and **the ERV** (Easy-to-Read Version: "They will look to me, the one they stabbed"). **The AKJV is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("they shall look on me whom they have pierced"), matching KJV's wording exactly, as expected given its preservation method. **The WEB is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("they will look to me whom they have pierced"). **The CPDV is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("they will look upon me, whom they have pierced"). **Smith's Literal Translation is also confirmed directly** in this camp ("they looked to me whom they pierced").
- **"Him" (removing the direct first-person divine identification):** confirmed directly, **the RSV** ("they look on him whom they have pierced") and **the NABRE** ("they look on him whom they have thrust through," confirmed directly via its official USCCB text, with an accompanying note that "another possible rendering is 'they shall look to me concerning him... thrust through'" -- meaning NABRE's own editors explicitly flag the alternative reading in their notes even while choosing "him" for the main text); **RSV2CE's Old Testament** would be expected to follow the RSV's "him" reading.
- **A further genuinely distinct construction, confirmed directly and not previously documented:** **the NRSV** departs from its own RSV parent text's plain "him," instead rendering "when they look on the one whom they have pierced" -- a more gender-neutral paraphrase that avoids both "me" and "him" directly in its main text, while its own footnote explicitly states "the Hebrew means 'look on me.'" By direct extension, **the NRSV-CE** would be expected to follow this same distinct "the one" construction rather than simply matching RSV's "him."
- **A hybrid construction retaining both pronouns within the same verse:** **ESV** is confirmed directly rendering "they will look on me, on him whom they have pierced" -- preserving the Hebrew's own difficult, dual-pronoun construction directly in English rather than resolving it toward either "me" alone or "him" alone. **NIV** similarly renders "They will look on me, the one they have pierced, and they will mourn for him" -- also retaining both pronouns, opening with "me" before shifting to "him" partway through the verse.
- **Not independently confirmed in this research pass:** TRB was not checked against its own text for this specific verse.

**Reconciliation:** Most conservative commentators read this shift from first to third person within the same verse as a deliberate and theologically significant feature of the original Hebrew, rather than a copying error -- the verse's own grammar holds together an identification between the LORD speaking and the pierced figure being mourned, consistent with later, fuller New Testament revelation regarding the incarnate Son's own full identification with God while remaining personally distinguishable within the Godhead (addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding related Old Testament theophanies and christological readings, including Isaiah 6 and Daniel 7's 'son of man' vision). Some ancient scribes, apparently finding the construction difficult or theologically startling in isolation, produced a small number of variant Hebrew manuscripts reading 'him' instead of 'me' at the verse's opening (removing the direct first-person divine identification), a variant reflected in a minority of ancient versions, though the great majority of Hebrew manuscripts and the standard Masoretic tradition read 'me,' which the majority of tracked translations follow -- some scholars specifically note this is the harder, less theologically comfortable reading, and therefore, by the ordinary text-critical principle that scribes are more likely to smooth over a difficult reading than introduce one, the more likely original text. Notably, at least two of the tracked translations (ESV and NIV) address the difficulty not by choosing one pronoun over the other but by retaining both within the verse, directly mirroring the Hebrew's own unresolved grammatical shift rather than resolving it in either direction.

### 5. The LORD's feet standing on the Mount of Olives, splitting it in two -- the same location as Christ's ascension

**References:** Zechariah 14:1-4; Acts 1:9-12

**The claim:** Zechariah 14:4 describes a climactic future 'day of the LORD' in which God himself will stand 'on the Mount of Olives, which lies before Jerusalem on the east,' and the mountain 'shall be split in two, from east to west, by a very wide valley' -- a specific, striking geographic detail. Acts 1:9-12 records Jesus' own ascension into heaven occurring from this same Mount of Olives, with the accompanying angels promising 'this Jesus... will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven' -- raising a question about whether these two texts describe or anticipate the same specific future event at the same specific location.

**How the translations render it:** All 27 tracked translations render Zechariah 14:4 and Acts 1:9-12 plainly and consistently as given, without further specifying the relationship between the two events in the text itself; this is a matter of eschatological interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Many commentators find the shared geographic location a meaningful, and likely deliberate, connection between Zechariah's climactic 'day of the LORD' oracle and the New Testament's own promise of Christ's future return, reading Acts 1:11's promise that Jesus will return 'in the same way' (understood by many to include returning to the same specific location) as directly anticipating a future fulfillment of Zechariah 14's dramatic vision, with the two texts describing the same climactic, future divine intervention at the same specific geographic point. This project does not adjudicate between the range of more detailed positions various interpretive schools hold regarding the precise timing, sequence, and further details of this event's fulfillment (a question bound up with the same broader range of eschatological interpretation this project has declined to resolve at several other points, including regarding Daniel's 'seventy weeks' and Ezekiel's Gog and Magog), noting only that the connection between Zechariah's ancient prophecy and the New Testament's promise of Christ's return to the same location is widely recognized across a broad range of interpretive traditions, whatever their further disagreements about its precise unfolding.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 6. Joshua the high priest is crowned -- an act ordinarily reserved for kings

**References:** Zechariah 6:9-15

**The claim:** Zechariah 6:9-15 describes an unusual symbolic act: crowns are made from silver and gold and placed on the head of Joshua, the high priest -- crowning being ordinarily and exclusively an act performed for kings, not priests, throughout the rest of the Old Testament. The prophecy accompanying this act refers to a coming figure called 'the Branch' (a recognized messianic title elsewhere in Scripture, compare Jeremiah 23:5, 33:15) who will 'build the temple of the LORD' and 'sit and rule on his throne,' with 'a priest on his throne' and 'the counsel of peace' between the two offices -- combining royal and priestly roles in a single figure in a way that has led some scholars to propose the passage's original text named both Joshua and Zerubbabel (the actual Davidic-line governor) as recipients of two separate crowns, with the current text having lost or obscured a reference to Zerubbabel specifically.

**How the translations render it:** All 27 tracked translations render Zechariah 6:9-15 exactly as given, crowning Joshua and not Zerubbabel in the text as it stands, without adding a reference to Zerubbabel being crowned; the question of whether the original oracle involved a second crown for Zerubbabel is a matter of historical-critical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated textual and historical question, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Some scholars propose that the original oracle envisioned two crowns -- one for Joshua as high priest, one for Zerubbabel as the reigning Davidic governor -- symbolizing a coordinated priestly-royal partnership, and that Zerubbabel's own crowning may have been quietly dropped from the text's transmission (or never actually carried out) due to the political sensitivity of publicly crowning a living descendant of David under Persian imperial oversight, which could have been read as outright rebellion against Persian rule. Other scholars read the text as originally intended exactly as it stands, with Joshua alone symbolically crowned as a forward-looking, prophetic sign of a future messianic figure (the Branch) who would combine both priestly and royal roles in a single person -- a role neither Joshua nor Zerubbabel personally fulfilled in their own lifetimes, but which most Christian interpreters read as directly anticipating Christ's own combined priestly and kingly office (developed at length in Hebrews' treatment of Christ as a priest 'after the order of Melchizedek,' addressed in this project's discussion of Psalm 110 and related passages), making the crowning of a priest, rather than the reigning governor, itself a deliberate and meaningful prophetic sign rather than an incomplete or garbled text.

### 7. 'What are these wounds between your hands?' -- often popularly connected to Christ, but originally about disgraced false prophets

**References:** Zechariah 13:2-6

**The claim:** Zechariah 13:6 has someone ask a figure, 'What are these wounds on your back?' (or, in some translations, 'between your hands'), to which the figure replies, 'The wounds I received in the house of my friends' -- language some readers, especially in popular devotional use, have connected to Christ's own crucifixion wounds. Read in its actual context (13:2-6), however, the passage describes a future day when false prophets will be so thoroughly disgraced and rejected that they will deny their own prophetic identity, and the specific 'wounds' in question are most naturally read as self-inflicted marks from pagan ecstatic prophetic rituals (compare the Baal prophets who 'cut themselves... as was their custom' in 1 Kings 18:28) -- wounds a disgraced false prophet would now falsely claim were received in an ordinary domestic accident 'in the house of my friends,' rather than admitting their true, discrediting origin.

**How the translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** Translations vary between 'hands' (KJV, following an older reading tradition that most directly invites the popular Christological association) and other renderings ('back,' ESV; 'on your body,' NIV; 'between your arms,' NASB and NKJV) that more directly reflect the Hebrew term's likely original sense and better fit the passage's own clear context regarding disgraced false prophets; this genuine translation variance affects how readily an English reader might mistakenly connect this verse to Christ apart from reading its actual surrounding context.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'What are these wounds on your back?' following a different, and more contextually apt, reading of the underlying Hebrew term (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'What are these wounds in thine hands?' -- a rendering whose wording most directly invites the popular (though contextually mistaken) association with Christ's crucifixion wounds.
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'What are these wounds between your arms?' -- matching NASB's wording exactly, and notably departing from the KJV's 'hands' despite NKJV's usual close tracking of the KJV tradition (verified against the published text).
- **The "hands" camp, extended:** confirmed directly, **the AKJV** ("wounds in your hands," matching KJV exactly), **the CPDV** ("wounds in the middle of your hands"), **Darby** ("wounds in thy hands"), **Webster's Bible** ("wounds in thy hands," matching KJV exactly), **Young's Literal Translation** ("wounds in thy hands"), **the ERV** ("wounds in your hands"), and **the Douay-Rheims Bible** ("wounds in the midst of thy hands").
- **The "arms" camp, extended:** confirmed directly, **the ASV** ("wounds between thine arms") and **the WEB** ("wounds between your arms").
- **A third, genuinely distinct camp, confirmed directly and not previously documented for this entry:** **the NET Bible** ("wounds on your chest") and **the BSB** ("wounds on your chest") independently arrive at the same "chest" rendering -- distinct from both "hands" and "arms."
- **Smith's Literal Translation** offers its own hybrid phrasing, confirmed directly: "wounds between thy hands" -- combining the "hands" camp's noun with the "between" construction otherwise associated with the "arms" camp.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'What are these wounds between your arms?'
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'What are these wounds on your body?' similarly avoiding 'hands.'

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable case, addressed at points elsewhere in this project regarding similarly popular but contextually mistaken applications of specific verses, of a phrase's popular devotional use diverging from its actual, original literary context. The passage's own immediate surrounding verses (13:2-5) are explicitly and exclusively about the shameful future exposure and self-denial of false prophets, with no messianic or Christological content indicated anywhere in the passage's own stated subject or context -- the resemblance some readers find to Christ's own crucifixion wounds rests entirely on the isolated phrase's surface wording apart from its clear surrounding context, which identifies the wounded figure as a disgraced false prophet attempting to conceal self-inflicted ritual wounds, not as any messianic figure. Most careful commentators, including many with a high view of the book's messianic content elsewhere (addressed in entries 2, 3, and 4 above), do not read this specific verse as messianic prophecy at all, treating the popular association as a case of a memorable phrase being detached from its actual, unambiguous context rather than a genuine feature of the passage's own intended meaning.
