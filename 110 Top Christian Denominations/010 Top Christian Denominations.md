# Major U.S. Christian Denominations

This section profiles 27 prominent Christian denominations and denominational families in the United States, ranked by adherents per the 2020 U.S. Religion Census -- the most comprehensive, methodologically consistent count available for this kind of comparison -- with the following disclosed exceptions. Three bodies have no usable Census figure and are placed at the end of the list rather than ranked: the Global Methodist Church (formed in 2022, after the Census), the United Pentecostal Church International (the Census counted its congregations but no adherents), and the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church, which does have a Census figure (~305,000) but was added specifically for doctrinal breadth -- it completes the historically Black Methodist family represented elsewhere in this section -- and happens to fall in that position by size in any case. Two entries carry a denomination-reported figure alongside the Census bracket because their exact 2020 Census counts were not verified for this edition: the Christian and Missionary Alliance and the Presbyterian Church in America, both of which the Census places in the 100,000–999,999 range. Several historically Black denominations (the Church of God in Christ, AME Zion, and the two National Baptist conventions) are known to be substantially under-counted by the Census, which locates congregations from address lists rather than receiving denominational reports; each such entry gives both figures and is ranked by the Census number for consistency. Recently-split bodies (the United Methodist Church and the Global Methodist Church) are listed as separate entries with their own distinct positions, rather than blended into one profile that would erase a real, current disagreement. The UPCI is the only Oneness (non-Trinitarian) body profiled here, a genuinely distinct doctrinal position none of the other 26 entries hold.

For each denomination, this section documents three tables of positions, 31 in all: eight core doctrinal positions (view of Scripture, salvation, baptism, the Lord's Supper, church government, eschatology, spiritual gifts, and women's ordination); ten ethical and lifestyle positions (abortion, homosexuality and same-sex marriage, alcohol, divorce and remarriage, contraception, gambling, dancing, premarital sex and cohabitation, war and pacifism, and the death penalty); and thirteen other divisive positions -- the questions that have most often split churches from one another without fitting the first two tables (Scripture and Tradition; the Trinity and the person of Christ; predestination and free will; sanctification and holiness; hell and the fate of the lost; Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead; the day, style and liturgy of worship; church and state; race and the church; creation and the age of the earth; tithing and the prosperity gospel; Bible translations and KJV-onlyism; and marriage roles). Each ethical/lifestyle and other divisive position is sourced explicitly -- distinguishing an official confessional statement from a documented general practice or cultural norm -- since these are different kinds of claims with different kinds of evidence behind them. Where a tradition is internally split or actively debating a question, this section says so directly rather than forcing a single label onto a live disagreement.

**A note on alcohol specifically:** nearly every tradition profiled in this section distinguishes moderate use (which most, though not all, permit) from drunkenness/overuse, which is treated as sinful essentially universally, including by traditions that otherwise permit or even use alcohol sacramentally. Entries note this distinction explicitly rather than let a single word ("permitted" or "prohibited") obscure it.

**A note on posture:** this section describes what each denomination officially teaches or has documented, not a judgment about which position is correct. Where official teaching and the private views or practice of a denomination's own members diverge -- as polling shows is often the case, particularly on abortion -- this section notes that candidly rather than treating official statements as a complete description of what everyone in a tradition actually believes.

**A note on Messianic Judaism:** this movement, combining Jewish religious practice and identity with faith in Yeshua (Jesus) as the promised Messiah, is not profiled as a separate entry in this section, unlike its coverage under Prominent English Study Bibles (in connection with the Complete Jewish Bible). Readers looking for its fuller doctrinal profile should consult that section instead.

## 1. The Catholic Church

**U.S. adherents:** ~61,858,137 (2020 U.S. Religion Census) — the largest single Christian body in the country by a wide margin.
**Governance:** Episcopal (bishops in apostolic succession, under the Pope as Bishop of Rome); U.S. dioceses coordinate through the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops (USCCB), though each diocese is governed by its own bishop.
**Origin:** Traces its institutional continuity to the apostolic church; the term "Catholic" in this organized sense is attested from the 2nd century.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Scripture and Sacred Tradition together form a single, authoritative deposit of faith, interpreted authoritatively by the Magisterium (the teaching office of the bishops in communion with the Pope). |
| Salvation | Grace-initiated, received through faith and expressed in cooperation with grace through the sacraments and good works; final salvation is not considered settled by a single past decision. |
| Baptism | Infant baptism is the norm; baptism is held to actually confer regenerating grace (not merely symbolize it). **Mode:** both immersion and pouring (affusion) are valid; canon law and the Catechism describe immersion as the fuller, more expressive sign (and it remains the customary mode in Eastern Catholic rites), but pouring is in practice the most common mode for infant baptism in the Latin (Western) rite today. Canon 854 permits only immersion or pouring, so sprinkling is not a permitted mode. |
| The Lord's Supper | The Eucharist — transubstantiation: the bread and wine are believed to become the actual body and blood of Christ while retaining their outward appearance. Central to weekly (often daily) worship. |
| Church government | Episcopal, hierarchical, globally unified under the papacy. |
| Eschatology | No official, detailed end-times timeline is dogmatically required; affirms Christ's return, final judgment, and the resurrection of the body as core creedal commitments, and the Church "has rejected even modified forms" of millenarianism (Catechism §676), which places it in the amillennial camp. |
| Spiritual gifts | Officially open to the continuation of charismatic gifts (affirmed particularly since the mid-20th-century Catholic Charismatic Renewal), though not central to ordinary parish life for most Catholics. |
| Women's ordination | Reserved to men; officially closed to further debate (*Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*, 1994). Women may serve as religious sisters, in lay ministry, and in most parish leadership roles short of ordained priesthood. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | Opposed in all cases where the direct intent is to end the life of the unborn; officially unchanged and described by the Church itself as unchangeable (Catechism §2271). | Official Magisterial teaching |
| Homosexuality | Attraction itself is not considered sinful; homosexual acts are taught to be "intrinsically disordered" and not morally permissible. Persons are to be "accepted with respect, compassion, and sensitivity" (Catechism §2357–2358). Same-sex marriage is not recognized. | Official Magisterial teaching |
| Alcohol | Moderate use is accepted, and wine is used sacramentally in the Eucharist itself; drunkenness, however, is treated as a real sin (traditionally classed among the "vices" opposed to the virtue of temperance), distinct from the acceptance of moderate use. | Longstanding practice, not a matter of official prohibition |
| Divorce and remarriage | Marriage is held to be indissoluble; a divorced Catholic may not remarry in the Church without an annulment (a declaration that no valid sacramental marriage existed in the first place). Remarrying civilly without an annulment bars a person from receiving Communion under ordinary circumstances. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §1650-1651) |
| Contraception | Artificial contraception is opposed in all forms; only natural family planning (periodic abstinence) is permitted for spacing births. This teaching, restated in *Humanae Vitae* (1968), remains official and unchanged, though widely disregarded in practice by many self-identified American Catholics. | Official Magisterial teaching (*Humanae Vitae*, 1968) |
| Gambling | Not prohibited in itself; the Catechism treats games of chance as morally neutral unless they become excessive or deprive others of necessities. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §2413) |
| Dancing | No doctrinal position; not treated as a moral category. | Not addressed |
| Premarital sex/cohabitation | Opposed; chastity outside marriage is required, consistent with the Church's broader sexual ethic. | Official Magisterial teaching |
| War/pacifism | Just war theory, not pacifism, is the official position — force may be used under specific, narrowly defined conditions (just cause, last resort, proportionality). | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §2309) |
| Death penalty | Opposed in all cases as of a 2018 revision to the Catechism, which now describes capital punishment as "inadmissible" and commits the Church to working for its abolition worldwide — a significant change from the Church's earlier, more permissive language. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §2267, revised 2018) |

### Other divisive positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture and Tradition | Scripture and Sacred Tradition are two modes of a single deposit of faith, flowing from the same divine wellspring; Tradition is authoritative in its own right and the Magisterium alone interprets both authentically. *Sola scriptura* is rejected. | Official Magisterial teaching (*Dei Verbum* §9–10, 1965; Catechism §80–86) |
| The Trinity and the person of Christ | Nicene-Constantinopolitan Trinity and Chalcedonian Christology (one person, two natures); the Western *filioque* ("and the Son") is affirmed in the Creed. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §232–267, §464–483) |
| Predestination and free will | Grace has absolute priority, but it is received by a free will that cooperates with it; God "predestines no one to go to hell" (Catechism §1037). Double predestination was rejected at Trent; the Thomist–Molinist debate on how grace and freedom relate remains open and undecided. | Official Magisterial teaching (Council of Trent, Decree on Justification, 1547; Catechism §600, §1037, §2008) |
| Sanctification and holiness | Justification and sanctification are not sharply separated: the justified grow in holiness through grace, the sacraments and merit cooperating with grace; final purification after death (purgatory) completes it for those who die in grace but imperfectly purified. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §1987–2029, §1030–1032) |
| Hell, judgment and the fate of the lost | Hell is real and eternal ("eternal fire"), a "state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God" (§1033); "God predestines no one to go to hell" (§1037), and those who through no fault of their own do not know the Gospel but sincerely seek God "may achieve eternal salvation" (§847). | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §1033–1037, §847) |
| Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead | Mary is honored as Mother of God, Immaculately Conceived (1854), Ever-Virgin and Assumed into heaven (1950); the saints are venerated (not worshipped) and asked to intercede; prayer and Masses for the dead are offered on the basis of purgatory. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §487–511, §956–958, §966, §971, §1030–1032) |
| Worship: day, style and liturgy | Sunday is the obligatory day of worship (the Mass); worship is fixed and liturgical, following the Roman Missal (1970 *Novus Ordo*; the pre-1970 Latin Mass is permitted only under restrictions since *Traditionis Custodes*, 2021) and the liturgical calendar. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §2174–2188; *Sacrosanctum Concilium*, 1963; *Traditionis Custodes*, 2021) |
| Church and state / political engagement | Religious liberty for all is affirmed (*Dignitatis Humanae*, 1965); the Church engages public policy directly through the USCCB (e.g., *Forming Consciences for Faithful Citizenship*) without endorsing parties or candidates; clergy are barred from holding public office (Canon 285 §3). | Official Magisterial teaching and USCCB documents |
| Race, ethnicity and the church | Racism is a sin that denies the dignity of the human person; the U.S. bishops have issued two pastoral letters on it (*Brothers and Sisters to Us*, 1979; *Open Wide Our Hearts*, 2018) acknowledging the Church's own historical complicity. | Official USCCB pastoral letters (1979, 2018) |
| Creation and the age of the earth | No required position on the age of the earth; the Catechism welcomes the sciences' findings on "the age and dimensions of the cosmos, the development of life-forms and the appearance of man" (§283) and says the account of the fall in Genesis 3 "uses figurative language" (§390); the evolution of the human body from pre-existing living matter may be discussed provided the soul's immediate creation by God and descent from a single Adam are held (*Humani Generis* §36–37, 1950; John Paul II, 1996). | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §283–289, §390; *Humani Generis*, 1950) |
| Tithing and the prosperity gospel | No fixed tithe is required; the faithful "have the duty of providing for the material needs of the Church, each according to his abilities," a duty the Catechism states alongside the five precepts of the Church (§2043). The prosperity gospel is not taught and has been criticized in papal addresses, though not in a formal document. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §2043); prosperity-gospel criticism in papal remarks, not a formal document |
| Bible translations and KJV-onlyism | Translations used in worship or published as Catholic require ecclesiastical approval (an *imprimatur*); the Latin *Nova Vulgata* is the reference text for liturgical translation; in the U.S. the lectionary is based on the New American Bible. KJV-onlyism has no standing. | Official (Code of Canon Law c. 825; *Liturgiam Authenticam*, 2001) |
| Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian | Husband and wife have equal dignity and are called to mutual self-giving; Ephesians 5 is read as "mutual subjection" with the husband's headship as servant love (*Mulieris Dignitatem*, 1988). Neither the complementarian nor the egalitarian label is adopted. | Official Magisterial teaching (Catechism §1601–1666, §2331–2336; *Mulieris Dignitatem*, 1988) |

**Note on internal diversity:** Official Magisterial teaching and the practice or private views of individual American Catholics diverge substantially on some of these points, particularly abortion — polling consistently finds a majority of self-identified U.S. Catholics support legal abortion in most or all cases, a gap the entries in this section describe candidly rather than treat as settling the question of the Church's own official position.

## 2. Non-denominational Christian Churches

**U.S. adherents:** ~21,095,641 (2020 U.S. Religion Census) — the second-largest Christian grouping in the country, encompassing thousands of independent congregations rather than one organized body.
**Governance:** Congregational; by definition, not affiliated with any denominational hierarchy, though many voluntarily network through associations (e.g., the Association of Related Churches).

Because this category spans thousands of independent congregations, no single doctrinal or confessional statement applies the way it does for the other entries in this section. The summary below describes the general pattern most common among U.S. non-denominational churches, not an enforceable, uniform position.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Most hold to some form of biblical inerrancy or high view of Scripture's authority, consistent with broad evangelicalism, though no single confessional statement binds all congregations under this label. |
| Salvation | Predominantly evangelical/Arminian-leaning in practice, though this varies by congregation; no single confessional position. |
| Baptism | Generally believer's baptism by immersion, the most common practice among non-denominational churches, though this varies by congregation. |
| The Lord's Supper | Generally a memorial view, consistent with broad evangelical practice, though this varies by congregation. |
| Church government | Congregational, by definition. |
| Eschatology | No single position; varies by congregation. |
| Spiritual gifts | Ranges widely from cessationist to continuationist depending on the specific congregation's tradition of origin. |
| Women's ordination | Varies considerably by congregation; no single position. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | The large majority of non-denominational churches, being predominantly evangelical in orientation, oppose abortion, though this is a general pattern rather than an enforceable position given the absence of a confessional body. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Homosexuality | The large majority hold same-sex marriage to be outside biblical teaching, though, as above, this is a general pattern rather than a uniform, enforceable position. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Alcohol | No single position; varies by congregation, consistent with the absence of any binding confessional statement for this category as a whole. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Divorce and remarriage | Varies considerably by congregation; most permit remarriage on some biblical grounds, consistent with broad evangelical practice, though there is no confessional body to survey for a uniform position. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Contraception | No single position; varies by congregation. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Gambling | Generally discouraged among conservative evangelical congregations, though this varies and is not uniformly mandated. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Dancing | No single position; varies considerably by congregation. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Premarital sex/cohabitation | The large majority of non-denominational churches, being predominantly evangelical, oppose both, though this is a general pattern rather than an enforceable position. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| War/pacifism | No single position; varies by congregation. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Death penalty | No single position; varies by congregation. | Not applicable — no confessional body |

### Other divisive positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture and Tradition | Scripture alone is treated as the final authority; historic creeds are generally respected but not binding, and many congregations publish only a short statement of faith. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| The Trinity and the person of Christ | Almost universally Trinitarian and orthodox on the deity and humanity of Christ; a congregation's statement of faith is usually the only written source. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Predestination and free will | Mixed: many lean Arminian/"free will" with an "eternal security" emphasis, while a substantial Reformed-leaning stream (influenced by the Young, Restless and Reformed movement) holds unconditional election. No common position exists. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Sanctification and holiness | Progressive sanctification through Scripture, prayer and community is the usual teaching; the Wesleyan "second blessing" is rare outside Pentecostal-influenced congregations. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Hell, judgment and the fate of the lost | Eternal conscious punishment is the majority position; annihilationism and a hopeful agnosticism about the fate of the lost appear in a minority of congregations, and public preaching on hell is often muted. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead | Mary is honored as the mother of Jesus but not venerated or invoked; prayer to or for the dead is not practiced. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Worship: day, style and liturgy | Sunday worship, non-liturgical and typically contemporary (band-led music, extended sermon); no shared calendar or prayer book, though some congregations observe Advent and Lent. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Church and state / political engagement | Varies widely: many congregations are politically conservative and some host voter-registration or issue advocacy, while others deliberately avoid political speech from the platform. No common policy exists. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Race, ethnicity and the church | Varies by congregation; the "multi-ethnic church" movement is strongest in this stream, yet many congregations remain racially homogeneous. No common statement exists. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Creation and the age of the earth | Varies; a literal, young-earth reading is common in conservative congregations, while others leave the question open or accept an old earth. No common position exists. | Not applicable — no confessional body |
| Tithing and the prosperity gospel | Tithing (10%) is widely taught as the baseline of giving; the prosperity gospel is explicitly taught in some large congregations and firmly rejected in most others. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Bible translations and KJV-onlyism | No shared translation; the NIV, ESV, NLT and CSB predominate in preaching. KJV-onlyism is rare in this stream and mostly confined to independent fundamental Baptist churches, which are not profiled here. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |
| Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian | Mostly complementarian in practice (male lead pastors and elders), with a visible egalitarian minority; positions are set congregation by congregation. | General pattern, not a confessional statement; not independently documented |

## 3. Southern Baptist Convention (SBC)

**U.S. adherents:** ~17,649,040 (2020 U.S. Religion Census); the largest Protestant denomination in the U.S. by membership and by number of congregations (over 47,000).
**Governance:** Congregational — each local church is independently self-governing; the Convention itself has no authority to bind a local church's beliefs or practice, only to determine which churches are recognized as in "friendly cooperation" with it.
**Confessional statement:** The Baptist Faith and Message (BF&M), most recently revised in 2000.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Biblical inerrancy explicitly affirmed in the BF&M's own language ("truth, without any mixture of error, for its matter"). |
| Salvation | Predominantly Arminian-leaning to moderately Reformed in practice, spanning a real range; the BF&M itself does not mandate a single Calvinist/Arminian position, and both views are represented among Southern Baptist pastors and theologians. |
| Baptism | Believer's baptism only; infant baptism is not practiced or recognized as valid baptism. **Mode:** immersion exclusively — considered the only valid mode, a defining Baptist distinctive (reflected in the denomination's own name); sprinkling or pouring are not recognized as valid Christian baptism. |
| The Lord's Supper | Memorial view — bread and cup symbolize, rather than become, Christ's body and blood. |
| Church government | Congregational at the local level; the Convention is a voluntary cooperative association of autonomous churches, not a hierarchy. |
| Eschatology | No single view is confessionally mandated, though premillennial and dispensationalist views have historically been common among Southern Baptists. |
| Spiritual gifts | No uniform position; individual churches and pastors range from cessationist to continuationist. |
| Women's ordination | The BF&M restricts the office of pastor to men. As of this writing, a constitutional amendment to make this exclusion explicitly enforceable passed its first required vote at the June 2026 annual meeting (6,028–2,026); a second successful vote at the 2027 annual meeting is required before it formally amends the SBC constitution. Independent of that amendment's status, the Convention has already removed individual churches from cooperation for having women serving as pastors (e.g., Saddleback Church, 2023), using existing disciplinary mechanisms. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | Opposed; the BF&M calls Christians to "contend for the sanctity of all human life from conception to natural death" (Art. XV). | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. XV) |
| Homosexuality | Considered sinful; same-sex marriage not recognized or performed. | Official confessional statement |
| Alcohol | Total abstinence is the Convention's stated position — going beyond merely condemning drunkenness/overuse (which virtually every Christian tradition, including this one, treats as sinful) to counsel abstaining from use itself, a distinctly stronger position than most other evangelical bodies and the product of a temperance heritage running back to an 1886 resolution. The position rests on Convention resolutions, not on the BF&M, which does not mention alcohol; the 2006 resolution reaffirmed "total abstinence" and urged that no user of alcoholic beverages be elected to any Convention entity or committee. | Official Convention resolutions (most recently 2006); not in the *Baptist Faith and Message* |
| Divorce and remarriage | The BF&M defines marriage as a covenant "for a lifetime" (Art. XVIII) and does not address divorce; Southern Baptist teaching and practice generally permit divorce and remarriage on biblical grounds (chiefly sexual immorality or abandonment), and a divorced person's eligibility for pastoral leadership has been a matter of significant internal debate. | *Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. XVIII, on permanence; grounds for divorce are a general pattern, not a confessional statement |
| Contraception | No confessional position; generally treated as a matter of individual conscience within marriage, not a doctrinal question. | Not addressed in the BF&M |
| Gambling | Opposed; the Convention has passed numerous resolutions condemning gambling, most recently a 2025 resolution on the "harmful and predatory nature of sports betting." | Official Convention resolutions (most recently 2025) |
| Dancing | No confessional statement; historically discouraged in much of Southern Baptist culture, particularly where associated with alcohol or venues like nightclubs, but not a matter of current formal doctrine, and practice varies widely by congregation today. | Cultural/historical, not current confessional doctrine |
| Premarital sex/cohabitation | Opposed; the BF&M opposes "all forms of sexual immorality" (Art. XV) and defines marriage as the sole framework for sexual expression (Art. XVIII). | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. XV, XVIII) |
| War/pacifism | Not pacifist: the BF&M's article on Peace and War (Art. XVI) makes it "the duty of Christians to seek peace with all men" and "to do all in their power to put an end to war," while individual Southern Baptists and chaplains serve in the military, consistent with a general (though not formally stated) just-war orientation. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. XVI); just-war orientation is a general pattern |
| Death penalty | No single confessional position; Southern Baptist resolutions have historically affirmed the government's right to use capital punishment while urging its just and consistent application, distinct from the Catholic Church's current categorical opposition. | Official Convention resolutions |

### Other divisive positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture and Tradition | Scripture alone is the supreme standard; confessions, including the *Baptist Faith and Message* itself, are described as statements of belief with "no authority over the conscience" and are revisable. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Preamble and Art. I) |
| The Trinity and the person of Christ | Trinitarian; Christ fully God and fully man, virgin-born, bodily risen. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. II) |
| Predestination and free will | "Election is the gracious purpose of God" and "is consistent with the free agency of man" — language deliberately broad enough for both Calvinists and non-Calvinists. The 2012 "Traditional Statement" and the 2013 Calvinism Advisory Committee report document a live internal debate that the Convention has chosen not to settle. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. V); debate documented in 2013 Calvinism Advisory Committee report |
| Sanctification and holiness | Progressive: sanctification begins at regeneration and continues throughout life; the Wesleyan doctrine of entire sanctification is not held. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. IV.B) |
| Hell, judgment and the fate of the lost | "The unrighteous will be consigned to Hell, the place of everlasting punishment." | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. X) |
| Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead | Mary is not venerated or invoked and the dead are not prayed for; the confession affirms Christ as sole mediator and the priesthood of all believers rather than addressing these practices by name. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. II.B, VI), by implication |
| Worship: day, style and liturgy | Sunday is "the Lord's Day," to be observed with worship and spiritual devotion; worship is non-liturgical and ranges from traditional hymn-and-choir services to contemporary formats, set by each autonomous congregation. | Official confessional statement on the day (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. VIII); style is a general pattern |
| Church and state / political engagement | Church and state "should be separate"; the state owes every church protection and full freedom. The Convention engages public policy through the Ethics & Religious Liberty Commission and annual resolutions, and does not endorse candidates. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. XVII) and annual resolutions |
| Race, ethnicity and the church | Founded in 1845 in defense of slaveholding missionaries; the 1995 Resolution on Racial Reconciliation apologized for slavery and segregation, a 2017 resolution condemned alt-right white supremacy, and 2019–2021 resolutions on critical race theory revealed continuing internal disagreement. | Official resolutions (1995, 2017, 2019, 2021) |
| Creation and the age of the earth | Man is "the special creation of God, made in His own image"; no age of the earth is specified, but a 1982 resolution supported teaching scientific creationism alongside evolution, and a young-earth reading predominates among Southern Baptist leaders. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. III); 1982 resolution; predominance is a general pattern |
| Tithing and the prosperity gospel | Members are to give "cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally"; a tithe is the customary benchmark though not named in the confession. The prosperity gospel is rejected in Southern Baptist seminaries and leadership, without a dedicated resolution. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. XIII); prosperity-gospel rejection is a general pattern |
| Bible translations and KJV-onlyism | No official translation; the Convention's publisher (Lifeway) produced the Holman Christian Standard Bible (2004) and the Christian Standard Bible (2017), and the CSB, NIV, ESV and KJV are all in wide use. KJV-onlyism is not an SBC position. | Official publishing practice (Lifeway); not a confessional matter |
| Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian | Complementarian: the husband is to provide "servant leadership" and the wife is to "submit herself graciously"; the pastoral office is limited to men. Added to the confession in 1998 and 2000. | Official confessional statement (*Baptist Faith and Message* 2000, Art. XVIII, VI) |

## 4. United Methodist Church (UMC)

**U.S. adherents:** ~8,018,629 (2020 U.S. Religion Census).
**Governance:** Connectional, episcopal; the General Conference is the church's top legislative body.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | "Scripture is the primary source and criterion for Christian doctrine" (*Book of Discipline*, ¶105 "Our Theological Task"), read with tradition, experience and reason — the faith "revealed in Scripture, illumined by tradition, vivified in personal experience, and confirmed by reason," the pattern popularly called the "Wesleyan Quadrilateral," a label ¶105 itself does not use; Article V affirms that "the Holy Scriptures contain all things necessary to salvation," and the word "inerrant" appears in neither text. |
| Salvation | Wesleyan-Arminian: prevenient grace, "the divine love that surrounds all humanity and precedes any and all of our conscious impulses," then "justifying grace with accepting and pardoning love" and "assurance of our present salvation" (*Book of Discipline*, "Distinctive Wesleyan Emphases"); Article VIII holds that fallen humanity has "no power to do good works … without the grace of God by Christ preventing us." Grace is available to all and can be resisted, in contrast to Calvinist traditions elsewhere in this section. |
| Baptism | Both infant and believer's baptism: "the baptism of infants and adults, both male and female, is the sign of this covenant" (*By Water and the Spirit*, approved as the church's official interpretive statement on baptism by the 1996 General Conference). **Mode:** "the water of baptism may be administered by sprinkling, pouring, or immersion"; sprinkling is customary. |
| The Lord's Supper | Article XVIII: "a sacrament of our redemption by Christ's death" in which, "to such as rightly, worthily, and with faith receive the same," the bread "is a partaking of the body of Christ"; transubstantiation "is repugnant to the plain words of Scripture," and the body of Christ is "eaten in the Supper only after a heavenly and spiritual manner," the means being faith. In practice an "open table": the liturgy invites "all who love him, who earnestly repent of their sin, and seek to live in peace with one another," and *This Holy Mystery* (the church's official statement on Communion) asks that the unbaptized who receive be "counseled and nurtured toward baptism as soon as possible." |
| Church government | Connectional, episcopal; the General Conference "shall have full legislative power over all matters distinctively connectional" (*Book of Discipline*, Constitution ¶17), including the power "to define and fix the powers, duties, and privileges of the episcopacy," which a Restrictive Rule (¶20) forbids it to abolish. |
| Eschatology | No specific end-times framework doctrinally mandated, consistent with the wider Wesleyan tradition. |
| Spiritual gifts | No official cessationist or continuationist position; not a defining doctrinal emphasis. |
| Women's ordination | Fully open; the Methodist Church's General Conference granted women full clergy rights on May 4, 1956 (the United Brethren had done so in 1889). |

## 5. Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS/Mormon)

**U.S. adherents:** ~6,721,031 (2020 U.S. Religion Census).
**Governance:** A distinct restorationist tradition, not part of the wider Nicene Christian family in the way every other entry in this section is; hierarchical, led by a President ("prophet"), a Quorum of Twelve Apostles, and a lay priesthood open to worthy male members.
**Scripture:** The Bible, alongside the Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great Price, which the LDS Church holds as additional, equally authoritative scripture — a doctrinal distinctive that separates this body from every other tradition profiled in this section.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | "We believe the Bible to be the word of God as far as it is translated correctly; we also believe the Book of Mormon to be the word of God" (Articles of Faith 8); the Church "accepts four books as scripture" — Bible, Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, Pearl of Great Price — as "the standard works," and "the inspired words of our living prophets become scripture" (*Gospel Principles*, ch. 10) — a distinctive setting this body apart from every other tradition in this section. |
| Salvation | "Through the Atonement of Christ, all mankind may be saved, by obedience to the laws and ordinances of the Gospel" (Articles of Faith 3), with multiple degrees of eternal glory — celestial, terrestrial and telestial (Doctrine and Covenants 76) — theologically distinct from the Protestant/Catholic/Orthodox frameworks elsewhere in this section. |
| Baptism | "Baptism by immersion for the remission of sins" (Articles of Faith 4), performed from the age of accountability — "their children shall be baptized for the remission of their sins when eight years old" (Doctrine and Covenants 68:27) — not in infancy; also performed vicariously on behalf of the dead in temples, since "we without them cannot be made perfect; neither can they without us" (Doctrine and Covenants 128:18). |
| The Lord's Supper | A weekly sacrament taken "in remembrance of the body" and "in remembrance of the blood of thy Son" (the prayers in Doctrine and Covenants 20:77, 79); water is used rather than wine, on the principle that "it mattereth not what ye shall eat or what ye shall drink when ye partake of the sacrament" (Doctrine and Covenants 27:2); no change in the elements is taught. |
| Church government | Hierarchical; led by a President ("prophet"), a Quorum of Twelve Apostles, and a lay priesthood. |
| Eschatology | A distinctive eschatology: "the literal gathering of Israel," Zion "built upon the American continent," Christ reigning "personally upon the earth," and the earth receiving "its paradisiacal glory" (Articles of Faith 10), plus multiple degrees of post-resurrection glory (Doctrine and Covenants 76); not aligned with the dispensational/covenantal frameworks used elsewhere in this section. |
| Spiritual gifts | Affirms "the gift of tongues, prophecy, revelation, visions, healing, interpretation of tongues, and so forth" (Articles of Faith 7) and ongoing revelation — "all that He does now reveal" and "will yet reveal" (Articles of Faith 9) — through a living prophet, distinct from the continuationist/cessationist framework used to describe other traditions in this section. |
| Women's ordination | Priesthood ordination reserved to worthy male members (extended to men of all races only since 1978); women do not hold priesthood office, though serve extensively in other leadership auxiliaries. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | "The Church opposes elective abortion for personal or social convenience" and allows "possible exceptions" when pregnancy results from rape or incest, when a competent physician determines the mother's life or health is in serious jeopardy, or when the fetus has severe defects that will not allow survival beyond birth — adding that "even these exceptions do not automatically justify abortion"; a position with named exceptions, distinct from the Catholic Church's categorical opposition. | Official Church statement (Newsroom, "Abortion"; *General Handbook* 38.6.1) |
| Homosexuality | "Marriage between a man and a woman is ordained of God" (*The Family: A Proclamation to the World*, 1995), so the Church does not perform or recognize same-sex marriages; its official statement distinguishes attraction from behavior — "the attraction itself is not a sin, but acting on it is" — and members striving to live the law of chastity "may receive Church callings, hold temple recommends, and receive temple ordinances." | Official Church statement (Newsroom, "Same-Sex Attraction"); *Family Proclamation* (1995) |

| Contraception | Not prohibited: "the decision of how many children to have and when to have them is a private matter for a husband and wife," and "decisions about birth control and the consequences of those decisions rest solely with each married couple" — a notable contrast to the Church's much stricter, formally codified position on alcohol and tobacco. | Official Church guidance (Gospel Topics, "Birth Control") |

| Death penalty | Neutral by official statement: the Church "regards the question of whether and in what circumstances the state should impose capital punishment" as a matter for civil law, and "we neither promote nor oppose capital punishment." | Official Church statement (Newsroom, "Capital Punishment") |

| Category | Position | Source |

| Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead | Mary is honored as the mother of Jesus but not venerated or invoked; the dead are not prayed to or for, but proxy baptism and other temple ordinances are performed on their behalf, since "we without them cannot be made perfect; neither can they without us be made perfect" (Doctrine and Covenants 128:18). | Official Church teaching (Doctrine and Covenants 127–128); the statements about Mary are a general pattern not cited to a document here |
| Worship: day, style and liturgy | Sunday sacrament meeting, lay-led without vestments or fixed liturgy beyond the sacrament prayers; a two-hour block since 2019; temple worship is separate and restricted to members in good standing. | Official Church practice (*General Handbook*) |
| Church and state / political engagement | Formal partisan neutrality, restated each election cycle, with members urged to vote; the Church does take public positions on ballot questions it deems moral (e.g., California Proposition 8, 2008; Utah anti-discrimination compromise, 2015). | Official Church statements (Articles of Faith 12; Doctrine and Covenants 134) |
| Race, ethnicity and the church | A ban on Black men holding the priesthood and on Black members entering temples was lifted by revelation in 1978; a 2013 essay disavowed the theories once used to justify it, and the Church has partnered with the NAACP since 2018. | Official Church teaching (Official Declaration 2, 1978; *Gospel Topics* essay "Race and the Priesthood," 2013) |
| Creation and the age of the earth | No official position on the age of the earth, which the First Presidency's 1909 statement *The Origin of Man* does not address; that statement proclaims man "the direct and lineal offspring of Deity," "formed in the divine image," and rejects descent from lower animals as "the theories of men" without describing a mechanism of creation; Church universities teach evolution. | Official Church statement (First Presidency, "The Origin of Man," *Improvement Era*, Nov. 1909; reprinted *Ensign*, Feb. 2002) |
| Tithing and the prosperity gospel | A full tithe (10% of income) is required and reviewed annually in "tithing settlement"; it is a condition of a temple recommend. Temporal blessings are promised to tithe-payers, but the prosperity gospel as such is not taught. | Official Church teaching (Doctrine and Covenants 119; *General Handbook*) |
| Bible translations and KJV-onlyism | The King James Version is "the English language Bible used by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints" (First Presidency letter, 1992), published in the Church's own edition (1979, rev. 2013) with footnotes from the Joseph Smith Translation; members are encouraged "to have their own copies of the complete standard works." The KJV is preferred for its doctrinal fit, not held to be inerrant. | Official Church statement (First Presidency letter, reported in *Church News*, 20 June 1992) |
| Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian | Fathers "preside over their families in love and righteousness" and provide; mothers are "primarily responsible for the nurture of their children"; spouses are "equal partners." Priesthood office is held by men only. | Official Church proclamation (*The Family: A Proclamation to the World*, 1995) |

## 6. Evangelical Lutheran Church in America (ELCA)

**U.S. adherents:** ~3,139,413 (2020 U.S. Religion Census); the largest Lutheran body in the U.S.
**Governance:** Episcopal/synodical; the Churchwide Assembly is the highest legislative body.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | The church "accepts the canonical Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments as the inspired Word of God and the authoritative source and norm of its proclamation, faith, and life," read with the three ecumenical creeds and "the Unaltered Augsburg Confession as a true witness to the Gospel" (ELCA Constitution, Confession of Faith, 2.03–2.05); inerrancy in the stricter evangelical sense is not affirmed. |
| Salvation | Historic Lutheran doctrine: the church is to "proclaim God's saving Gospel of justification by grace for Christ's sake through faith alone" (ELCA Constitution 4.02). |
| Baptism | Infant baptism is the norm: the Augsburg Confession (IX) teaches that "Baptism is necessary for salvation," that "God's grace is offered through Baptism," and that "children are to be baptized," condemning those "who reject the Baptism of children." **Mode:** pouring is customary, though not exclusively required. |
| The Lord's Supper | Sacramental union — "the body and blood of Christ are truly present and distributed to those who eat the Lord's Supper" (Augsburg Confession X): a real, though not transubstantiated, presence of Christ in, with, and under the bread and wine. |
| Church government | Episcopal/synodical; the Churchwide Assembly is the highest legislative body. |
| Eschatology | No specific end-times framework doctrinally mandated. |
| Spiritual gifts | No official cessationist or continuationist position; not a defining doctrinal emphasis. |
| Women's ordination | Fully open; both predecessor bodies (the Lutheran Church in America and the American Lutheran Church) voted to ordain women in 1970, and Elizabeth Platz, ordained 22 November 1970, was the first woman ordained by a Lutheran church body in North America. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | The 1991 social statement *Abortion* holds that "abortion ought to be an option only of last resort" while recognizing "that there can be sound reasons for ending a pregnancy through induced abortion"; it opposes "ending intrauterine life when a fetus is developed enough to live outside a uterus," and opposes "laws that deny access to safe and affordable services for morally justifiable abortions" — a discernment position, more permissive than the evangelical bodies in this section but qualified rather than unconditionally supportive. | *Abortion*, ELCA social statement (1991), pp. 3–10 |
| Homosexuality | The 2009 Churchwide Assembly adopted the social statement *Human Sexuality: Gift and Trust*, which recognized four "bound conscience" positions on same-gender relationships, permitting (but not requiring) congregations to recognize them, and the same Assembly opened rostered ministry to people in publicly accountable, lifelong, monogamous same-gender relationships; the 2025 Churchwide Assembly amended the statement's Section IV by a two-thirds vote (724–46) as part of an ongoing reconsideration, so the text is in motion. | Official 2009 social statement, amended 2025 |
| Alcohol | No prohibition; moderate use accepted, consistent with historic Lutheran practice; drunkenness alone is condemned. | General practice; not independently documented |
| Divorce and remarriage | Permitted with pastoral care; the 2009 social statement *Human Sexuality: Gift and Trust* says "this church does not treat divorce lightly nor does it disregard the responsibilities of marriage," and offers pastoral support to those who wish to remarry. | *Human Sexuality: Gift and Trust* (2009), p. 10 |
| Contraception | No prohibition; the 2009 social statement treats "information about birth control, including the encouragement and support of sexual abstinence" as part of responsible sexual conduct and expects partners to protect each other from "the possibility of an unwanted pregnancy." | *Human Sexuality: Gift and Trust* (2009), pp. 14, 17 |
| Gambling | No social statement or social message on gambling appears among the ELCA's adopted statements; concern about gambling's social harms has been voiced in the church's economic-life teaching and by synods without a churchwide rule, and individual abstinence is not mandated. | Not addressed by a separate churchwide statement (the ELCA's list of social statements and messages contains none on gambling) |
| Dancing | No doctrinal position; not treated as a moral category. | Not addressed |
| Premarital sex/cohabitation | The 2009 social statement states that this church "does not favor cohabitation arrangements outside of marriage," urging couples toward the fuller social and legal support of marriage, and expects pastors to explain that position to cohabiting couples — a stated disfavor with pastoral accompaniment rather than a disciplinary rule. | *Human Sexuality: Gift and Trust* (2009), p. 17 |
| War/pacifism | The 1995 social statement *For Peace in God's World* "affirms just war teaching in the service of just peace" while recognizing the "significant witness of conscientious objectors" who on grounds of faith cannot support war in any circumstances — holding the two in tension rather than mandating one. | *For Peace in God's World*, ELCA social statement (1995) |
| Death penalty | Opposed since the 1991 social statement *The Death Penalty*: "The ELCA objects to the use of the death penalty because it is not used fairly and has failed to make society safer." | Official 1991 social statement |

### Other divisive positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture and Tradition | The canonical Scriptures are "the inspired Word of God and the authoritative source and norm of its proclamation, faith, and life"; the Apostles', Nicene and Athanasian Creeds are accepted "as true declarations of the faith," the Unaltered Augsburg Confession "as a true witness to the Gospel," and the other writings of the Book of Concord "as further valid interpretations of the faith of the Church" — accepted as witnesses, not as a second source of revelation. | Official (ELCA Constitution 2.03–2.06, mirrored in the Model Constitution for Congregations C2.03–C2.06) |
| The Trinity and the person of Christ | Trinitarian: the church "confesses the Triune God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit" (2.01), "Jesus Christ as Lord and Savior" and "the Word of God incarnate" (2.02), and accepts the Apostles', Nicene and Athanasian Creeds (2.04); Chalcedonian Christology. | Official (ELCA Constitution 2.01–2.04) |

| Worship: day, style and liturgy | Sunday — "the primary day on which Christians gather to worship" (Principle 6) — Word-and-Sacrament worship, the proclamation of the Word and the sacramental meal being "so intimately connected as to form one act of worship" (Principle 34), following *Evangelical Lutheran Worship* (2006) and the Revised Common Lectionary; weekly Communion is the stated norm: "Lutheran congregations celebrate the Holy Communion every Sunday and festival," a confession that "remains the norm for our practice" (Principle 35). Style ranges from traditional liturgical to contemporary settings of the same order. | Official (*The Use of the Means of Grace*, adopted by the 1997 Churchwide Assembly; *Evangelical Lutheran Worship*); style is a general pattern |

**Governance:** Highly centralized; led by the Governing Body, headquartered in Warwick, New York, publishing through the Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society.

## 10. Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod (LCMS)

**U.S. adherents:** ~1,802,680 (2020 U.S. Religion Census); the second-largest Lutheran body in the U.S. and, doctrinally, the more theologically conservative counterpart to the ELCA.
**Governance:** Congregational with synodical cooperation.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Biblical inerrancy affirmed, holding to the historic Book of Concord without the doctrinal revisions the ELCA has since made. |
| Salvation | Historic Lutheran doctrine of justification by grace through faith alone. |
| Baptism | Infant baptism is the norm: Augsburg Confession IX teaches that "Baptism is necessary for salvation," that "God's grace is offered through Baptism," that "children are to be baptized," and condemns "the Anabaptists, who reject the Baptism of children." |
| The Lord's Supper | Augsburg Confession X: "the body and blood of Christ are truly present and distributed to those who eat the Lord's Supper," and those who teach otherwise are rejected — the Lutheran "sacramental union," a real presence confessed without the doctrine of transubstantiation. |
| Church government | Congregational with synodical cooperation. |
| Eschatology | Amillennial by confession: the *Brief Statement* (1932, §42) rejects "every type of millennialism, or chiliasm," affirms a single resurrection and leaves the time of the Last Day unknown, following Augsburg Confession XVII; the CTCR's *The End Times: A Study on Eschatology and Millennialism* (1989) develops the position. |
| Spiritual gifts | Not a defining doctrinal emphasis and no cessationist decree; the Synod has studied the question in CTCR reports (*The Charismatic Movement and Lutheran Theology*, 1972; *The Lutheran Church and the Charismatic Movement*, 1977; *Spiritual Gifts*, 1994). |
| Women's ordination | Women are not ordained to the pastoral office, a position the LCMS has maintained even as the ELCA moved in the opposite direction — the clearest and most direct doctrinal contrast between two bodies sharing the same broad Lutheran heritage found anywhere in this section, alongside the UMC/GMC and PC(USA)/PCA pairs. |

| Category | Position | Source |

| Alcohol | No prohibition; moderate use accepted, consistent with confessional Lutheran tradition generally (Martin Luther himself was famously no proponent of abstinence); drunkenness alone is condemned. | General practice |
| Divorce and remarriage | Permitted on biblical grounds, consistent with confessional Lutheran teaching, while affirming marriage's intended permanence. | Confessional Lutheran teaching; not independently documented |
| Contraception | No prohibition; treated as a matter of individual conscience within marriage, consistent with confessional Lutheran teaching generally. | General practice; not independently documented |
| Gambling | Addressed in a CTCR report (*Gambling*, 1996) that sets out four principles, among them that "gambling encourages the sins of greed and covetousness," without declaring every form of gambling sinful; conscience remains the operative standard within those principles. | Official (CTCR report *Gambling*, 1996, as summarized in *LCMS Views*) |

## 11. Episcopal Church

**U.S. adherents:** ~1,576,611 (2020 U.S. Religion Census).
**Governance:** Episcopal, the U.S. member church of the worldwide Anglican Communion; governed by a General Convention (bishops and elected lay/clergy deputies) between which the Presiding Bishop and Executive Council carry on the church's work.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | The Prayer Book Catechism calls the Scriptures "the Word of God because God inspired their human authors and because God still speaks to us through the Bible," understood "by the help of the Holy Spirit, who guides the Church in the true interpretation of the Scriptures"; the Apocrypha is "often included." Scripture, tradition and reason together (the "three-legged stool" often associated with Anglican theological method, though not a Prayer Book formula) mark a different framework from the "Scripture alone" emphasis found in most evangelical bodies profiled in this section. |
| Salvation | The Catechism defines redemption as "the act of God which sets us free from the power of evil, sin, and death," through "the Messiah, or Christ, … Jesus of Nazareth, the only Son of God"; beyond that, wide latitude across the theological breadth of the Anglican Communion (from Anglo-Catholic to evangelical wings). |
| Baptism | Both infant and adult baptism practiced; infants and younger children are presented by parents and godparents, who answer for them. **Mode:** the *Book of Common Prayer* rubric has the celebrant "immerse, or pour water upon, the candidate" — pouring is the usual practice, and sprinkling is not named in the rite. |
| The Lord's Supper | The Eucharist, understood sacramentally: the Catechism calls its inward and spiritual grace "the Body and Blood of Christ given to his people, and received by faith," and the church "affirms that Christ is really present in the Eucharist" while allowing "differing views on how that occurs" — without the doctrinal precision of transubstantiation; central to Sunday worship. |
| Church government | Episcopal: the Catechism names the ministers of the Church as "lay persons, bishops, priests, and deacons," the bishop being "apostle, chief priest, and pastor of a diocese"; the U.S. member church of the worldwide Anglican Communion. |
| Eschatology | No specific end-times framework doctrinally mandated; the Catechism's "Christian Hope" says only that Christ "will come in glory and judge the living and the dead" and "make all things new," defining heaven as "eternal life in our enjoyment of God" and hell as "eternal death in our rejection of God." |
| Spiritual gifts | No official cessationist or continuationist position; not a defining doctrinal emphasis. |
| Women's ordination | Fully open; the 1976 General Convention opened the priesthood and episcopate to women (the first regular ordinations followed in January 1977, after the irregular "Philadelphia Eleven" ordinations of 1974) — the last of the seven large mainline Protestant bodies to do so — and Barbara Harris was consecrated the first woman bishop in 1989. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | General Convention has supported legal access while calling life sacred: the 1967 statement (reaffirmed by 1976-D095) declared "unequivocal opposition to any legislation on the part of the national or state governments which would abridge or deny the right of individuals to reach informed decisions" about ending a pregnancy; the 1988 Statement on Childbirth and Abortion (C047, reaffirmed 1994-A054) holds that "all human life is sacred ... from its inception until death"; 1982-A065 condemned abortion for gender selection; 2018-D032 asked that reproductive health procedures be "treated as all other medical procedures"; 2022-D083 addressed the erosion of reproductive rights after *Dobbs*. A position closer to the UMC's or ELCA's qualified statements than to a categorical prohibition. | General Convention resolutions 1967–2022, as indexed by the Church's Office of Government Relations |
| Homosexuality | The Episcopal Church consecrated an openly gay bishop in a same-sex relationship in 2003 (Gene Robinson, Bishop of New Hampshire) — among the earliest and most publicly significant such actions of any major U.S. denomination — and has since authorized a trial-use rite for blessing same-sex unions (2012-A049), revised Canon I.18 from "a man and a woman" to "both parties" and approved trial marriage liturgies for all couples (2015-A036 and A054, effective Advent 2015), and in 2018 (B012, adopted 13 July 2018) gave congregations access to those rites in every diocese, with delegated episcopal oversight where the bishop objects. | Divorce and remarriage | Permitted; after refusing to remarry divorced persons with a living former spouse (policy from 1804, codified 1868), the church revised its canons in 1973 to allow remarriage after divorce, with the cleric required to consult the bishop and obtain the bishop's consent and to be satisfied that a civil court has dissolved the prior marriage. | Canon I.19 (remarriage after divorce), revised 1973; Episcopal News Service summary, 2000 |
| Contraception | Not prohibited; the Anglican Communion's 1930 Lambeth Conference (Resolution 15, carried 193–67) was among the first major Christian bodies to formally permit contraception within marriage "where there is a morally sound reason for avoiding complete abstinence," and the Episcopal Church has continued to hold that position (General Convention 1982-D016 reaffirmed the right to use artificial conception control). | Lambeth Conference 1930, Resolution 15; General Convention 1982-D016 |
| Gambling | No single mandated position; General Convention statements have at times raised concerns about the societal effects of gambling without a categorical prohibition. | General Convention statements (none identified); not independently documented |
| Dancing | No doctrinal position; not treated as a moral category. | Not addressed |
| Premarital sex/cohabitation | No categorical prohibition; Episcopal teaching generally emphasizes pastoral discernment over a fixed rule, consistent with its broader approach to personal ethical questions. | General teaching, wide latitude across the Anglican Communion's theological breadth; not independently documented |
| War/pacifism | Just-war tradition, consistent with historic Anglican teaching, while also recognizing pacifism as a legitimate Christian vocation for individuals. | General Convention statements and historic Anglican teaching (none identified); not independently documented |
| Death penalty | The General Convention went "on record as opposed to capital punishment" in 1958 (Journal, pp. 206–208) and has reaffirmed that opposition in later Conventions — one of the earliest such positions among the mainline bodies in this section. | General Convention 1958 resolution (Journal pp. 206–208) and later reaffirmations |

### Other divisive positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|

| Creation and the age of the earth | Evolution is affirmed as compatible with faith: General Convention Resolution 2006-A129, *Affirm Evolution and Science Education*, resolved that "the theory of evolution provides a fruitful and unifying scientific explanation for the emergence of life on earth" and that "an acceptance of evolution is entirely compatible with an authentic and living Christian faith," and asked legislatures and education boards to set science standards "based on the best available scientific knowledge" — without naming creationism or intelligent design. | Official (General Convention Resolution 2006-A129, concurred as amended, 21 June 2006) |
| Tithing and the prosperity gospel | The 1982 General Convention affirmed the tithe "as the standard of giving for Episcopalians" (Resolution A-116), and later Conventions have reaffirmed it as "the minimum standard of giving" (2009-D055); the prosperity gospel is not taught. | Official (General Convention 1982-A116; 2009-D055 and other reaffirmations) |
| Bible translations and KJV-onlyism | Translations authorized for public worship are listed by canon (Canon II.2: the KJV with the 1901 marginal readings, the English and American Revised Versions and the RSV, the Jerusalem and New Jerusalem Bibles, the NEB and REB, the Good News Bible, the New American Bible, the NIV, the NRSV, the CEV and the Common English Bible, with other versions by a bishop's authorization); the NRSV predominates. ## 13. Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.) [PC(USA)]

**U.S. adherents:** ~1,491,775 (2020 U.S. Religion Census); PC(USA)'s own more recent reporting places active membership closer to 1.0–1.3 million as of 2024–2025, reflecting continued membership decline common across mainline denominations.
**Governance:** Presbyterian (representative governance through elected elders, organized in ascending regional and national bodies — session, presbytery, synod, General Assembly).
**Origin:** Formed in 1983 by the reunion of the northern and southern American Presbyterian churches, which had been divided since the Civil War era; traces its theological roots to 16th-century Calvinism.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Scripture is authoritative and "the Word of God," interpreted through the lens of Reformed confessional standards (the Book of Confessions) rather than affirmed as inerrant in the term's stricter evangelical sense. |
| Salvation | Reformed/Calvinist theological heritage (God's sovereign grace in salvation), though practice and preaching vary by congregation. |
| Baptism | Both infant and believer's baptism are practiced and considered valid, and "baptism is received only once"; infant baptism is the historic Reformed norm. **Mode:** the Directory for Worship says the water "shall be applied to the person by pouring, sprinkling, or immersion," all equally valid; in practice, pouring or sprinkling is by far the most common mode, consistent with the tradition's own historic confession (the Westminster Confession states "dipping of the person into the water is not necessary"). |
| The Lord's Supper | Spiritual/real presence in the Reformed (Calvinist) sense — Christ is spiritually present to faith in the meal, distinct from both a bare memorial and from transubstantiation. |
| Church government | Presbyterian — representative, through elected elders and a connectional structure of governing bodies. |
| Eschatology | No confessionally mandated position; historically amillennial in Reformed tradition, but not treated as a defining or tested doctrine today. |
| Spiritual gifts | No official cessationist or continuationist position; not a defining doctrinal emphasis. |
| Women's ordination | Fully open; women have been eligible for ordination as ministers, elders, and deacons since 1956 (a predecessor body) and are ordained without restriction today. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|

## 16. Seventh-day Adventist Church

**U.S. adherents:** ~1,339,830 (2020 U.S. Religion Census).
**Governance:** Representative, organized in an ascending structure of local church, conference, union, and General Conference.
**Distinctive practice:** Observes the seventh-day Sabbath (Saturday, sundown Friday to sundown Saturday) as the biblical day of rest and worship, rather than Sunday — the clearest and most consistently distinguishing practice of this body relative to every other entry in this section.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | The Bible is "the infallible revelation of His will" and "the authoritative revealer of doctrines" (Fundamental Belief 1 — the church's own term is infallibility, and it does not use the word inerrancy), alongside a distinctive ongoing role attributed to the writings of Ellen G. White, one of the denomination's founders, as "a continuing and authoritative source of truth" that remains subject to the Bible as the standard (Fundamental Belief 18). |
| Salvation | Evangelical/Arminian-leaning soteriology, with a strong historical emphasis on the imminent, literal Second Coming of Christ. |
| Baptism | Full immersion, for believers only (not practiced in infancy). |
| The Lord's Supper | Observed quarterly, including a footwashing ceremony beforehand — a distinctive practice not shared by most other bodies in this section. |
| Church government | "A representative form of church government": authority flows from the members of the local church, "the structural foundation," upward through the local conference, the union conference and (within the General Conference) thirteen world divisions to the General Conference, which has "the final say" (Columbia Union Conference, "How Church Structure Works"). |
| Eschatology | A strong historical emphasis on the imminent, literal Second Coming of Christ, and a distinctive doctrine of the investigative judgment, a teaching unique to this body among those profiled in this section. |
| Spiritual gifts | Affirms the ongoing gift of prophecy specifically (as historically exercised by Ellen G. White), without organizing broadly around the continuationist/cessationist framework used to describe Pentecostal traditions in this section. |
| Women's ordination | Women may be ordained as local elders in many conferences, but ordination to the gospel ministry remains unresolved: at the 2015 General Conference Session (San Antonio, 8 July 2015) delegates voted 1,381 to 977 against allowing division executive committees "to make provision for the ordination of women to the gospel ministry" in their territories. |

## 20. Church of God in Christ (COGIC)

## 21. Church of the Nazarene

**U.S. adherents:** ~905,690 (2020 U.S. Religion Census; formed 1908 from a merger of Holiness groups).
**Governance:** Representative, connectional; part of the Wesleyan-Holiness tradition (alongside the Wesleyan Church and Free Methodist Church).

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | The Scriptures are held to be given by plenary inspiration, "inerrantly revealing the will of God concerning us in all things necessary to our salvation" (Article of Faith IV) — an inerrancy defined by its soteriological scope rather than the Chicago Statement's. |
| Salvation | Wesleyan-Arminian theology, with a strong historical emphasis on "entire sanctification" — "that act of God, subsequent to regeneration, by which believers are made free from original sin" (Articles of Faith X) — a distinctive the Nazarene church shares with the broader Holiness movement but which is not held by most other bodies in this section. |
| Baptism | Both infant and believer's baptism: baptism is "to be administered to believers and declarative of their faith," and, "being a symbol of the new covenant, young children may be baptized, upon request of parents or guardians who shall give assurance for them of necessary Christian training." **Mode:** "sprinkling, pouring, or immersion, according to the choice of the applicant" (*Manual*, Articles of Faith XII). |
| The Lord's Supper | "A means of grace in which Christ is present by the Spirit"; "all are invited to participate by faith in Christ," and "those who have faith in Christ and love for the saints are invited by Christ to participate as often as possible" (*Manual*, Articles of Faith XIII). |
| Church government | Representative, connectional. |
| Eschatology | No specific end-times framework doctrinally mandated; the Articles affirm only "future judgment in which every person shall appear before God to be judged according to his or her deeds in this life" and the second coming (*Manual*, Articles of Faith XV–XVI). |
| Spiritual gifts | No official cessationist or continuationist position; not a defining doctrinal emphasis relative to this body's emphasis on entire sanctification. |
| Women's ordination | Fully open; women have been ordained since the denomination's founding in 1908, and the Nazarene church has one of the longest continuous records of women's ordination among the bodies in this section — nearly 20 percent of its early clergy were women. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |

## 22. Eastern Orthodox Church (combined U.S. jurisdictions)

**U.S. adherents:** ~675,765 combined across all Eastern Orthodox jurisdictions (2020 U.S. Religion Census, via its Census of Orthodox Christian Churches; Oriental Orthodox bodies, counted separately at ~491,000, are not included here) (Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America, Antiochian Orthodox Christian Archdiocese, Orthodox Church in America, and others) — treated as one entry here because these jurisdictions share full doctrinal and sacramental communion with one another, differing administratively (often along historic ethnic lines) rather than theologically.
**Governance:** Episcopal, organized into autonomous or semi-autonomous jurisdictions in the U.S., coordinated through the Assembly of Canonical Orthodox Bishops of the United States of America; no single U.S. Orthodox "denomination" exists in the way other entries in this section do.
**Origin:** Traces continuous institutional and liturgical continuity to the undivided early church, formally distinct from Roman Catholicism since the mutual excommunications of 1054.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Scripture is authoritative, read within Holy Tradition — "the ongoing life of God's People," which, "although containing many written documents … is not at all limited to what is written"; among its elements "the Bible holds the first place," followed by "the Church's liturgical life and its prayer," the councils, "the writings of the church fathers, the lives of the saints, the canon laws, and finally the iconographic tradition" (OCA, *The Orthodox Faith*, "Tradition"). |
| Salvation | *Theosis* (deification/union with God) is the central framework — salvation is described less in the West's forensic/legal terms and more as a lifelong process of transformation into the likeness of God, synergistic (involving both grace and human cooperation). |
| Baptism | Infant baptism, with the godparent speaking "on behalf of the child," immediately followed by chrismation — "the gift of the Holy Spirit in the sacrament of Chrismation" before the baptismal procession and first Communion — and understood as "the act of a person's death and resurrection in and with Jesus" and "new birth by water and the Holy Spirit." **Mode:** "the person is immersed three times in the water in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit" (OCA, *The Orthodox Faith*, "Baptism"). |
| The Lord's Supper | The Eucharist is "the real presence of Christ, His true Body and Blood mystically present in the bread and wine"; explanations of the how "often became too rationalistic," and "the mystery of the holy eucharist defies analysis and explanation in purely rational and logical terms" (OCA, *The Orthodox Faith*, "Holy Eucharist") — so Orthodoxy affirms the change without the Western term *transubstantiation*, while rejecting a merely symbolic view. |
| Church government | Episcopal; a communion of self-governing (autocephalous) national/regional churches in full sacramental unity, without a single earthly head (the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople holds a primacy of honor, not of universal jurisdiction). |
| Eschatology | No detailed end-times timeline is doctrinally defined; affirms Christ's return, final judgment, and bodily resurrection as creedal essentials, generally without the more specific frameworks (dispensationalism, etc.) found in some Western Protestant traditions. |
| Spiritual gifts | Not a defining doctrinal category in the way it is in Pentecostal/charismatic traditions; the miraculous is affirmed as ongoing (particularly through the lives of saints) without organizing around a cessationist/continuationist framework. |
| Women's ordination | Reserved to men for the priesthood and episcopate; women may serve as deaconesses in some jurisdictions (a role with historical precedent currently being revisited in parts of the Orthodox world), and extensively in monastic, catechetical, and lay leadership roles. |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | Opposed; the Assembly of Canonical Orthodox Bishops' joint statement with the U.S. Catholic bishops ("Seeking Life, Mercy, and Healing," January 2016) affirms that "life begins at the earliest moments of conception" and is sacred at every stage, and denounces abortion as a response to unwanted pregnancy or economic hardship. | Assembly of Canonical Orthodox Bishops of the USA, joint statement with the USCCB (2016) |
| Homosexuality | Considered sinful when acted upon; the Assembly of Bishops' 2013 statement on marriage and sexuality holds that "acting upon any sexual attraction outside of sacramental marriage, whether the attraction is heterosexual or homosexual," alienates us from God, and its 2015 response to *Obergefell* reaffirms marriage as "the lifelong sacramental union of a man and a woman." Same-sex marriage is not performed or recognized. | Assembly of Canonical Orthodox Bishops, 2013 statement on marriage and sexuality; 2015 response to *Obergefell v. Hodges* |
| Alcohol | Moderate use is not prohibited; wine is integral to the Eucharist. | Divorce and remarriage | Uniquely among the traditions in this section, Orthodoxy permits remarriage after divorce — "a second and even a third time" — by economy, "an exception to the rule," while treating a first marriage as the ideal; the rite for a second or third marriage differs from the first and "bears a penitential character." | Longstanding canonical practice (economia), as stated by the Orthodox Church in America |

| Gambling | No formal doctrinal position; addressed pastorally, if at all, under general principles against excess. | Not a matter of official teaching |
| Dancing | No doctrinal position; not treated as a moral category. | Not addressed |

| War/pacifism | No single mandated position; historically closer to a just-war framework, though war is treated as a tragic necessity rather than a moral good, and some canonical traditions historically imposed penance on soldiers who killed even in a just cause. | Historical canonical tradition, not a single formal doctrine |

### Other divisive positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture and Tradition | Scripture lives within Holy Tradition, "the total life and experience of the entire Church transferred from place to place and from generation to generation," whose elements — Bible, liturgy, councils, Fathers, saints, canons, icons — "are organically linked together in real life"; *sola scriptura* is rejected, and the Bible is itself the first element of Tradition. | Official (OCA, *The Orthodox Faith*, "Tradition"; teaching of the Ecumenical Councils) |
| The Trinity and the person of Christ | The Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed without the Western *filioque* — "a doctrinal addition unacceptable to Orthodoxy since it is both unscriptural" and "inconsistent with the Orthodox vision of God," the Father being "the eternal origin and source of the Spirit" (OCA, *The Orthodox Faith*, "Holy Spirit"); Chalcedonian Christology — Christ "is one person (or hypostasis) having two full and complete natures: human and divine," "true God and true Man," "without mixture and without change, without separation and without division" (OCA, "Incarnation"); and the Palamite distinction between God's essence and energies. | Official (Ecumenical Councils as presented in OCA, *The Orthodox Faith*); the Palamite distinction (Synodal decisions of 1351) is standard teaching not cited to a text here |

| Sanctification and holiness | *Theosis* (deification) — growing participation in the divine life through the sacraments, prayer and ascetic struggle; justification and sanctification are not separated as in Western theology. | Official (patristic and conciliar teaching, no text identified); not independently documented |
| Hell, judgment and the fate of the lost | Hell is real and eternal, described as the experience of God's presence by those who reject him — his "consuming fire" and "unapproachable light" "only produces hatred and anguish in those who do not 'love His appearing'"; "for those who hate God, resurrection from the dead and the presence of God will be hell," though eternal hell "does not mean that God actively tortures people" (OCA, *The Orthodox Faith*, "Judgment"). | Worship: day, style and liturgy | Sunday Divine Liturgy (normally of St. John Chrysostom) in a fixed form, with a full daily and festal cycle; chanted, unaccompanied, with icons and incense. | Official (liturgical books, none identified); not independently documented |

| Race, ethnicity and the church | Racism is condemned: the Assembly of Canonical Orthodox Bishops' Executive Committee statement of 1 June 2020 stands "in unequivocal solidarity and peaceful protest with all those who condemn racism and inequality" and declares "we condemn all actions and words that promote hatred and racism"; American Orthodoxy's organization into ethnic jurisdictions is acknowledged as an uncanonical anomaly, and Archbishop Iakovos's march with Martin Luther King Jr. at Selma (1965) is a celebrated witness. | Official (Assembly of Canonical Orthodox Bishops, Executive Committee statement, 1 June 2020) |
| Creation and the age of the earth | No dogmatic position on the age of the earth; the Fathers read Genesis in both literal and spiritual senses, and both young-earth and evolutionary views are held by Orthodox theologians without censure. | Not addressed dogmatically; range is a general pattern |
| Tithing and the prosperity gospel | No required tithe; proportionate stewardship is taught; the prosperity gospel is rejected. | General pattern, not independently documented |
| Bible translations and KJV-onlyism | The Church "uses primarily translations from the Greek version of the Old Testament known as the Septuagint," "the version of the Old Testament used in the many quotations of the Old Testament found in the New Testament" (OCA, liturgical outlines); English Orthodox Bibles include the *Orthodox Study Bible* (Thomas Nelson, 2008), with the New King James Version New Testament and the St. Athanasius Academy Septuagint translation of the Old Testament. KJV-onlyism has no standing. | Official (OCA on the Septuagint as the Church's Old Testament); publishing practice |
| Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian | The husband is head of the wife as Christ is head of the Church, within mutual love and self-sacrifice (the marriage rite of "crowning"); the priesthood is restricted to men, and the restoration of ordained deaconesses has been discussed. | Official (liturgical rite of marriage; canonical tradition) |

**Note on consistency across jurisdictions:** Because Orthodox practice is mediated substantially through the relationship between an individual and their spiritual father/confessor, actual pastoral application of these positions (especially around sacramental access) can vary meaningfully by parish and jurisdiction even where doctrine itself does not.

## 24. Presbyterian Church in America (PCA)

**U.S. adherents:** 372,696 in 1,873 congregations per the 2020 U.S. Religion Census (up from 341,431 in 2010; the PCA's own 2025 report gives 405,634); the second-largest Presbyterian body in the U.S. and the largest theologically conservative Reformed/Calvinist denomination in the country.
**Governance:** Presbyterian, paralleling PC(USA)'s structure (session, presbytery, General Assembly).
**Origin:** Formed in 1973 by conservative congregations separating from the body that would go on to merge into today's PC(USA), specifically over concerns about theological liberalism.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Biblical inerrancy affirmed, holding to the historic Westminster Confession of Faith without PC(USA)'s later revisions. |
| Salvation | Confessionally Reformed/Calvinist soteriology. |
| Baptism | Both infant and believer's baptism: the Westminster Confession extends baptism to "the infants of one or both believing parents" (ch. XXVIII.4). **Mode:** immersion is not required — baptism is "rightly administered by pouring or sprinkling water upon the person" (ch. XXVIII.3) — and pouring or sprinkling is the general practice. |
| The Lord's Supper | Spiritual presence in the Reformed sense: worthy receivers "really and indeed, yet not carnally and corporally, but spiritually, receive and feed upon Christ" (Westminster Confession ch. XXIX.7), while transubstantiation is rejected as repugnant to Scripture and to "common-sense and reason" (ch. XXIX.6). |
| Church government | Presbyterian, paralleling PC(USA)'s structure (session, presbytery, General Assembly). |
| Eschatology | No single millennial framework is mandated; the Westminster Confession affirms the resurrection of the dead "with the self-same bodies" (ch. | Spiritual gifts | Generally cessationist, consistent with confessional Reformed tradition. |

## 26. Global Methodist Church (GMC)

**U.S. congregations:** ~7,000 self-reported (as of 2025); a newly formed denomination, not captured in the 2020 Religion Census, which predates its founding. It is placed near the end of this section for that reason -- it has no Census figure to rank by -- rather than beside the United Methodist Church it split from.
**Governance:** Connectional, with a modified episcopal polity — bishops serve fixed terms rather than for life, a deliberate departure from the United Methodist model.
**Origin:** Formed May 1, 2022, by clergy and congregations disaffiliating from the United Methodist Church, explicitly over disagreement regarding the ordination of non-celibate gay clergy and the recognition of same-sex marriage. Its permanent Book of Doctrines and Discipline was adopted at its first General Conference (Costa Rica, September 2024) and took effect January 1, 2025.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Scripture is "the primary rule" for faith, morals and service (¶104) and "containeth all things necessary to salvation" (Articles of Religion V); historically Wesleyan in reading Scripture alongside tradition, reason and experience, while placing greater emphasis on Scripture's primacy than its parent denomination's recent trajectory. |
| Salvation | Wesleyan-Arminian: "penitent sinners are justified or accounted righteous before God only by faith in our Lord Jesus Christ," and, "although we have experienced regeneration, it is possible to depart from grace and fall into sin; and we may even then, by the grace of God, be renewed in righteousness" (Confession of Faith, Art. IX, *Book of Doctrines and Discipline* ¶106.2) — grace available to all and forfeitable, in contrast to Calvinist traditions' view of the perseverance of the elect. |
| Baptism | Both infant and believer's baptism: "children are under the atonement of Christ and as heirs of the Kingdom of God are acceptable subjects for Christian Baptism," and are later "by profession of faith [to] confirm their Baptism" (Confession of Faith, Art. VI); "the Baptism of young children is to be retained in the Church" (Articles of Religion, Art. XVII). **Mode:** sprinkling, pouring or immersion are all practised in Methodism, sprinkling most customarily; the Confession and Articles do not prescribe a mode. |
| The Lord's Supper | A means of grace — the sacraments are "means of grace by which God works invisibly in us" — and "a representation of our redemption, a memorial of the sufferings and death of Christ," in which those who "rightly, worthily and in faith" receive "partake of the body and blood of Christ in a spiritual manner" (Confession of Faith, Art. VI); open to baptized Christians generally ("open table"), consistent with Methodist tradition. |
| Church government | Connectional; a modified episcopal system with term-limited bishops and a Connectional Council, explicitly designed to increase clergy and laity accountability relative to the UMC's structure. |
| Eschatology | No specific end-times framework doctrinally mandated: the Confession affirms the return of Christ and "the resurrection of the dead; the righteous to life eternal and the wicked to endless condemnation" (Art. XII), and the church hopes for "the final triumph of righteousness when the kingdoms of this world shall become the Kingdom of Christ" (¶302.14), without a millennial scheme. |
| Spiritual gifts | No cessationist/continuationist position specified in its foundational documents; not a defining doctrinal emphasis. |
| Women's ordination | Fully open — the GMC ordains both women and men as clergy, continuing historic Methodism's comparatively early and consistent practice of women's ordination (a position it did not revise in separating from the UMC). |

### Ethical and lifestyle positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abortion | Opposed with a narrow exception; the 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline's Social Witness (¶302.3) says "the sacredness of all life compels us to resist the practice of abortion except in the cases of tragic conflicts of life against life" and rejects abortion "as a means of birth control or gender selection." | 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline, Social Witness ¶302.2–302.3 |
| Homosexuality | Sexual expression is affirmed only "within the legal and spiritual covenant of a loving and monogamous marriage between one man and one woman" (¶302.7); the ordination of non-celibate gay clergy and the performance of same-sex marriages are the specific points of disagreement that caused the GMC to separate from the UMC in the first place. | 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline, Social Witness ¶302.7–302.8; founding rationale |
| Alcohol | The General Rules the GMC retains from Wesley (¶108) still list among the things to be avoided "Drunkenness: buying or selling spirituous liquors, or drinking them, unless in cases of extreme necessity" — a historic temperance rule the Book keeps as a General Rule rather than restating as a current disciplinary offense; in practice the GMC's teaching emphasis is on the sexuality and ordination questions, and drunkenness itself is condemned regardless of the use/abstinence question. | 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline, General Rules ¶108 |
| Divorce and remarriage | No specific restriction beyond general pastoral care; consistent with the broader Wesleyan tradition's practice of permitting remarriage. | General practice; not independently documented |
| Contraception | No prohibition; treated as a matter of individual conscience within marriage. | General practice; not independently documented |
| Gambling | Not addressed in the 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline's Doctrinal Standards or Social Witness; the GMC's founding documents focus primarily on the sexuality and ordination questions that caused its separation from the UMC. | Not addressed in the Book of Doctrines and Discipline |
| Dancing | No doctrinal position; not treated as a moral category. | Not addressed |
| Premarital sex/cohabitation | Opposed; the Social Witness affirms sexuality only within marriage between one man and one woman (¶302.7) and is "saddened by all expressions of sexual behavior, including pornography, polygamy, and promiscuity," that fall outside it (¶302.8). | 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline, Social Witness ¶302.7–302.8 |
| War/pacifism | No single mandated position; the inherited Confession of Faith Article XVI (¶106) states "we believe war and bloodshed are contrary to the gospel and spirit of Christ," and the Social Witness commends "the pursuit of peace both between nations and individuals" (¶302.11), without a formal just-war or pacifist stance. | 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline, Confession of Faith Art. XVI; Social Witness ¶302.11 |
| Death penalty | Not addressed in the 2024 Book of Doctrines and Discipline's Doctrinal Standards or Social Witness. | Not addressed in the Book of Doctrines and Discipline |

### Other divisive positions
| Category | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture and Tradition | Scripture is the primary rule (¶104), with the Apostles' Creed, Nicene Creed and Definition of Chalcedon as normative creeds (¶105), the Articles of Religion and Confession of Faith as the Wesleyan standards (¶106), and Wesley's Standard Sermons and *Notes* (¶107); the denomination intentionally reasserts Scripture's primacy over the "quadrilateral" model. | Official (*Book of Doctrines and Discipline*, ¶104–107) |
| The Trinity and the person of Christ | Trinitarian; the Apostles' Creed, Nicene Creed and Definition of Chalcedon are adopted as normative creeds (¶105) alongside the Articles of Religion (Art. I–II). | Official (*Book of Doctrines and Discipline*, ¶105–106) |
| Predestination and free will | Arminian/Wesleyan: man "cannot turn and prepare himself, by his own natural strength and works, to faith" without "the grace of God by Christ preventing us" (Articles of Religion VIII), yet "influenced and empowered by the Holy Spirit is responsible in freedom to exercise his will for good" (Confession of Faith VII); predestination to damnation is rejected. | Official (Articles of Religion VIII; Confession of Faith VII) |
| Sanctification and holiness | Entire sanctification — "a state of perfect love, righteousness and true holiness" which a regenerate believer may obtain (Confession of Faith XI) — is affirmed as a Wesleyan distinctive the new denomination intends to recover and teach. | Official (*Book of Doctrines and Discipline*; Confession of Faith XI) |
| Hell, judgment and the fate of the lost | All stand under the judgment of Jesus Christ "both now and in the last day"; the righteous to life eternal and the wicked to endless condemnation. | Official (Confession of Faith XII) |
| Mary, the saints and prayer for the dead | "The Romish doctrine concerning purgatory, pardon, worshiping, and adoration, as well of images as of relics," and invocation of saints, is "grounded upon no warrant of Scripture" (Article XIV); prayers for the dead are not practiced. | Official (Articles of Religion XIV) |
| Worship: day, style and liturgy | Sunday worship using inherited Methodist resources, in traditional and contemporary styles by congregation. | General pattern, not independently documented |
| Church and state / political engagement | Civil government "derives its just powers from the sovereign God" (Confession of Faith XVI); public witness is affirmed in the Social Witness, but the denomination has deliberately not established a Washington lobbying office, emphasizing local-church witness. | Official (Confession of Faith XVI; *Book of Doctrines and Discipline*, Social Witness) |
| Race, ethnicity and the church | "We denounce as sin racism, sexism, and other expressions that unjustly discriminate against any person" (Social Witness ¶302.1), and the church affirms "the participation and leadership of those of all races, ethnicities, nationalities, sexes, and ages" (¶103.5); the denomination is global from its founding, with large African conferences. | Official (*Book of Doctrines and Discipline*, ¶103.5, ¶302.1) |
| Creation and the age of the earth | Not addressed. | Not addressed |
| Tithing and the prosperity gospel | Tithing is encouraged in the *Book of Doctrines and Discipline*; not confirmed as a formal requirement of membership. The prosperity gospel is not taught. | *Book of Doctrines and Discipline*; status as a requirement not confirmed |
| Bible translations and KJV-onlyism | No official translation; varies by congregation. | Not addressed |
| Marriage roles: complementarian and egalitarian | Women are ordained with full clergy rights, continuing Methodist practice (¶406 forbids discrimination by gender in "the membership and leadership of the Church at any level"); marriage is between one man and one woman (¶302.7), described in terms of mutual love without a complementarian headship rule. | Official (*Book of Doctrines and Discipline*, ¶406, ¶302.7) |

**The UMC/GMC split, side by side:** As of January 1, 2025, the United Methodist Church (the body the GMC separated from) now permits both the ordination of LGBTQ+ clergy and the performance of same-sex marriages — the GMC was formed specifically to continue the position the UMC has since changed. Presenting both bodies (a future entry will cover the UMC itself) side by side is intended to make this live, recent split visible rather than treat "Methodist" as one uniform position.

## 27. United Pentecostal Church International (UPCI)

**U.S. adherents:** figures vary by source; the UPCI itself reports roughly 6.2 million constituents worldwide, while other sources place U.S.-specific membership considerably lower (on the order of 1–1.5 million). The 2020 U.S. Religion Census reports its congregations (1,692 counties) but no adherent count, so it has no Census figure to rank by and is placed last -- the largest predominantly white Oneness Pentecostal body in the country either way, and, doctrinally, the one denomination in this section that departs from Trinitarian orthodoxy shared by every other body profiled here.
**Governance:** Congregational-presbyterian hybrid; local churches are self-governing (electing their own pastors, owning their own property, and setting their own budgets and membership), while ministers also meet in sectional, district, and general conferences.
**Origin:** Formed in 1945 in St. Louis, Missouri, by the merger of the Pentecostal Church, Inc. and the Pentecostal Assemblies of Jesus Christ, both tracing back to the Oneness ("Jesus' Name") wing of the early Pentecostal movement that emerged from the 1906 Azusa Street Revival.

### Doctrinal positions
| Category | Position |
|---|---|
| View of Scripture | Biblical inerrancy affirmed, consistent with broad Pentecostal evangelicalism. |
| Salvation | Requires repentance, water baptism "in the name of Jesus Christ" (rather than the traditional Trinitarian formula), and the baptism of the Holy Spirit evidenced by speaking in tongues -- all three held to be necessary elements of the new birth (Acts 2:38), a distinctively Oneness Pentecostal soteriology setting the UPCI apart from every other Pentecostal body profiled in this section. |
| Baptism | Believer's baptism only, by full immersion, performed specifically "in the name of Jesus" rather than the traditional Trinitarian formula ("Father, Son, and Holy Spirit") used by other denominations in this section. |
| The Lord's Supper | Observed as an ordinance/memorial, alongside foot washing as a further practiced ordinance in many congregations. |
| Church government | Congregational-presbyterian hybrid, organized into districts and a general conference. |
| Eschatology | Premillennial and dispensationalist, consistent with much of the wider Pentecostal movement. |
| Spiritual gifts | Explicitly continuationist and central to its identity, as with other Pentecostal bodies in this section -- but distinctively **Oneness** rather than Trinitarian: God is understood as a single person (not three coequal, coeternal persons) who is revealed as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in different modes or manifestations, a position historic Christian creeds (Nicene, Athanasian) explicitly reject as the heresy of modalism. This is the defining doctrinal distinctive separating the UPCI from every other denomination profiled in this section. |
| Women's ordination | Women may be licensed as evangelists, missionaries, and ministers, and have long served in these roles, but the office of bishop/general leadership has historically been reserved to men; practice on ordaining women to the pastorate varies somewhat by district. |
