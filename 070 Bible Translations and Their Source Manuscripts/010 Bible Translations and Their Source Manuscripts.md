****Notes****

- ****Masoretic Text (MT)**** / Biblia Hebraica (Kittel, Stuttgartensia/BHS, Quinta/BHQ) is the standard Hebrew base for most Protestant and Jewish translations; the Leningrad Codex is the primary complete medieval exemplar.

- ****Textus Receptus (TR)**** underlies the KJV tradition and related versions (NKJV, YLT, WBT, AKJV). WEB is related but distinct: its New Testament started from the ASV (a critical-text base) and was revised toward the Byzantine **Majority Text**, which overlaps with but is not identical to the TR (the TR is a small, late printed slice of the Byzantine tradition with a handful of unique readings, such as the Comma Johanneum, that the Majority Text does not share).

- ****Critical / eclectic texts**** (Nestle-Aland/NA, United Bible Societies/UBS) underlie most 20th–21st-century translations and prioritize earlier manuscripts (Alexandrian text-type).

- ****Majority / Byzantine Text**** is used by WEB and noted in some footnotes elsewhere.

- Douay-Rheims and CPDV are Vulgate-based (Latin tradition of Jerome, standardized in Clementine editions). NABRE, NRSV-CE, RSV-2CE, and related Catholic editions incorporate the deuterocanonicals primarily from Greek (LXX) sources.

- JPS is OT-only (Tanakh). SLT refers to Julia E. Smith’s highly literal 1876 translation. ERV here is the modern Easy-to-Read Version (not the 1881–1885 English Revised Version).

- Publication years reflect the first complete (or major complete) edition most commonly referenced; many have later updates. Exact manuscript editions used can vary slightly by revision.

Additional Notes

- ****Byzantine / Majority Text****: The text-type found in the large majority of later Greek manuscripts (mostly 9th century onward). Underlies the Textus Receptus and is the base for the World English Bible (WEB). Valued for its numerical dominance and relative uniformity, but generally considered secondary to earlier Alexandrian witnesses by most modern critics.

- ****Papyri**** (e.g., P46, P66, P75): 2nd–3rd century fragmentary NT manuscripts. Extremely important early evidence that often supports the Alexandrian text of Vaticanus and Sinaiticus.

- Reliability is relative: All these witnesses are copies of copies. Differences are mostly minor (spelling, word order, small additions/omissions). A common evangelical-critical position holds that no major Christian doctrine hinges solely on a disputed variant, since the classical doctrines can be argued from other, undisputed passages; this is a theological judgment rather than a manuscript fact, and it is worth noting that the Comma Johanneum is the most explicit Trinitarian formula in the NT tradition, that Mark's Gospel lacks any resurrection appearance without 16:9–20, and that Isaiah 7:14's "virgin" vs. "young woman" was the flashpoint of the RSV controversy—so the variants are not simply *unaffecting* doctrine, even if the doctrines themselves are not solely dependent on them. Modern translations typically print the preferred reading in the main text and note significant alternatives in footnotes.

- For OT work, the combination of MT (via Leningrad/BHS), DSS, and LXX provides the strongest basis. For NT, the critical editions (building on Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and early papyri) are the scholarly consensus standard.
