# Well-documented Differences That Affect Meaning

Old Testament / Hebrew Bible

1\. Isaiah 7:14 – “young woman” vs. “virgin”

- **MT**: *hinneh ha-’almah harah* – “behold, the young woman is pregnant / shall conceive…” (*’almah* = young woman of marriageable age).
- **LXX**: *idou hē parthenos en gastri hexei* – “behold, the virgin shall conceive…” (*parthenos* = virgin).
- **Meaning impact**: The LXX reading (quoted in Matthew 1:23) became the classic Christian messianic prophecy of the virgin birth. The MT reading is more naturally understood as a near-term sign involving a young woman in Isaiah’s own time (often linked to the birth of Immanuel as a contemporary figure).
- **Witnesses**: All preserved Hebrew manuscripts (including the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran, 1QIsaᵃ) support *’almah*. The LXX and its dependent versions support *parthenos*.
- **Consensus**: The Hebrew is clearly *’almah*; the LXX rendered it with the stronger term *parthenos*. Whether this was interpretive translation or reflected a different Hebrew reading is debated, but no Hebrew manuscript supports “virgin.”

2\. Psalm 22:16 (Hebrew 22:17) – “like a lion” vs. “they pierced”

- **MT**: *ka’ari yaday v’raglay* – “like a lion, my hands and my feet.”
- **LXX / some other witnesses**: “they dug / pierced my hands and my feet.”
- **Meaning impact**: The “pierced” reading is the one cited in Christian tradition as a prophecy of crucifixion. The MT reading is grammatically awkward and has been understood as a metaphor of being surrounded or mauled.
- **Witnesses**: The great majority of Masoretic manuscripts read “like a lion.” However, the Greek (LXX), Syriac Peshitta, some Vulgate traditions, and a Dead Sea Scrolls fragment from Nahal Hever (5/6HevPs) support a verbal form meaning “they pierced” or “they dug.” The Hever spelling (*ka’aru*) is itself grammatically awkward, and a separate Qumran fragment (4QPsᶠ) is too broken at this point to settle the question. A few medieval Hebrew manuscripts and the Masoretic marginal notes also show awareness of the alternative.
- **Consensus**: This is a genuinely disputed point, not a settled one. The “pierced/dug” reading is an ancient and widely attested alternative to the MT, and many critical scholars find it plausible or original. But it is not the unanimous scholarly view: Jewish scholarship and a good number of critical scholars continue to defend “like a lion,” and others regard both the MT noun and the verbal reading behind “pierced” as independent corruptions of an uncertain original verb. “Ancient and widely attested alternative” is a safer description than “the original reading.”

3\. Significant Dead Sea Scrolls alignments

- **Jeremiah**: The LXX is about one-eighth shorter than the MT and has a different chapter order. DSS fragments (esp. 4QJerᵇ,ᵈ) support the shorter Hebrew text underlying the LXX. This shows that two literary editions of Jeremiah circulated in antiquity; the MT represents an expanded edition.
- **1–2 Samuel**: Numerous DSS readings (4QSamᵃ especially) align with the LXX against the MT and often make better sense of difficult passages (e.g., the height of Goliath, certain details in the David narratives). The MT of Samuel is widely regarded as having suffered more textual corruption than most other books.
- **Deuteronomy 32:8, 43**: DSS and LXX read “sons of God” / “angels of God” where the MT has “sons of Israel.” The DSS/LXX reading preserves an older polytheistic or divine-council background that the MT appears to have theologically altered.
- **Isaiah**: The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) is generally close to the MT but contains hundreds of variants, some of which agree with the LXX and affect sense (e.g., orthographic, morphological, and occasional substantive differences).

4\. Samaritan expansions in the Pentateuch

- **The Gerizim commandment**: After the traditional tenth commandment, the SP inserts a lengthy passage (drawn mainly from Deuteronomy 27 and 11) commanding that an altar be built on Mount Gerizim and that sacrifice be offered there. This makes Gerizim the uniquely chosen place and elevates it to a tenth commandment.
- **Other expansions**: Frequent harmonizations (e.g., inserting material from Deuteronomy into Exodus and Numbers so that Moses’ speeches match later retellings) and occasional ideological changes.
- **Meaning impact**: The SP presents a Torah in which Mount Gerizim, not Jerusalem, is the central sanctuary from the beginning. Pre-Samaritan DSS (e.g., 4QpaleoExodᵐ, 4QNumᵇ) already show many of the same expansions and harmonizations, proving they are ancient Palestinian textual features later adopted and intensified by the Samaritans.
- **Consensus**: The expansions are secondary relative to the shorter MT form, but they witness to a real, circulating textual tradition in the Second Temple period.

## New Testament

**5. The Longer Ending of Mark (Mark 16:9–20)**

- **The issue**: The earliest and best Greek manuscripts (Sinaiticus ℵ, Vaticanus B, and others) end at 16:8 (“for they were afraid”). A longer ending (appearances of the risen Jesus, the Great Commission, signs that will accompany believers, and the ascension) appears in the majority of later manuscripts and in the Textus Receptus.
- **Meaning impact**: Without the longer ending, Mark ends abruptly with fear and silence; with it, the Gospel includes post-resurrection appearances, explicit missionary commands, and promises of miraculous signs.
- **Witnesses**: Absent from ℵ B (and the earliest Syriac and some Armenian/Georgian evidence); present in the Byzantine majority, A C D (with variations), Latin, and most versions. An intermediate “Shorter Ending” also exists in a few witnesses.
- **Consensus**: Most critical scholars regard 16:9–20 as a secondary addition (early, but not original to Mark). It remains in many Bibles, often marked with brackets or notes.

6\. The Pericope Adulterae (John 7:53–8:11 – the woman caught in adultery)

- **The issue**: This story is absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts (𝔓⁶⁶ 𝔓⁷⁵ ℵ B and others) and from the earliest versions and Church Fathers. It appears in later manuscripts, usually at this location, sometimes elsewhere in John or even in Luke.
- **Meaning impact**: The narrative of Jesus’ refusal to condemn the woman and his challenge (“Let him who is without sin…”) is one of the most memorable illustrations of mercy and judgment in the Gospels. Its absence removes that episode from the earliest recoverable text of John.
- **Witnesses**: Missing from the Alexandrian tradition and early papyri; present in the Byzantine majority and the Textus Receptus; some manuscripts mark it with asterisks or obeli.
- **Consensus**: Almost universally regarded as a later insertion (though an early and widespread one). Most modern critical editions place it in brackets or in a footnote.

7\. The Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8)

- **The issue**: The Textus Receptus and KJV read: “For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth…” The words in italics (the heavenly witnesses) are absent from all Greek manuscripts before the late medieval period (and from the early versions and Fathers).
- **Meaning impact**: The longer reading supplies the most explicit Trinitarian formula in the New Testament. Without it, the verse speaks only of the threefold earthly witness (Spirit, water, and blood).
- **Witnesses**: Absent from the entire Greek manuscript tradition until the 14th–15th centuries (and then only in a few late copies, some clearly influenced by the Latin); present in the medieval Latin Vulgate tradition and thus in the Textus Receptus.
- **Consensus**: Universally regarded by modern textual critics as a Latin interpolation that entered the Greek tradition very late. Critical editions omit it.

## Brief additional examples that also affect meaning

- **Genesis 4:8**: MT lacks Cain’s words to Abel; LXX, SP, Peshitta, and Vulgate supply “Let us go out into the field,” making the murder sequence clearer.
- **Exodus 1:5 / Acts 7:14**: MT “seventy” souls (so also Deuteronomy 10:22, stable in both MT and LXX); LXX and some DSS witnesses give “seventy-five” at Exodus 1:5, the figure Stephen uses in Acts 7:14.

- **Western text of Acts**: Substantially longer and sometimes different in narrative detail from the Alexandrian text; the differences affect the portrayal of events and speeches.

These variants illustrate the main patterns: (1) secondary expansions or liturgical/theological additions (longer Mark, Pericope Adulterae, Comma, Samaritan Gerizim commandment); (2) genuine textual plurality already present in the Second Temple period (Jeremiah, Samuel, Deuteronomy 32, Isaiah 7:14 rendering); and (3) places where the traditional MT or the later Byzantine/TR form appears to be secondary to earlier evidence preserved in the DSS, LXX, or early Alexandrian manuscripts.
