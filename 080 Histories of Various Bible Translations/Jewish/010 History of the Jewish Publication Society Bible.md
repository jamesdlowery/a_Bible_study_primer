# History of the Jewish Publication Society Bible

**The Jewish Publication Society (JPS) has produced the most influential English translations of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) for Jewish readers in the modern era.** Two major versions stand out: the 1917 translation (often called the Old JPS or OJPS) and the completely new translation completed in 1985 (the New JPS or NJPS, commonly known as the *JPS TANAKH*). A further gender-sensitive revision of the 1985 text has appeared in recent years. Together these editions have shaped Jewish Bible reading, study, and education in the English-speaking world for more than a century.

What follows traces the origins of the Society’s Bible projects, the distinctive character of each major translation, and their lasting significance.

## The 1917 Translation (Old JPS)

The Jewish Publication Society was founded in Philadelphia in 1888 to publish Jewish literature in English for American Jews. At its second biennial convention in 1892 the Society identified as its highest priority “a new and popular English rendition” of the Bible. The project drew representatives from the three leading American Jewish institutions of higher learning at the time: Hebrew Union College (Reform), the Jewish Theological Seminary (Conservative), and Dropsie College.

After early organizational difficulties, the distinguished biblical and Semitic scholar Max L. Margolis was appointed editor-in-chief. He and a board of editors produced a translation heavily indebted to the English Revised Version and the American Standard Version, yet carefully adjusted to reflect traditional Jewish interpretation and to remove renderings regarded as un-Jewish or anti-Jewish. The work was completed in 1915 and published in 1917 under the title *The Holy Scriptures According to the Masoretic Text: A New Translation with the Aid of Previous Versions and with Constant Consultation of Jewish Authorities*.

The 1917 version retained much of the cadence and vocabulary of the King James tradition (including archaic forms such as “thee” and “thou”) while presenting a distinctly Jewish reading of the text—for example, rendering Isaiah 7:14 as “young woman” rather than “virgin.” It quickly became the standard English Bible for American Jews and remained widely used for decades, including in the popular Soncino commentaries and the Hertz Torah commentary. The text is now in the public domain.

## The New Jewish Publication Society Translation (NJPS / 1985 TANAKH)

By the mid-twentieth century the language of the 1917 version felt increasingly dated. In 1955 the Jewish Publication Society authorized a completely new translation from the traditional Masoretic Text into contemporary English. The project was led by an interdenominational committee of Jewish scholars and rabbis. Harry M. Orlinsky of Hebrew Union College served as editor-in-chief for the Torah; other key figures included H. L. Ginsberg, E. A. Speiser, and later contributors to the Prophets and Writings.

The translation was issued in stages: - *The Torah* (1962; second edition 1967) - Portions of the Prophets and Writings in the 1970s - *The Prophets (Nevi’im)* (1978) - *The Writings (Ketuvim)* (commonly dated 1980 in JPS’s own materials, though sometimes given as 1982) - The complete one-volume *TANAKH: The Holy Scriptures* (1985), which is the date that matters for the completed NJPS translation

Unlike the 1917 version, the NJPS was not a revision of any existing English Bible. It was an original translation that sought accuracy to the Hebrew, clarity in modern English, and fidelity to Jewish interpretive tradition. It adopted a more idiomatic style than the formal, somewhat archaic 1917 text and made greater use of gender-neutral language where the Hebrew permitted. The 1985 *JPS TANAKH* became the most widely used English Jewish Bible of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries.

## Later Developments

In recent years the Jewish Publication Society has issued a gender-sensitive revision of the 1985 translation (sometimes designated RJPS or the Revised JPS edition). This update reflects advances in scholarship and changes in English usage while seeking to remain faithful to the Hebrew text. It employs gender-inclusive language for human beings where appropriate and generally avoids masculine pronouns for God, often rendering the Tetragrammaton as “GOD” in small capitals.

## Distinctive Character and Influence

Both major JPS translations are based on the traditional Masoretic Text and are produced by Jewish scholars for a primarily Jewish readership. They differ markedly in style and method:

- The **1917 version** stands closer to the classic English Bible tradition in tone and vocabulary while ensuring Jewish readings of key passages.
- The **1985 version** is a fresh, modern translation that prioritizes contemporary English readability and current biblical scholarship.

The JPS translations have been used not only in synagogues, schools, and homes but also by Christian and academic readers seeking a reliable Jewish rendering of the Hebrew Bible. The 1985 *TANAKH* in particular has sold over a million copies and remains a standard reference.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The Jewish Publication Society Bible translations occupy a unique place in the history of English Scripture. They represent the first sustained, committee-based effort by American Jewish scholars to provide their community with an authoritative English Tanakh. From the 1917 version’s dialogue with the King James and Revised Version traditions to the 1985 version’s independent modern rendering, and onward to recent gender-sensitive updates, the JPS project has continually sought to make the Hebrew Bible accessible, accurate, and resonant for English-speaking Jews while remaining rooted in the Masoretic text and Jewish interpretive heritage.
