# Catholic Editions of the RSV Family: NRSV-CE and RSV-2CE

**Both the New Revised Standard Version Catholic Edition (1991) and the Revised Standard Version, Second Catholic Edition (2006) adapt the same root translation — the RSV, itself a revision of the ASV — for Catholic use, but they descend from two different moments in the RSV's own evolution and reflect two different answers to how far a translation should move from that root text.**

## Shared Background: The RSV and Its First Catholic Edition

The Revised Standard Version (1946-1952), a revision of the 1901 American Standard Version sponsored by the National Council of the Churches of Christ in the U.S.A. (covered fully in its own entry alongside the ESV), was itself first adapted for Catholic use by the Catholic Biblical Association of Great Britain: a Catholic Edition (RSV-CE) appeared in 1965-1966, making minimal changes to the RSV text while reordering the deuterocanonical books into traditional Catholic sequence. The RSV-CE gained a loyal following for its dignity and accuracy and was later used in the English edition of the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*.

## The New Revised Standard Version Catholic Edition (1991)

In 1974 the National Council of Churches authorized a full revision of the RSV to incorporate further manuscript discoveries, linguistic advances, and evolving English usage; the resulting New Revised Standard Version (1989) was produced by a committee including Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, and Jewish scholars, under the summarized philosophy "as literal as possible, as free as necessary." Because Catholic scholars had already participated directly in the NRSV's own translation committee, preparing a Catholic Edition required little beyond including and properly ordering the deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1-2 Maccabees, and the longer Esther and Daniel) and omitting books Catholic tradition does not accept (1-2 Esdras, the Prayer of Manasseh). The NRSV-CE received the imprimatur of the U.S. and Canadian Conferences of Catholic Bishops in 1991.

The NRSV-CE is approved for private study and devotional reading, but has not become the primary lectionary text for most English-speaking dioceses — the New American Bible Revised Edition serves that role in the United States, though Canada received Vatican recognition for a modified NRSV lectionary text beginning in 2008. A further update, the NRSV Updated Edition (NRSVue, 2021), has more recently received its own Catholic Edition imprimatur, continuing the lineage.

## The Revised Standard Version, Second Catholic Edition (2006)

By the early 2000s, several factors prompted a different kind of Catholic revision of the RSV. The original RSV-CE still retained archaic pronouns ("thee," "thou") when addressing God; the 2001 Vatican instruction *Liturgiam authenticam* set clearer norms favoring fidelity to original texts and traditional Catholic terminology for translations intended for liturgical use; and the NRSV's move toward gender-inclusive language led many traditional-minded Catholics to prefer a version that stayed closer to the RSV's original formal style. Ignatius Press, a longtime champion of the RSV-CE, undertook the work.

Published in 2006, the RSV-2CE (also called the Ignatius Bible) removed the remaining archaic second-person pronouns while going in the opposite direction from the NRSV on several specific renderings, restoring more traditional Catholic phrasing: "virgin" rather than "young woman" at Isaiah 7:14, "full of grace" rather than "O favored one" at Luke 1:28, "begotten" restored at John 1:18 and parallel passages, and "mercy" (rather than "steadfast love") for Hebrew *hesed* throughout many Psalms. It has been approved for liturgical use in the Personal Ordinariates for former Anglicans and is frequently recommended by Catholic apologists and study-Bible editors, including those behind the Ignatius Catholic Study Bible (profiled in this book's Prominent English Study Bibles section).

## Two Editions, Two Directions from the Same Root

The NRSV-CE and RSV-2CE illustrate a genuine fork in how the same root translation could serve Catholic readers. The NRSV-CE inherited the 1989 NRSV's own committee-driven modernization, including its move toward gender-accurate language reflecting inclusive language in the underlying Hebrew or Greek. The RSV-2CE deliberately moved the opposite direction from the RSV's own 1971 text — modernizing only the archaic pronouns while actively restoring older, more traditional renderings the RSV itself had departed from. Both hold valid Catholic ecclesiastical approval; the choice between them functions largely as a choice between two different Catholic translation philosophies rather than between accurate and inaccurate options.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Together with the original RSV-CE from which both ultimately proceed, these two editions demonstrate how thoroughly the RSV's mid-century translation work came to serve traditions well beyond the mainline Protestantism that originally sponsored it — reaching, by the early 21st century, into specifically Catholic liturgical and devotional use through two quite different editorial philosophies.
