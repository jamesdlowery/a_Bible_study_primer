# The Vulgate Tradition in English: Douay-Rheims and the Catholic Public Domain Version

**The Douay-Rheims Bible (1582/1609-1610) and the Catholic Public Domain Version (2009) are separated by more than four centuries, but both share a distinctive commitment not shared by any other Catholic translation in this book: translating from the Latin Vulgate itself, rather than the Hebrew and Greek, as the Church's own authoritative text — with the CPDV explicitly using the Challoner-revised Douay-Rheims as a guide to traditional English phrasing.**

## The Douay-Rheims Bible: Origins in Exile

After the English Reformation, William Allen (later Cardinal Allen) founded the English College at Douai in 1568 to train priests for the English mission and preserve Catholic scholarship among exiles who refused to conform. Political unrest forced a temporary relocation to Rheims (1578-1593), where the translation work began. **Gregory Martin** (c. 1540-1582), an Oxford scholar and close associate of St. Edmund Campion, translated roughly two chapters a day and completed a full draft by about 1580; a small team including William Allen reviewed and revised his work.

The translators deliberately chose the Vulgate as their source, since the Council of Trent had declared it "authentic" and they regarded Greek and Hebrew manuscripts as suspect in non-Catholic hands — a choice that produced English often densely Latinate in its determination to stay close to the Latin. The Rheims New Testament (1582) included extensive polemical annotations defending Catholic doctrine against Protestant readings; financial constraints delayed the Old Testament until 1609-1610 (Douay).

By the mid-18th century the original text had grown archaic. **Bishop Richard Challoner** (1691-1781) undertook a thorough revision (1749-1752), modernizing vocabulary, smoothing Latinisms, removing the original polemical notes, and in places drawing on King James Version phrasing that had entered common English usage. Nearly every "Douay-Rheims" Bible printed since is in fact the Challoner revision. It remained the standard English Catholic Bible for two centuries, shaping Catholic preaching, catechesis, and devotion until new translations from the original languages appeared in the mid-20th century (the Confraternity Version, the Jerusalem Bible, the New American Bible).

## The Catholic Public Domain Version: A Modern Vulgate Recovery

More than two centuries later, **Ronald L. Conte Jr.**, a Roman Catholic lay theologian with a philosophy and theology degree from Boston College, set out to do for the 21st century what the Douay-Rheims translators had done for the 16th: render the Vulgate itself into current English, free of copyright. Working essentially alone, he began on 14 March 2004 and completed the entire Bible — Old Testament, Deuterocanon, and New Testament — on 28 March 2009.

Conte translated from the Clementine Vulgate (the same 16th-century edition underlying Douay-Rheims), relying especially on the 1914 Hetzenauer edition, and used the Challoner revision explicitly as a guide to traditional Catholic English phrasing — making the CPDV a fresh rendering of the Latin rather than either an independent Hebrew/Greek translation or a mere modernization of Challoner's English. Consistent with his stated aim of remaining faithful to Catholic doctrine and tradition, Conte avoided inclusive-language conventions, rendering nouns strictly according to the Latin's own gender and number. On completion he placed the entire work in the public domain without copyright, trademark, or licensing restriction of any kind, maintained at SacredBible.org.

## Two Translations, One Textual Commitment, Different Eras

Both works reflect the same underlying conviction — that the Latin Vulgate, not the Hebrew and Greek, is the Church's own authoritative scriptural text, a position rooted in the Council of Trent's declaration of the Vulgate's authenticity. Where they differ is institutional weight and audience: Douay-Rheims was a communal project undertaken by exiled scholars under ecclesiastical sponsorship, becoming the de facto standard English Catholic Bible for two centuries; the CPDV is the unsponsored work of a single 21st-century layman, offered freely rather than imposed institutionally, and reaching a comparatively modest audience of readers specifically interested in a modern-English window onto the Vulgate tradition. Neither is a current lectionary text — the New American Bible Revised Edition (covered in its own entry) holds that role in the United States today — but both remain valued precisely for continuing a textual commitment most other modern Catholic translations have moved away from.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Most modern Bible translation, Catholic and Protestant alike, works from the original Hebrew and Greek — a shift the Catholic Church itself formally encouraged through *Divino afflante Spiritu* (1943) and the Second Vatican Council. Douay-Rheims and the CPDV stand as the two most significant English-language exceptions to that broader trend, each a deliberate act of continuity with the Vulgate tradition that shaped Western Catholic theology, liturgy, and devotion for over a thousand years before either was written.
