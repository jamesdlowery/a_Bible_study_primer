# History of Young's Literal Translation

**Young’s Literal Translation (YLT) is an extremely literal English rendering of the Bible produced by the Scottish publisher and self-taught biblical scholar Robert Young (1822–1888).** First published in 1862, with a revised edition in 1887 and a further revision issued posthumously in 1898, it seeks to preserve as closely as possible the vocabulary, word order, and especially the verb tenses of the original Hebrew and Greek. Young is also remembered as the compiler of the widely used *Young’s Analytical Concordance to the Bible*.

What follows traces Robert Young’s background, the principles and publication of his translation, its distinctive features, and its enduring value for careful study of the Scriptures.

## Robert Young

Robert Young was born in Edinburgh on 10 September 1822, the son of a bookbinder. He served an apprenticeship in printing while teaching himself a remarkable range of languages, including Hebrew, Chaldee (Aramaic), Syriac, Samaritan, Greek, Latin, and later Gujarati. He established his own printing and bookselling business in Edinburgh, specializing in works related to Old Testament studies and the original languages of Scripture.

Young was associated with the Free Church of Scotland and for a time served in connection with mission work and the mission press in Surat, India. His scholarly labors produced not only the Literal Translation but also the monumental *Analytical Concordance to the Bible*, *Concise Critical Comments on the Holy Bible*, and other linguistic tools. He died in Edinburgh on 14 October 1888.

## Purpose and Principles of the Translation

Young was convinced that many English versions of his day altered the force of the original by changing verb tenses and smoothing idioms. He set out to produce a translation that would adhere as rigidly as possible to the letter and idioms of the Hebrew and Greek, so that English readers might more nearly see what the sacred writers actually wrote.

In the preface he stated that it was no part of his plan to construct a new Hebrew or Greek text; he therefore adhered to the received texts of his day—the Masoretic Text for the Old Testament and the Textus Receptus for the New Testament. He deliberately kept the work of translation distinct from the work of textual criticism, believing that few persons were qualified for both and that altering the base text without full explanation would leave the reader uncertain.

A hallmark of the YLT is its treatment of verb tenses. Young frequently retained present-tense forms in narrative contexts where other versions use the past tense, reflecting the aspectual character of the original languages rather than forcing them into conventional English narrative style. He also rendered the Tetragrammaton consistently as “Jehovah” (especially in the later editions) rather than the traditional “LORD.”

## Publication History

- **1862 (first edition)**: *The Holy Bible, Containing the Old and New Covenants, Literally and Idiomatically Translated out of the Original Languages.*
- **1887**: A revised edition prepared by Young himself, refining accuracy while leaving the underlying principles unchanged.
- **1898**: A third edition issued by the publishers after Young’s death. The publishers’ note explains that the text received a further careful revision for accuracy on the same principles, without altering the translation philosophy. This 1898 edition is the form most commonly reprinted and used today.

The translation never aimed at popular readability or liturgical use. Its stiff, often foreign-sounding English limited its appeal for ordinary reading, yet it found a steady audience among students who valued its transparency to the original.

## Distinctive Character and Use

Young’s Literal Translation is among the most consistently formal-equivalence English Bibles ever produced. Its strengths lie in the clarity it can bring to matters of tense, repeated vocabulary, and close verbal parallels that are often obscured in smoother versions. Readers familiar with Hebrew or Greek frequently find that the YLT helps them “see” features of the original that other translations conceal.

Its limitations are the reverse of its strengths: the English is frequently awkward, non-idiomatic, and difficult for continuous reading. It is best used as a study tool alongside more readable versions rather than as a primary reading Bible.

In recent years a major updating of Young’s approach into contemporary English, the Literal Standard Version (2020), has sought to carry forward the same literalist ideal in more accessible language.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Young’s Literal Translation stands in the nineteenth-century stream of rigorous, scholar-driven efforts to bring English readers closer to the wording of the original texts. Alongside works such as the Darby Bible and Julia Smith’s translation, it represents an extreme commitment to formal equivalence. Produced by a self-taught polyglot publisher rather than a large committee, it remains a singular monument of individual scholarly dedication. For those who wish to examine the Scriptures with maximal attention to the precise forms of the Hebrew and Greek, the YLT continues to serve as a valuable and distinctive resource.
