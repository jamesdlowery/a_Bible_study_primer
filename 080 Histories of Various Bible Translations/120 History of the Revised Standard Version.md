# History of the Revised Standard Version

**The Revised Standard Version (RSV) is a mid-twentieth-century English translation of the Bible, published in stages between 1946 and 1957 (with a New Testament second edition in 1971).** It is an authorized revision of the American Standard Version of 1901, itself a revision of the (British) Revised Version of 1881–1885, which in turn revised the King James Version of 1611. Sponsored by the National Council of the Churches of Christ in the U.S.A., the RSV sought to combine the best results of modern scholarship with English diction suitable for public and private worship, while preserving the enduring qualities of the Tyndale–King James tradition.

What follows traces its origins, production, reception (including controversy), subsequent editions, and lasting influence.

## Origins and Authorization

The immediate predecessor of the RSV was the American Standard Version (ASV) of 1901. In 1928 the International Council of Religious Education acquired the copyright of the ASV and appointed a committee of scholars to consider whether further revision was needed. After careful study, the Council formally authorized a revision in 1937. The work was carried out by a committee of American biblical scholars, meeting regularly (often at Yale Divinity School) under the chairmanship of Luther A. Weigle, Dean of Yale Divinity School.

When the International Council of Religious Education merged into the newly formed National Council of the Churches of Christ in the U.S.A. (1950), the project continued under the Council’s Division of Christian Education. The translators worked in two sections—one for the Old Testament and one for the New Testament—drawing on the best available critical texts and the growing body of archaeological and linguistic knowledge.

## Publication Timeline

- **1946**: Revised Standard Version of the New Testament published.
- **1952**: Complete RSV Bible (Old and New Testaments) released on 30 September amid widespread interdenominational celebrations.
- **1957**: RSV Apocrypha / Deuterocanonical books published at the request of the Episcopal Church.
- **1971**: Second edition of the New Testament, incorporating further textual and linguistic advances.

The translation was the first major English Bible to make significant use of the Dead Sea Scrolls (notably the Isaiah Scroll) in the Old Testament.

## Translation Principles

The committee aimed to produce a version that would be both accurate according to contemporary scholarship and suitable for liturgical and devotional use. It retained much of the cadence and familiar phrasing of the King James tradition while eliminating archaic pronouns (“thee,” “thou”) in most contexts and updating vocabulary. The style is generally formal and dignified. For the divine name the translators preferred “LORD” or “GOD” (in small capitals) rather than the ASV’s “Jehovah.”

The textual base for the Old Testament was the Masoretic Text, informed by the ancient versions and the newly available Qumran manuscripts. The New Testament rested on the critical Greek text of the time (Nestle-Aland).

## Controversy and Reception

The 1946 New Testament was generally well received. The 1952 Old Testament, however, provoked sharp controversy, especially among conservative Protestants. The most famous point of contention was Isaiah 7:14, where the RSV rendered the Hebrew *ʿalmāh* as “young woman” rather than the traditional “virgin.” Critics charged that the translators had adopted a non-Christian (or even Jewish) interpretive stance that severed the link between Old Testament prophecy and New Testament fulfillment. Other Christological passages were similarly scrutinized. The sponsorship of the National Council of Churches further fueled suspicion in some circles.

These debates contributed to the rise of the modern King James Only movement and helped motivate the later production of the New International Version and other evangelical translations. At the same time, many mainline Protestant churches, scholars, and educational institutions warmly welcomed the RSV for its accuracy, readability, and suitability for public worship.

## Later Developments

A Catholic Edition of the RSV (RSV-CE) appeared in 1965–1966, with a second edition (RSV-2CE) later published by Ignatius Press. The RSV text also served as the direct base for the New Revised Standard Version (NRSV, 1989) and, in a more conservative direction, for the English Standard Version (ESV, 2001). Thus the RSV stands as a pivotal mid-century link in the continuing revision of the English Bible tradition that began with the King James Version.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The Revised Standard Version occupies a central place in the history of the English Bible. It brought the formal, essentially literal tradition of the King James–Revised Version–American Standard Version line into the language and scholarship of the mid-twentieth century. Though it never achieved the universal acceptance its sponsors hoped for, it became the standard Bible for a generation of mainline Protestants, influenced countless study tools and commentaries, and provided the textual foundation for several major subsequent versions. Its story illustrates both the possibilities and the tensions that arise when new scholarship meets long-cherished traditional renderings of Sacred Scripture.
