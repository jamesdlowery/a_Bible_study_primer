# History of the Douay Rheims Bible

**The Douay-Rheims Bible is the classic English translation of Sacred Scripture produced by English Catholic scholars in exile during the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.** Translated from the Latin Vulgate, it was the first complete Catholic Bible in English and remained the standard English Catholic version for centuries. The New Testament was published at Rheims in 1582; the Old Testament followed at Douay in 1609–1610. The form most widely known and used today is the substantial revision undertaken by Bishop Richard Challoner in the mid-eighteenth century.

What follows traces its origins in exile, the original translation, the Challoner revision, and its enduring place in the English Catholic tradition.

## Origins in Exile

In the wake of the English Reformation and the Elizabethan settlement, many English Catholics who refused to conform left their homeland. In 1568 William Allen (later Cardinal Allen) founded the English College at Douai in the Spanish Netherlands (present-day northern France) to train priests for the English mission and to preserve Catholic learning. The college became a center of English Catholic scholarship in exile, drawing Oxford-educated men who had left rather than accept the new religious order.

Political unrest forced the college to relocate temporarily to Rheims (Reims) from 1578 to 1593. It was there that the great work of translating the Bible into English began.

## Gregory Martin and the Original Translation

The principal translator was Gregory Martin (c. 1540–1582), an Oxford scholar and close associate of St. Edmund Campion. Martin began the translation at Rheims in October 1578, working with remarkable discipline—roughly two chapters a day—and is believed to have completed the entire Bible by about July 1580. His drafts were reviewed and revised by a small team that included William Allen, Richard Bristow, Thomas Worthington, and others, all Oxford men.

The translators deliberately chose the Latin Vulgate as their source text. The Council of Trent had declared the Vulgate “authentic,” and the English scholars regarded it as the Church’s official Bible, preferred over Greek and Hebrew manuscripts that they believed had been subject to corruption in non-Catholic hands. They conferred with the Greek and other editions, but the Vulgate remained the final authority. The resulting English was often densely Latinate, reflecting a determination to stay close to the Latin wording even at the cost of smooth English style.

The **Rheims New Testament** was published in 1582. It included extensive polemical annotations defending Catholic doctrine against Protestant interpretations. Financial constraints delayed the Old Testament. After the college returned to Douay, the **Douay Old Testament** appeared in two volumes: Genesis–Job in 1609 and Psalms–2 Maccabees (with the Vulgate appendix books) in 1610. The complete work is therefore known as the Douay-Rheims (or Douai-Rheims) Bible.

## The Challoner Revision (1749–1752)

By the mid-eighteenth century the original Douay-Rheims text had become increasingly archaic and difficult for ordinary readers. Bishop Richard Challoner (1691–1781), Vicar Apostolic in England, undertook a thorough revision. Working anonymously, he produced:

- A revised New Testament in 1749
- A complete Bible (with a lightly revised New Testament) in 1750
- A further, more substantial New Testament revision in 1752

Challoner modernized vocabulary and phrasing, smoothed many of the more awkward Latinisms, reduced or eliminated the original polemical notes, and in places drew upon the language of the King James Version that had by then entered common English usage. The result was a more readable text that still claimed fidelity to the Vulgate (specifically the Clementine edition).

Nearly every edition of the “Douay-Rheims” Bible published from the late eighteenth century onward is in fact the Challoner revision (or a further light updating of it). The original 1582/1609–1610 text is now far less commonly encountered.

## Later History and Influence

The Challoner Douay-Rheims became the standard English Catholic Bible for more than two centuries. It shaped Catholic preaching, catechesis, private devotion, and literature in the English-speaking world. Various nineteenth- and early twentieth-century printings introduced minor updates in spelling and punctuation, but the essential Challoner text remained dominant until the appearance of new Catholic translations in the mid-twentieth century (notably the Confraternity Version and later the Jerusalem Bible, New American Bible, and others that worked primarily from the original languages).

The Douay-Rheims tradition also influenced later private and traditionalist efforts to recover or update the Vulgate in English, including the Catholic Public Domain Version of the early twenty-first century.

## Distinctive Character

- **Vulgate-based**: Unlike the Protestant English Bibles of the same era (Tyndale, Coverdale, Geneva, Bishops’, King James), which worked from Hebrew and Greek, the Douay-Rheims deliberately translated the Latin text authorized by the Church.
- **Literal and Latinate**: Especially in its original form, it prioritized closeness to the Vulgate over idiomatic English.
- **Catholic orientation**: Prefaces, annotations (in the original), and translational choices reflected Counter-Reformation theology and a concern to safeguard doctrine.
- **Enduring liturgical and devotional use**: Even after newer translations appeared, many English-speaking Catholics continued to treasure the Douay-Rheims for its traditional language and theological reliability.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The Douay-Rheims Bible stands as the foundational English Catholic translation. Born in exile and persecution, it provided English-speaking Catholics with a complete Bible drawn from the Church’s official Latin text at a time when Protestant versions dominated the English landscape. Through Challoner’s revision it became accessible to successive generations and remained the primary English Catholic Bible until the twentieth century. Its influence continues among those who value the Vulgate tradition and the classic cadences of English Catholic Scripture.
