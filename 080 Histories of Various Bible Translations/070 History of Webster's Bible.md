# History of Webster's Bible

**Webster’s Bible (sometimes called the Webster Bible or Webster’s Revision) is a limited revision of the King James Version prepared by the American lexicographer and educator Noah Webster and published in New Haven in 1833.** Formally titled *The Holy Bible, Containing the Old and New Testaments, in the Common Version, with Amendments of the Language*, it sought to modernize obsolete or offensive words, correct grammatical irregularities, and adapt the language slightly for American readers while preserving the overall character and cadence of the Authorized Version. It remains one of the few significant English Bible revisions published between the King James Version of 1611 and the Revised Version of 1881–1885.

What follows traces Noah Webster’s background, his reasons for the revision, the nature of the changes, and the work’s modest but distinctive place in the history of the English Bible.

## Noah Webster

Noah Webster (1758–1843) is best known as the compiler of *An American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828) and as the author of the widely used “blue-backed” spelling books that shaped American education for generations. A graduate of Yale, he was a teacher, lawyer, political writer, and tireless advocate for a distinctly American form of the English language. He believed that the United States, having achieved political independence, should also cultivate linguistic and cultural independence from Britain.

Webster was a devout Christian who regarded the Bible as central to moral and educational life. He was concerned that the language of the King James Version, after more than two centuries, contained obsolete words, awkward grammar, and expressions that he considered indelicate or unsuitable for reading in schools and families—especially by young people and women.

## Purpose of the Revision

In the preface to his 1833 Bible, Webster explained that the King James Version was, in general, excellent in style and adapted to its subjects, yet certain words had fallen out of use or changed meaning, while others were “so offensive, especially to females, as to create a reluctance in young persons to attend Bible classes.” He undertook the revision to remove these obstacles without altering the “general character of the style.”

He described the principal changes as falling into three classes: 1. Substitution of current words and phrases for those that were obsolete or undignified.
2. Correction of grammatical errors or inconsistencies.
3. Removal or softening of language he judged coarse or indelicate.

Webster insisted that he had avoided unnecessary innovation and had consulted the original languages only where needed to clarify the sense. The underlying textual base remained that of the King James Version (Masoretic Text for the Old Testament and Textus Receptus for the New Testament).

## Nature of the Changes

The revision is notably conservative. Most of the text is identical or nearly identical to the King James Version. Typical alterations include:

- “Why” for “wherefore”
- “Its” for “his” when referring to inanimate objects
- “Holy Spirit” for “Holy Ghost”
- “Male child” for “man child”
- “Passover” for “Easter” in Acts 12:4
- Softened renderings of words Webster found offensive (e.g., “lewd woman” in place of “whore”)
- Modernized spellings consistent with Webster’s American orthography

The result is a Bible that still reads very much like the King James Version, with only the most archaic or problematic expressions adjusted. Modern observers often remark on how lightly Webster revised the text.

## Publication and Reception

The complete Bible was published in 1833 by Durrie and Peck of New Haven. A separate New Testament appeared in 1839, and a second edition of the full Bible in 1841. Despite Webster’s reputation and the careful nature of the work, the revision did not achieve widespread adoption. Traditionalists preferred the unaltered King James Version; those seeking more thorough modernization found Webster’s changes too modest. Sales were limited, and the edition gradually faded from common use.

Nevertheless, the work did not disappear entirely. It has been reprinted in modern times (notably by Baker Book House in 1987) and is freely available in digital form. Scholars value it as a window into early nineteenth-century American attitudes toward biblical language and as a minor but genuine contribution to the Americanization of the English Bible.

## Influence and Legacy

Webster’s Bible exerted a quiet influence on later American revision efforts. Some of the linguistic judgments he made—particularly regarding obsolete or awkward King James phrasing—were shared by the American scholars who later participated in the Revised Version project and shaped the American Standard Version of 1901. In this indirect way, the lexicographer who defined American English also helped shape the language of subsequent American Bibles.

The edition is sometimes referred to as the “Common Version,” reflecting Webster’s desire to present an improved form of the Bible already in common use.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Webster’s Bible stands as a characteristically American project of the early republic: a respectful, limited modernization of the King James Version undertaken by the nation’s leading language authority. It neither challenged the textual basis of the Authorized Version nor sought to produce a fully contemporary translation. Instead, it aimed to make the familiar text more usable in American schools, homes, and churches by removing linguistic barriers that time had erected. Though it never displaced the King James Version, it remains a significant artifact of American biblical and linguistic history—the work of a man who believed that both the English language and the English Bible should speak clearly to the new nation.
