# History of the Legacy Standard Bible

**The Legacy Standard Bible (LSB) is a 2021 translation that deliberately preserves and refines the formal-equivalence tradition of the New American Standard Bible (NASB), particularly its 1995 edition.**

## Origins

In 2020 leaders from the Lockman Foundation, Three Sixteen Publishing, and the John MacArthur Charitable Trust met to explore an update that would specifically preserve the legacy of the NASB 1995. The resulting project, the Legacy Standard Bible, was translated by faculty of The Master's University and The Master's Seminary and reviewed by an international team of more than seventy scholars, pastors, and readers.

The LSB is not an independent new translation but a careful refinement of the NASB 1995. Translators returned to the original Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek for every verse, retaining the NASB wording where it was judged accurate and adjusting it where greater consistency, grammatical precision, or tighter phrasing was possible.

## Distinctive Features

- Consistent recovery of the divine name as "Yahweh" in the Old Testament (rather than the traditional "LORD").
- More consistent rendering of the Greek *doulos* as "slave" rather than "servant" or "bond-servant."
- Heightened attention to matching English words consistently with their original-language counterparts and preserving original grammatical structures.

The New Testament with Psalms and Proverbs appeared in early 2021; the full Bible followed later that year. The LSB is presented as a "legacy" edition intended to carry forward the NASB tradition of formal equivalence for future generations of readers and preachers.

## Textual Basis

Like the NASB, the LSB relies on critical editions of the original languages: the Hebrew and Greek text underlying its NASB 1995 base ultimately traces to Kittel's *Biblia Hebraica* and the Nestle-Aland / United Bible Societies Greek New Testament, with the LSB's own translators additionally consulting *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* and *Biblia Hebraica Quinta*, both available by the time of the LSB's 2021 completion.

## Reception and Influence

The LSB continues the NASB's trajectory, deliberately preserving the strengths of the 1995 text while applying its principles more consistently and recovering certain traditional renderings (most notably "Yahweh"). Together with the NASB, it forms a coherent stream within the broader English Bible tradition that values precision and transparency to the original languages. Its early and vocal adoption by John MacArthur and the Grace to You ministry, along with faculty across The Master's University and Seminary, has anchored its initial readership in expository-preaching circles already committed to the NASB tradition, though it remains a considerably newer and less widely adopted translation than its parent text.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The LSB positions itself as a conscious act of preservation rather than innovation -- a deliberate attempt to carry the NASB 1995's formal-equivalence commitments forward for a new generation of readers, at a moment when the Lockman Foundation's own 2020 revision of the NASB was moving in a different direction (toward greater gender-inclusive language in places). In that sense the LSB and the NASB 2020 represent a fork in the same translation lineage: two publishers' differing judgments about how the same 1995 base text should evolve, made within a few years of each other. Alongside the ESV and NASB, the LSB now stands as one of the most formally literal English translations in current print, distinguished chiefly by its restoration of the divine name "Yahweh" and its consistent rendering of *doulos* as "slave" -- choices that make it, of the three, the one most willing to sacrifice traditional or softened phrasing for what its translators judge to be greater fidelity to the original text.
