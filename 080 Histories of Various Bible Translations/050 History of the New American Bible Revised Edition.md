# History of the New American Bible Revised Edition

**The New American Bible Revised Edition (NABRE) is the current standard English translation of the Bible for many American Catholics, published in 2011.** It represents a thorough revision of the Old Testament (including the Psalms) of the original New American Bible (NAB) of 1970, combined with the already-revised New Testament of 1986. Sponsored by the Confraternity of Christian Doctrine and carried out by scholars of the Catholic Biblical Association, the NABRE aims at greater accuracy, consistency, and contemporary English while remaining a formal-equivalence translation from the original languages.

What follows traces the longer history of the New American Bible tradition, the specific work that produced the 2011 Revised Edition, and its place in Catholic biblical life in the United States.

## Origins of the New American Bible

The roots of the NAB lie in the Confraternity of Christian Doctrine (CCD) and the desire for an American Catholic translation suitable for study and devotion. In 1936, under the leadership of Bishop Edwin V. O’Hara, plans were made to revise the Challoner-Rheims (Douay-Rheims) version. The project soon expanded. After Pope Pius XII’s encyclical *Divino afflante Spiritu* (1943) encouraged translation from the original languages rather than solely from the Latin Vulgate, the work shifted decisively to Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek.

Scholars of the newly formed Catholic Biblical Association produced the Old Testament in stages (published in four volumes between 1952 and 1969) and a new New Testament. When the complete Bible appeared in 1970, it was given the name *New American Bible*. It was the first complete American Catholic Bible translated primarily from the original languages and included the deuterocanonical books as an integral part of the Old Testament. Non-Catholic scholars also participated, reflecting the ecumenical openness encouraged by the Second Vatican Council.

## Intermediate Revisions

The 1970 NAB was not static. A thorough revision of the New Testament was undertaken beginning in 1978 and published in 1986; this revised New Testament has remained the basis of the American lectionary. The Psalms were revised in 1991. By the early 1990s, however, the rest of the Old Testament still reflected the scholarship and English of the mid-twentieth century. Advances in textual criticism (especially the full publication and study of the Dead Sea Scrolls), linguistics, and English usage made a comprehensive revision desirable.

In 1990 the Catholic Biblical Association urged revision of the remaining Old Testament books. The project was formally authorized, and work began in 1994.

## Production of the NABRE

Nearly one hundred scholars, revisers, editors, and bishops took part in the work that culminated in the NABRE. The bulk of the Old Testament revision (approximately forty revisers and an editorial board) was completed by 2001–2002. The text was then reviewed by the bishops, returned for further refinement, and given final approval by the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops (USCCB) in 2008. A further revision of the Psalter was carried out between 2009 and 2010 to improve its suitability for liturgical proclamation and singing.

The finished Revised Edition was approved for publication in 2010 and released on 9 March 2011 (Ash Wednesday). It consists of: - The fully revised Old Testament (including the 2010 Psalter) - The 1986 revised New Testament

The translation is described as formal equivalence, seeking greater consistency in rendering key terms and closer adherence to the underlying Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek than the 1970 text in many places, while still producing readable contemporary English.

## Textual Basis and Character

For the Old Testament the primary base is the Masoretic Text, corrected or supplemented where warranted by the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and other ancient witnesses. The New Testament rests on the critical Greek text used for the 1986 revision. The NABRE includes extensive introductions, footnotes, and cross-references that reflect mainstream Catholic biblical scholarship.

Although the NABRE is approved for private study and devotion, the lectionary used at Mass in the dioceses of the United States continues to be based on the earlier NAB (with the 1986 New Testament). A further revision of the New Testament is underway so that, in time, a single consistent text may serve both personal reading and liturgical proclamation; plans have been announced for a future edition under a new name.

## Reception and Use

The NABRE quickly became the standard study Bible for many American Catholics. It is widely available in print, digital, and audio formats from multiple licensed publishers. Its notes and introductions make it particularly useful for catechesis, personal study, and academic work within a Catholic framework. Some readers prefer more traditional language or different translation philosophies, yet the NABRE remains the most widely authorized and used contemporary English Catholic Bible in the United States.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The New American Bible Revised Edition stands in the direct line of American Catholic efforts, stretching back to the Confraternity Version, to provide the faithful with Scripture in their own language translated from the original texts. It embodies the principles of *Divino afflante Spiritu* and the Second Vatican Council’s call for accessible, accurate translations. By updating the Old Testament in light of decades of further scholarship while retaining the already-revised New Testament, the NABRE offers a coherent, modern Catholic Bible that continues to serve the Church’s pastoral, liturgical, and educational needs.
