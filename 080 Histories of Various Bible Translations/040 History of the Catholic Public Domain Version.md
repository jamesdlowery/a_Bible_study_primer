# History of the Catholic Public Domain Version

**The Catholic Public Domain Version (CPDV) is a modern English translation of the entire Catholic Bible produced by a single translator, Ronald L. Conte Jr.** Completed in 2009 and placed entirely in the public domain, it is a fairly literal rendering of the Latin Vulgate (the Sixtus V and Clement VIII edition, commonly called the Clementine Vulgate), prepared with the Challoner revision of the Douay-Rheims Bible as a primary guide. The CPDV was undertaken as a work of private scholarship for the service of the Church and the greater glory of God, without institutional sponsorship or copyright restriction.

What follows traces its origins, method, distinctive features, and availability.

## Origins and Translator

Ronald L. Conte Jr., a Roman Catholic lay theologian with a bachelor’s degree in philosophy and theology from Boston College, began the translation on 14 March 2004. Working nearly every day for just over five years, he completed the entire Bible—Old Testament, Deuterocanonical books, and New Testament—on 28 March 2009. He has described the project as a solitary labor carried out under God’s providence, with no editorial committee or institutional oversight.

Conte’s stated purpose was to provide a faithful, modern-English version of the Latin Vulgate that would remain free of copyright, available for unrestricted use by the faithful, and consistent with Roman Catholic teaching. He has emphasized that the translation was made “in the light of the New Testament” for the Old Testament and “in the light of Roman Catholic doctrine” for both Testaments.

## Source Text and Translation Method

The CPDV is translated from the Clementine Vulgate (the edition prepared under Popes Sixtus V and Clement VIII in the late sixteenth century). Conte consulted multiple printed editions of this text, relying especially on the 1914 Hetzenauer edition, with reference to the 1861 Vercellone and 1885 Desclée editions, as well as comparative materials on the earlier Sixtine and Clementine printings.

As a guide to English phrasing and traditional Catholic renderings, he made extensive use of the Challoner revision of the Douay-Rheims Bible. The result is not a mere modernization of Challoner, nor an independent translation from the Hebrew and Greek; it is a new English rendering of the Latin that seeks both freshness and continuity with the classic English Catholic tradition.

The translation philosophy is deliberately literal (formal equivalence). Conte has stated that the CPDV avoids inclusive or gender-neutral language, rendering nouns according to the gender and number of the Latin source. He has also noted that the work adheres to the Vatican norms for Bible translation in force at the time. The Old Testament was translated with New Testament fulfillment in view, and the whole was shaped by Catholic doctrinal understanding.

## Publication and Public-Domain Status

Upon completion in 2009, Conte placed the entire translation in the public domain, declaring that it carries no copyright, trademark, or other legal restriction. Anyone may use, copy, print, distribute, or adapt the text freely. Master files have been maintained online at SacredBible.org, which serves as the authoritative digital source.

Printed editions have been issued through print-on-demand services (including multi-volume sets covering the Pentateuch, Historical Books, Kingdom Books, Wisdom Books, Prophets, and New Testament, as well as single-volume editions). Digital versions are widely available on various Catholic and general Bible websites.

Conte has also published a lightly edited edition of the Latin Clementine Vulgate itself and a Latin–English diglot containing both the Latin text and the CPDV, further supporting study of the Vulgate tradition.

## Distinctive Features

- **Vulgate-based**: Unlike most modern Catholic translations that work primarily from the Hebrew and Greek, the CPDV deliberately recovers and presents the Latin Scriptural tradition that shaped Western Catholic theology, liturgy, and spirituality for centuries.
- **Literal style**: It aims for close adherence to the wording and structure of the Latin, resulting in a text that is more formal than many contemporary dynamic translations.
- **Catholic orientation**: The translation reflects traditional Catholic readings and avoids the inclusive-language approaches adopted by some later twentieth- and twenty-first-century versions.
- **Complete Catholic canon**: All 73 books of the Catholic Bible are included, with the Deuterocanonical books fully integrated.
- **Free and unrestricted**: Its public-domain status makes it one of the few complete modern Catholic translations that can be used without licensing limitations.

The translator has acknowledged that, as a one-person work, the CPDV inevitably contains imperfections, and he has invited readers to use it prayerfully and critically.

## Reception and Place in the Tradition

The CPDV has found a modest but dedicated audience among readers who value a modern English window onto the Clementine Vulgate and the classic Douay-Rheims tradition. It is not an officially approved liturgical text and is intended primarily for private study and scholarly or devotional use. Its significance lies less in institutional endorsement than in its status as a free, complete, and deliberately traditional Catholic translation produced by a single devoted scholar.

In an age when most new English Bibles work from the original languages and often adopt contemporary linguistic conventions, the Catholic Public Domain Version stands as a deliberate recovery of the Latin Vulgate in accessible modern English—offered without restriction for the benefit of the Church.
