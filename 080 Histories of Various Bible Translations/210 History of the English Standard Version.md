# History of the English Standard Version

**The English Standard Version (ESV) is an essentially literal translation of the Bible into contemporary English, first published in 2001 by Crossway.** It stands consciously in the long tradition that runs from William Tyndale through the King James Version, the Revised Version, the American Standard Version, and the Revised Standard Version. Its makers sought a translation that would be both highly accurate to the original languages and suitably literary for public reading, private study, and the global church.

What follows traces its origins, the team that produced it, its textual foundations, subsequent revisions, and its place among modern English Bibles.

## Origins and Motivation

In the early 1990s, Lane T. Dennis, president of Crossway, began conversations with pastors and scholars about the need for a new essentially literal English Bible. Many felt that existing options either leaned too far toward dynamic equivalence or, in the case of some recent revisions, introduced gender-language choices that raised concerns among conservative evangelicals.

A decisive moment came in 1997 when John Piper urged Dennis to pursue the project and suggested obtaining rights to the 1971 edition of the Revised Standard Version (RSV) as a starting point. After negotiations with the National Council of Churches, Crossway secured permission in 1998 to revise the 1971 RSV text extensively enough to create a new translation. Work formally began that year under a Translation Oversight Committee.

The project was motivated by a desire to recover and continue the “Tyndale–King James” stream of English Bible translation: precise, literary, and suitable for the church’s teaching and worship, while updating archaic language and correcting perceived weaknesses in the RSV.

## The Translation Team

The work was overseen by a Translation Oversight Committee of approximately twelve to fourteen members, chaired by Lane Dennis. Key figures included J. I. Packer (general editor), Wayne Grudem, Paul House, C. John Collins, and others. More than fifty additional biblical scholars served as Translation Review Scholars, and a larger Advisory Council provided further input. The team was international and interdenominational, united by a shared commitment to the authority and inerrancy of Scripture and to historic Christian orthodoxy.

The committee met repeatedly in locations including Cambridge (England), Wheaton (Illinois), Orlando, and Hertford. Roughly 6–8 percent of the 1971 RSV wording was revised in the process of producing the first ESV edition.

## Publication and Textual Basis

The complete English Standard Version was published by Crossway in September/October 2001 (the first print edition appearing on Reformation Day, 31 October).

For the Old Testament the translators used the Masoretic Text as printed in the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (with consultation of the Dead Sea Scrolls and other ancient versions in difficult places). For the New Testament they relied on the United Bible Societies’ *Greek New Testament* (4th corrected edition) and the Nestle-Aland *Novum Testamentum Graece* (27th edition) available at the time; later editions have aligned with more recent critical texts (NA28/UBS5).

The stated philosophy is “essentially literal”: the translators aimed for word-for-word correspondence as far as English idiom and clarity would allow, while seeking literary excellence and the preservation of traditional theological vocabulary (e.g., “propitiation,” “justification,” “sanctification”).

## Subsequent Revisions

The ESV text has undergone several modest updates:

- **2002**: A small number of unannounced corrections to the original 2001 text.
- **2007**: A more substantial revision affecting several hundred places, improving grammar, consistency, and precision.
- **2011**: Further refinements (fewer than 500 words across 275 verses).
- **2016**: A final set of 52 word changes in 29 verses. Crossway initially announced this as a “Permanent Text Edition,” intending no further changes. That policy was soon modified; the publisher stated it would still consider minimal updates if required by new textual evidence or significant shifts in English usage. The edition is now generally referred to as the 2016 Text Edition.

An edition including the Apocrypha was published by Oxford University Press in 2009 for liturgical and academic use.

## Reception and Influence

The ESV quickly gained wide acceptance among evangelical churches, seminaries, and individual readers. Its combination of formal accuracy and literary quality made it popular for preaching, teaching, and study. The *ESV Study Bible* (2008) became a major publishing success and further cemented the translation’s influence. By the mid-2010s the ESV ranked among the most widely used English Bibles in the United States.

It has also drawn criticism. Some prefer more dynamic translations for readability; others argue that certain renderings (especially on gender language or specific contested verses such as Genesis 3:16 in the 2016 update) reflect particular theological commitments. Nevertheless, the ESV has established itself as a leading essentially literal option in the early twenty-first century.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The English Standard Version deliberately situates itself in the historic stream of English Bible translation that values precision, literary dignity, and continuity with the church’s received vocabulary. By revising the RSV rather than starting entirely afresh, and by maintaining an essentially literal philosophy, its makers sought to provide a Bible that could serve both the study desk and the pulpit for a new generation while remaining recognizably connected to the classic English tradition that began with Tyndale and reached a high point in the King James Version.

In an era of many competing translations, the ESV continues to be valued by those who desire a careful, literary, and theologically conservative rendering of the Scriptures.
