# History of the New King James Version

**The New King James Version (NKJV) stands as a deliberate bridge between the majestic tradition of the 1611 King James Bible and the linguistic needs of late-twentieth-century readers.** Published in full by Thomas Nelson in 1982, it sought neither to invent a new translation from scratch nor to abandon the textual and literary heritage of the Authorized Version. Instead, its makers aimed to “make a good one better,” echoing the very words of the original KJV translators, by updating archaic vocabulary and grammar while preserving the cadence, dignity, and formal equivalence that had shaped English-speaking Christianity for centuries.

What follows is an account of its origins, the scholars who labored over it, the textual decisions that defined it, the controversies it provoked, and its place in the ongoing transmission of Scripture.

## Origins and Motivation

By the mid-1970s the King James Version, last substantially revised in 1769, had become increasingly difficult for many ordinary readers. Words such as “conversation,” “prevent,” and “suffer” no longer meant what they once did; the second-person pronouns “thee,” “thou,” and “ye,” together with their corresponding verb endings, sounded foreign to modern ears. At the same time, a growing number of Christians still cherished the literary beauty and perceived doctrinal stability of the KJV and were reluctant to move to the newer dynamic-equivalence versions then appearing (most notably the NIV New Testament of 1973).

The immediate spark for the NKJV came within Thomas Nelson Publishers. According to accounts preserved by the publisher, a young Joe Moore asked his father, Sam Moore (who had acquired the historic Edinburgh firm in 1969), why the company that printed so many Bibles could not produce one he could understand. That simple question helped crystallize a larger vision already forming among some scholars: a thorough update of the KJV that would remain recognizably within the Tyndale–King James tradition.

In 1975 Thomas Nelson convened two foundational meetings—in Nashville and Chicago—bringing together approximately 130 biblical scholars, pastors, theologians, and Christian laypeople. Guidelines were drawn up, and the project was formally launched. The work would take seven years, roughly the same length of time required for the original 1611 translation.

## Leadership and Method of Work

**Dr. Arthur L. Farstad** served first as New Testament editor and later as Executive Editor of the entire project. A capable Greek scholar with a particular interest in the Byzantine (Majority) text tradition, Farstad coordinated the New Testament work and eventually joined the Old Testament review process. **Dr. James D. Price** chaired the Old Testament Executive Review Committee. Other key figures included Dr. William H. McDowell (English editor) and a broad interdenominational body of contributors drawn from Baptist, Presbyterian, Methodist, Independent, and other Protestant backgrounds.

The translators divided the text into portions, prepared drafts according to detailed guidelines, and submitted them to successive levels of review. The guiding principle was “complete equivalence”—a formal, essentially word-for-word approach that sought to retain as much of the original KJV sentence structure, rhythm, and theological vocabulary as modern English would allow. Archaic pronouns and verb forms were modernized (“you” and “your” replaced “thee,” “thou,” and “ye”), obsolete words were updated, and spelling and grammar were brought into contemporary usage, yet many of the classic phrases that had entered the bloodstream of English literature were deliberately retained.

The New Testament was published in 1979, the Book of Psalms in 1980, and the complete Bible in 1982. A minor revision followed in 1984.

## Textual Basis

For the **Old Testament** the translators used the 1967/1977 Stuttgart edition of the *Biblia Hebraica* (based on the Leningrad Codex), while making frequent comparisons with the 1524–25 Bomberg Rabbinic Bible prepared by Jacob ben Chayyim—the same Hebrew text tradition that underlay the original King James Version. The Septuagint and Latin Vulgate were also consulted.

For the **New Testament** the translators retained the *Textus Receptus* (the traditional printed Greek text that stands behind the KJV). This decision was made for continuity with the Authorized Version tradition rather than because the editors regarded the Textus Receptus as textually superior to all other witnesses. Farstad himself preferred the broader Majority (Byzantine) text and co-edited, with Zane Hodges, a Majority Text Greek New Testament. In the footnotes of the NKJV the editors therefore indicated significant differences among three textual streams:

- **TR** – the Textus Receptus reading adopted in the main text
- **NU** – the Nestle-Aland / United Bible Societies critical text (the basis of most modern translations)
- **M** – the Majority Text

The footnotes were intended to allow the reader to see the evidence without forcing a single verdict. As Farstad wrote, none of the three traditions was labeled “best” or “most reliable”; the reader was left to weigh the data.

## Reception and Controversy

The NKJV was warmly received by many churches and individuals who desired a readable Bible that still “sounded like the King James.” It quickly became one of the most widely used formal-equivalence translations in the English-speaking world and remains popular in conservative evangelical, Baptist, and independent circles.

It also attracted criticism from two directions.

On the one hand, some strict King-James-Only advocates objected that the very presence of critical-text footnotes “cast doubt” upon the traditional readings and that the language changes, however modest, constituted an unacceptable departure from the pure 1611/1769 text. Others noted that Farstad and certain committee members were not themselves committed Textus Receptus advocates and therefore questioned the consistency of the project.

On the other hand, scholars committed to the modern critical text sometimes faulted the NKJV for retaining the Textus Receptus as its base text, arguing that newer manuscript discoveries (especially early papyri and the great uncials) had rendered parts of the traditional text secondary.

Between these poles the NKJV has continued to occupy a mediating position: more traditional in textual base and style than the NIV or ESV, yet more accessible than the unrevised King James.

## Legacy

Nearly half a century after its publication, the New King James Version remains a living part of the English Bible tradition that began with Tyndale and reached a classical summit in 1611. It is neither a revolutionary break with that tradition nor a mere cosmetic polish of it. Rather, it is a careful, scholarly attempt to let the same Word speak with clarity to a new generation while still bearing the recognizable voice of the version that shaped the faith and language of the English-speaking church for three and a half centuries.

In an age of proliferating translations, the NKJV continues to serve those who desire both fidelity to the historic text and intelligibility in contemporary English—one more link in the long chain of those who have labored so that the Scriptures might be understood by the people of God.
