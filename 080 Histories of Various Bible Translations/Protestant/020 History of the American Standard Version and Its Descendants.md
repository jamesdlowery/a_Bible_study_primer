# The American Standard Version and Its Descendants: ASV, NASB, LSB, and WEB

**The American Standard Version of 1901 became the common ancestor of two genuinely different twentieth- and twenty-first-century trajectories profiled together here — a formal-equivalence lineage running through the New American Standard Bible to the Legacy Standard Bible, and a separate public-domain modernization, the World English Bible — alongside a third major branch, the Revised Standard Version and English Standard Version, covered in its own entry given its distinct institutional history.**

## The American Standard Version (1901)

In 1870 the Convocation of Canterbury authorized a revision of the King James Version; American scholars, invited to participate under church historian **Philip Schaff**, organized their own committee in 1871. By agreement, American preferences not accepted into the British Revised Version (1881-1885) were held back for independent publication until 1901, when the complete *Revised Version, Standard American Edition* — soon known simply as the American Standard Version — appeared through Thomas Nelson & Sons.

The ASV broke from the Textus Receptus underlying the KJV, relying instead on the newer critical Greek texts of Westcott and Hort (1881) for the New Testament, while consistently rendering the divine name as "Jehovah" rather than the traditional "LORD." Its very literal, sometimes stiff English (described by later critics as "strong in Greek, weak in English") limited its popular and liturgical use, but its formal accuracy made it the direct ancestor of an unusually large number of later translations: the RSV (1952), the NASB (1971), the Amplified Bible (1965), and, through the RSV, the NRSV and ESV as well.

## The New American Standard Bible (1971): Formal Equivalence, Renewed

Dewey and Minna Lockman founded the Lockman Foundation in 1942; by 1959 Dewey Lockman saw the need for an English Bible that would match the ASV's precision while incorporating manuscript evidence (including the Dead Sea Scrolls) unavailable in 1901. An interdenominational evangelical team produced the complete NASB in stages, dedicating the finished Bible in 1971. Its stated fourfold aim: fidelity to the original manuscripts, grammatical correctness, comprehensibility, and giving Christ his proper place.

The NASB underwent two further, increasingly substantial revisions: the 1995 Updated Edition removed remaining archaic pronouns and improved readability while keeping the formal-equivalence approach, and became the most widely used NASB text for a quarter-century (and later the direct base text for the Legacy Standard Bible, below); the 2020 edition modernized language further and introduced "gender accuracy" changes (e.g., *adelphoi* as "brothers and sisters" in many contexts), continuing to be published alongside the 1995 text rather than replacing it. The NASB's textual basis rests on Kittel's *Biblia Hebraica* and, in later editions, *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* and *Biblia Hebraica Quinta*, with the Nestle-Aland/UBS Greek New Testament throughout.

## The Legacy Standard Bible (2021): Preserving the 1995 Text

In 2020, as the Lockman Foundation's own NASB 2020 moved toward more gender-inclusive language, leaders from the Lockman Foundation, Three Sixteen Publishing, and the John MacArthur Charitable Trust instead launched a project to preserve and refine the NASB 1995 specifically. Translated by faculty of The Master's University and Seminary and reviewed by more than seventy scholars, the LSB is not an independent translation but a close refinement of the 1995 NASB text, restoring the divine name as "Yahweh" (rather than "LORD") and rendering the Greek *doulos* consistently as "slave" rather than "servant." The LSB and NASB 2020 represent a fork from the same 1995 base text, reflecting two publishers' differing judgments about how that text should evolve — the LSB choosing fidelity to traditional/literal renderings over the NASB 2020's move toward gender-inclusive language.

## The World English Bible (2000): A Public-Domain Branch

A separate branch of the ASV lineage emerged from a different motivation entirely. In 1994, convinced that no modern English Bible could be freely copied and distributed online without copyright restriction, **Michael Paul Johnson** began what would become the World English Bible, working under the nonprofit Rainbow Missions, Inc. Rather than attempt an independent translation from scratch — which he estimated would take longer than a single lifetime — Johnson chose the public-domain 1901 ASV as his base text, modernizing its language through roughly seven rounds of volunteer editing and proofreading per book, while shifting the New Testament's textual basis from the ASV's Westcott-Hort text toward the Byzantine Majority Text in places. A stable complete edition was reached around 2020. Like Darby's and Young's translations in a different entry in this section, the WEB renders the divine name as "Yahweh" in its classic edition. Because the WEB is entirely unencumbered by copyright, it has become widely used in software, websites, and mission contexts where royalty-free distribution matters — a legal and practical distinction rather than a stylistic one, since its underlying philosophy remains, like its ASV parent, essentially formal equivalence.

## Comparing the Three Branches

All three modern descendants chose fidelity to the 1901 ASV's precision as their starting point, but pursued different things with it. The NASB-to-LSB line pursued ever-greater formal consistency and lexical precision, culminating in the LSB's restoration of "Yahweh" and "slave." The WEB pursued the opposite kind of freedom — not doctrinal or stylistic innovation, but legal: a text anyone could copy without royalty or permission. Where the NASB/LSB line represents a continuing conversation about how literally the original languages should be rendered, the WEB represents a question about how freely the result should be allowed to travel.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Few single translations have proven as generative as the 1901 ASV. Besides the direct descendants profiled here, its influence runs — via the RSV — into the ESV and NRSV as well, making it, alongside the King James Version itself, one of the two most consequential ancestor texts in the entire modern English Bible tradition. Where the King James Version's descendants (covered in a separate entry) mostly asked how much of the old text's language could be preserved, the ASV's descendants have mostly asked how far formal precision could be pushed — a different question, answered differently by each of the translations descended from it.
