# History of the New International Version

**The New International Version (NIV) is one of the most widely used English Bible translations of the modern era.** First published in full in 1978, it was conceived as a completely new translation from the original languages into clear, contemporary English for the whole English-speaking world. Produced by a large international and interdenominational team of evangelical scholars under the oversight of the Committee on Bible Translation (CBT), the NIV has sought to balance accuracy with readability and has undergone several significant revisions while remaining the best-selling modern English Bible for decades.

What follows traces its origins, the translation process, major editions, controversies, and lasting influence.

## Origins and Vision

The vision for the NIV began in the mid-1950s with Howard Long, an engineer from Seattle and a devoted reader of the King James Version. As an evangelist he found that the seventeenth-century language of the KJV often failed to connect with the people he was trying to reach. After years of advocating for a new translation, his concern was taken up by the Christian Reformed Church and the National Association of Evangelicals.

In August 1965 a trans-denominational gathering of evangelical scholars met at Palos Heights, Illinois, and agreed that a new contemporary English translation should be undertaken as a collegiate endeavor. The decision was endorsed the following year by a larger assembly of church leaders in Chicago. Responsibility was given to a self-governing body of fifteen scholars, the Committee on Bible Translation (CBT). In 1967–1968 the New York Bible Society (later the International Bible Society, now Biblica) assumed financial sponsorship of the project, even mortgaging property to keep the work going. Zondervan later provided crucial support through advance royalties.

Unlike revisions of existing English versions, the NIV was designed from the beginning as a fresh translation from the best available Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek texts.

## The Translation Process

More than one hundred scholars from a wide range of evangelical denominations and from several English-speaking countries participated. Each book was assigned to a team of translators; the drafts then passed through multiple stages of review by intermediate committees and finally by the full CBT. The process emphasized both fidelity to the original languages and natural, readable English style. The philosophy has often been described as a mediating or dynamic-equivalence approach that seeks the best balance between formal and functional equivalence for clarity of meaning.

The New Testament was published in 1973. After further review and revision, the complete Bible appeared on 27 October 1978. Additional changes adopted by the CBT were incorporated in printings from 1984 onward; the 1984 edition became the standard text for the next quarter-century.

## Major Revisions and the TNIV Episode

**1984 Edition**
A modest but careful revision reflecting ongoing scholarly work and feedback since 1978. This text remained the dominant form of the NIV until 2011.

**Today’s New International Version (TNIV)**
In the late 1990s and early 2000s the CBT prepared a more substantial update that included greater use of gender-accurate (sometimes called gender-inclusive) language where the original texts were judged to address mixed audiences. The TNIV New Testament appeared in 2001 and the full Bible in 2005. It met strong resistance in parts of the evangelical world, was criticized as an unwarranted departure from traditional renderings, and never achieved significant market share. Publication of the TNIV was eventually discontinued.

**2011 Update**
In 2011 the CBT released a revised NIV that incorporated many of the scholarly advances and language updates considered in the TNIV process while moderating some of the more controversial gender-language choices. At the same time, the 1984 text and the TNIV were both withdrawn from print, leaving a single current NIV. Approximately 95 percent of the wording of the 1984 edition was retained; changes were made for reasons of accuracy, clarity, advances in biblical scholarship, and shifts in English usage. The Committee continues to meet periodically to review the text.

A simplified edition for new or less-fluent readers, the New International Reader’s Version (NIrV), was developed from the NIV and first published in the mid-1990s, with later updates aligned to the 2011 NIV.

## Textual Basis and Philosophy

For the Old Testament the translators used the Masoretic Text (primarily as represented in the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*), with consultation of the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and other ancient versions where appropriate. For the New Testament they relied on the critical Greek texts of the United Bible Societies and Nestle-Aland.

The NIV’s guiding aim has been to produce a translation that is both accurate to the meaning of the original and clear in contemporary English, suitable for public reading, private study, evangelism, and church life across a broad spectrum of evangelical readers.

## Reception and Influence

From the mid-1980s onward the NIV became the best-selling English Bible in the United States and one of the most widely distributed translations worldwide, with hundreds of millions of copies in print. Its combination of readability and evangelical scholarly credibility made it the default Bible for many churches, study programs, and individual believers. It has been produced in an enormous variety of editions, study Bibles, and ministry resources.

The translation has also been the subject of sustained debate, particularly around gender language and the balance between formal and dynamic equivalence. Critics from more formal-equivalence traditions have sometimes judged it insufficiently literal; others have welcomed its clarity and accessibility. The TNIV controversy of the early 2000s remains one of the more public episodes in recent English Bible history.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The New International Version arose from a distinctly twentieth-century evangelical desire: to provide a trustworthy, readable Bible for a global English-speaking audience without simply revising the King James tradition. By starting from the original languages with a large, international, and interdenominational team, and by committing to ongoing review, the NIV established itself as a major independent stream of English Bible translation. Its successive editions reflect both the enduring goal of clarity and the continuing challenges of rendering an ancient text into living English for successive generations of readers.
