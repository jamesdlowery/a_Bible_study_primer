# Nineteenth-Century Literalists: Darby, Young's, and Smith's Translations

**Darby's translation, Young's Literal Translation, and Smith's Literal Translation are grouped here not because one descends from another — each is an independent work by a single scholar — but because all three share a distinctive era, philosophy, and method: maximal literalness to Hebrew and Greek grammar and word order, produced by an individual working largely alone rather than a denominational or academic committee, within a roughly thirty-year span of the mid-to-late 19th century.**

## The Darby Bible (1867 NT; 1890 complete)

**John Nelson Darby** (1800-1882), a leading figure among the early Plymouth Brethren and a principal architect of modern dispensational premillennialism (ideas that later spread widely through the Scofield Reference Bible), also translated the Bible into German and French alongside his English work. Prepared explicitly for private study rather than public reading, his English New Testament appeared in stages through 1867, drawing on the critical Greek scholarship of Samuel Prideaux Tregelles rather than the Textus Receptus, with extensive textual and philological notes. Darby did not live to complete an English Old Testament; his students finished it after his death, based primarily on his own French and German versions, and the complete Darby Bible appeared in 1890. Darby consistently rendered the divine name as "Jehovah." Though never a popular reading Bible, it was consulted by the scholars who produced the English Revised Version (1881-1885), and remains valued within Brethren circles and among students of precise lexical study.

## Young's Literal Translation (1862; revised 1887, 1898)

**Robert Young** (1822-1888), a self-taught Scottish printer and publisher who also compiled the widely used *Young's Analytical Concordance to the Bible*, produced a translation explicitly organized around the conviction that English versions of his day distorted the original by smoothing verb tenses and idioms. Young deliberately kept translation separate from textual criticism, adhering throughout to the received Masoretic Text and Textus Receptus of his day rather than proposing his own textual judgments. His most distinctive feature is his treatment of verb tense — he frequently retained present-tense forms in narrative contexts where other versions use the past tense, reflecting the aspectual character of the original languages rather than conventional English narration. A revised edition appeared in 1887, and a further posthumous revision in 1898 (the form most commonly reprinted today). A modern effort to carry Young's literalist ideal into more accessible language, the Literal Standard Version, appeared in 2020.

## Smith's Literal Translation (1876)

**Julia Evelina Smith** (1792-1886) of Glastonbury, Connecticut, produced the first complete English Bible translation by a woman, working entirely alone without co-translators or a formal committee. Between roughly 1847 and 1855 she translated the entire Bible five times — twice from Hebrew, twice from Greek, once from the Latin Vulgate — remarking that she did not "see that anybody can know more about it than I do." Smith, active alongside her sister Abby in the women's suffrage and tax-resistance movements, did not publish the completed work for over two decades; she and Abby finally issued it at their own expense in 1876, at age eighty-four, intending to demonstrate that a woman was capable of the highest scholarly achievement rather than to turn a profit. Like Darby's and Young's work, Smith's translation renders the Tetragrammaton consistently as "Jehovah" and prioritizes literal tense and word-order fidelity over natural English idiom, arranging the Old Testament in traditional Hebrew book order.

## A Shared Impulse, Three Independent Paths

None of these three translators worked from or revised one another's texts — each approached the same problem independently, and each reached broadly the same conclusion: that smoothing the biblical languages into conventional English prose cost readers something real. What separates them is context rather than method. Darby translated from within a specific theological and ecclesiastical movement whose eschatology would go on to shape 20th-century American evangelicalism far beyond his translation's own modest circulation. Young combined his translation work with a broader career in biblical reference tools. Smith worked in near-total scholarly isolation, motivated as much by proving a point about women's capability as by a theological program. All three produced texts far better suited to close study alongside a more readable translation than to devotional or public reading — a limitation each accepted deliberately rather than as an oversight.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

These three translations represent the most extreme point on the formal-equivalence spectrum reached by any translations covered in this book — more literal even than the ASV or NASB lineage, which balanced precision against ordinary readability in a way none of these three attempted. Their continuing value lies exactly in that extremity: a reader who wants to see, as directly as English syntax permits, how a Hebrew tense or a Greek word order actually behaves has few better resources than this trio of translations, each produced by one person convinced that fidelity to the original mattered more than how the English sounded.
