# History of the Easy to Read Version

**The Easy-to-Read Version (ERV) is a modern English Bible translation produced by the World Bible Translation Center (WBTC), later associated with Bible League International.** It began as a specialized translation for Deaf readers and was first published as the *English Version for the Deaf* (EVD). Recognizing its usefulness for a wider audience—including children, new English learners, and those with limited reading skills—it was also issued under the title *Easy-to-Read Version*. The complete Bible appeared in 1987, with a major revision completed in 2004 (and further updates thereafter).

What follows traces its origins, translation approach, publication history, and ongoing purpose.

## Origins and Purpose

The World Bible Translation Center was founded in 1973 in Arlington, Texas. Its work on an accessible English Bible grew out of a recognition that many Deaf people, whose primary language is American Sign Language (or another signed language), often struggle with conventional written English Bibles. Sign languages have different grammar and structure from English, making traditional translations difficult to process.

The project aimed to produce a clear, simple rendering of Scripture that would communicate accurately while using shorter sentences, limited vocabulary, and straightforward syntax. Key figures in the early work included Ervin Bishop (Greek), David Stringham (Hebrew), and Benton L. Dibrell (a Deaf-language specialist, interpreter, and translator). Drafts were reviewed by additional scholars, among them Harvey Floyd and Everett Ferguson.

Although designed first for the Deaf community, the translation proved valuable for others who found standard English Bibles challenging—children, adults with limited literacy, second-language English speakers, and those in settings such as prisons or literacy programs.

## Publication Timeline

- **1978**: The New Testament of the *English Version for the Deaf* was published by Baker Book House.
- **1986–1987**: The Old Testament was completed. The full Bible appeared in 1987, issued both as the *English Version for the Deaf* and as the *Easy-to-Read Version* (the latter marketed more broadly without explicit reference to its original Deaf audience).
- **2004**: A major revision of the ERV was finished. It employed a somewhat broader vocabulary, refined style, and greater use of gender-inclusive language where the original texts allowed. The EVD text was left largely unchanged, so the two editions diverged.
- Later updates (including work around 2015) continued to refine the ERV for clarity, oral readability, and contemporary usage while remaining grounded in the original languages.

The translation has also been produced in numerous other languages under the Easy-to-Read approach, extending its reach globally.

## Translation Method and Textual Basis

The ERV is a meaning-based (functional or dynamic-equivalence) translation. The translators sought to convey the full sense of the original Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek in clear, natural, and simple English. Special attention was given to features that aid comprehension: short sentences, common vocabulary, and occasional brief explanations or synonyms placed in the text (often italicized).

For the Old Testament the primary base was the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (Masoretic Text), with consultation of the Dead Sea Scrolls and, in selected places, the Septuagint. For the New Testament the translators used the United Bible Societies’ *Greek New Testament* and the Nestle-Aland text (editions current at the time of the work).

Because the original EVD was shaped by the needs of signed-language users, it sometimes restructured English in ways that aligned more closely with the thought patterns of sign language. Later revisions of the ERV moved toward more standard written English while preserving the commitment to accessibility.

## Reception and Use

The ERV has found a steady niche among ministries focused on literacy, children’s work, prison outreach, English-as-a-second-language programs, and the Deaf community. Its simplicity makes it useful for oral reading and for readers who might otherwise be discouraged by denser translations. Critics sometimes note that the trade-off for readability can involve loss of certain nuances or a more interpretive style than formal-equivalence versions provide. Supporters value its clarity and its intentional design for those on the margins of traditional Bible reading.

After the WBTC’s partnership with Bible League International, the ERV continued to be distributed widely, both in English and in the growing family of Easy-to-Read translations in other languages.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The Easy-to-Read Version represents a late-twentieth-century effort to make the Bible genuinely accessible to readers who have historically been underserved by more literary or formal translations. Born from a specific concern for the Deaf community, it expanded into a broader ministry tool for anyone needing simple, accurate English. In the spectrum of modern versions it stands among the more readable, meaning-oriented translations—alongside works such as the New International Reader’s Version—while remaining distinct in its origins and its sustained focus on clarity for limited-literacy and second-language audiences.
