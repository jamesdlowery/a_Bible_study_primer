# The RSV-ESV Tradition: The Revised Standard Version and the English Standard Version

**The Revised Standard Version (1952) and the English Standard Version (2001) form a direct two-step lineage — the ESV is explicitly a revision of the RSV's own 1971 New Testament text — that runs, through the RSV, back to the American Standard Version and, through it, to the King James Version itself.**

## The Revised Standard Version (1946-1957)

In 1928 the International Council of Religious Education acquired the copyright to the 1901 American Standard Version and, after a decade of study, formally authorized a revision in 1937. A committee of American scholars, meeting at Yale Divinity School under **Luther A. Weigle**, produced the work in stages: the New Testament in 1946, the complete Bible in 1952 (released amid widespread interdenominational celebration), the Apocrypha in 1957, and a second New Testament edition in 1971. Sponsorship passed to the newly formed National Council of the Churches of Christ in the U.S.A. in 1950. The RSV was the first major English Bible to draw significantly on the Dead Sea Scrolls, particularly the Isaiah Scroll.

The 1952 Old Testament provoked sharp controversy among conservative Protestants, centered above all on Isaiah 7:14, where the RSV rendered Hebrew *almah* as "young woman" rather than the traditional "virgin" — critics charged the translators with adopting a non-Christian interpretive stance that severed Old Testament prophecy from New Testament fulfillment, and the controversy helped fuel both the modern King James Only movement and, indirectly, the later push for evangelical alternatives such as the NIV. At the same time, the RSV became the standard Bible for a generation of mainline Protestants and the direct textual basis for several later translations: a Catholic Edition (RSV-CE, 1965-1966, covered along with its own second edition in a separate entry in this section), the New Revised Standard Version (1989), and — in a more conservative direction — the English Standard Version.

## The English Standard Version (2001)

In the early 1990s, Crossway president **Lane T. Dennis** began exploring the need for a new essentially literal translation, motivated partly by concern that recent revisions had introduced gender-language choices some conservative evangelicals found troubling. In 1997, at John Piper's urging, Dennis pursued rights to revise the RSV's 1971 New Testament text; Crossway secured permission from the National Council of Churches in 1998 to revise it extensively enough to constitute a new translation. A Translation Oversight Committee of twelve to fourteen scholars — including general editor **J. I. Packer** and **Wayne Grudem** — ultimately changed roughly 6-8 percent of the 1971 RSV's wording. The complete ESV was published in 2001, deliberately positioning itself as a continuation of the "Tyndale-King James" stream: precise, literary, and explicitly affirming the authority and inerrancy of Scripture, in contrast to the RSV's more mainline theological sponsorship.

The ESV draws on the Masoretic Text via *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* for the Old Testament and the UBS/Nestle-Aland critical text for the New Testament, aiming for "essentially literal" word-for-word correspondence while preserving traditional theological vocabulary ("propitiation," "justification," "sanctification"). It has undergone several modest updates (2007, 2011, and a 2016 "Text Edition" that Crossway initially announced as permanent before softening that position), and the *ESV Study Bible* (2008) became a major publishing success that helped cement the translation's wide adoption among evangelical churches and seminaries.

## What Changed, and What Didn't, Between RSV and ESV

The ESV's revision of the RSV was deliberately conservative in scope (a 6-8% wording change) but significant in effect: where the RSV's Isaiah 7:14 rendering ("young woman") had been the flashpoint of 1950s evangelical controversy, the ESV returned to "virgin," among other changes aligning the text with historic evangelical doctrinal expectations the RSV's mainline sponsorship had not prioritized. The two translations thus share the overwhelming majority of their actual wording and their entire textual lineage back through the ASV, while occupying quite different positions in contemporary church life — the RSV as the mid-century standard for mainline Protestantism, the ESV as a leading translation among conservative evangelicals two generations later.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Together, the RSV and ESV carry the ASV's formal-equivalence commitments through the mid-twentieth century and into the twenty-first, while also illustrating how the same underlying text can be revised toward quite different theological communities depending on who holds the pen. The RSV's own Catholic and mainline Protestant descendants (the RSV-CE/RSV-2CE and the NRSV) are covered in a separate entry alongside the NRSV-CE and RSV-2CE specifically, underscoring how many of the most-used mid-to-late-twentieth-century English Bibles ultimately share this single root.
