# History of the New American Standard Bible

**The New American Standard Bible (NASB) is one of the most consistently formal-equivalence translations in modern English Bible history, and a direct descendant of the American Standard Version (1901).**

## Origins

The story begins with Dewey and Minna Lockman. On 3 December 1942 they donated a large portion of their citrus ranch in La Habra, California, to form the Lockman Foundation, a nonprofit ministry dedicated to Christian evangelism, education, and benevolence. In 1959 Dewey Lockman sensed the need for a new English Bible that would be clearly readable in contemporary language yet remain as precise as possible to the original Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek. The project was deliberately modeled on the scholarship and accuracy of the 1901 American Standard Version while incorporating manuscript discoveries (including the Dead Sea Scrolls) that had become available since 1901.

A team of scholars and pastors from a wide range of evangelical denominations was organized. Work proceeded portion by portion: the Gospel of John (1960), the Gospels (1962), the New Testament (1963), Psalms (1968), and the complete Bible, which was dedicated on 28 August 1971 at First Baptist Church of Anaheim, California. By 1977 the NASB had become a strong seller in America, with Christian Booksellers Association–linked rankings for that year placing it second, behind The Living Bible.

The translation philosophy was formal equivalence (word-for-word as far as English would allow), with special attention to verb tenses and original word order. The Lockman Foundation summarized its aims in a fourfold goal: to be true to the original manuscripts, grammatically correct, understandable, and to give the Lord Jesus Christ His proper place.

## Major Revisions

**1977**
A modest textual revision refined the 1971 text.

**1995 (NASB Updated Edition / NASB 95)**
A more substantial update removed remaining archaic forms ("thee," "thou," "thy") and improved clarity and readability while retaining the formal-equivalence approach. This edition became the most widely used form of the NASB for the next quarter-century, and later served as the base text for the Legacy Standard Bible (2021), a separate translation in the same tradition.

**2020 (NASB 2020)**
The Lockman Foundation produced a further revision that modernized language, improved readability, and introduced greater "gender accuracy" (for example, rendering *adelphoi* as "brothers and sisters" in many contexts, with added words in italics). Some verses previously retained in brackets were moved to footnotes. The 1995 text continued to be published alongside the 2020 edition.

Throughout these revisions the NASB maintained its reputation for literal precision and became a preferred study Bible for many expository preachers and teachers, including John MacArthur, Charles Stanley, and others.

## Textual Basis

The NASB relies on critical editions of the original languages: primarily Rudolf Kittel's *Biblia Hebraica* (the standard critical Hebrew text available at the time of the NASB's completion in 1971, since the newer *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* was not itself completed until 1977), and the Nestle-Aland / United Bible Societies Greek New Testament for the New Testament. Later NASB editions have been able to draw on *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* and *Biblia Hebraica Quinta* as those became available. The overriding commitment is formal equivalence — letting the English reader see as directly as possible the wording, structure, and meaning of the original text.

## Reception and Influence

The NASB established itself as one of the most trusted formal-equivalence translations of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Its successive updates sought to keep the language current without abandoning literal accuracy. It remains widely used for careful study and exposition, and its 1995 edition later served as the direct basis for the Legacy Standard Bible.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The NASB occupies the formal-equivalence end of the modern English Bible spectrum, alongside the ESV and (later) the LSB, standing in deliberate contrast to the dynamic-equivalence approach of translations like the NIV or NLT. As a direct descendant of the 1901 American Standard Version, it carries forward a lineage of English translation reaching back through the ASV to the Revised Version and, ultimately, to the King James tradition itself, while incorporating manuscript evidence unavailable to any of those earlier translators. Its reputation for word-for-word precision has made it a standard reference translation for pastors, seminary students, and expository preachers who want to see the original languages' structure reflected as directly as English allows, even where that costs some smoothness of style -- a trade-off the translation has always made deliberately rather than by accident.
