# History of The Readable Bible

**The Readable Bible (TRB) is a 2024 translation whose central innovation is not its translation philosophy but its presentation — the first cover-to-cover reformatting of the biblical text, its publisher argues, since the introduction of indented paragraphs in 1873.**

## Origins

The project traces to 2008, when Rod Laughlin — a pastor with an M.Div. from Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary, thirty-seven years into pastoral ministry — found himself in an airport bookstore looking for something to read, despite having a Bible in his briefcase. Recognizing that the Bible's plain-paragraph presentation was itself a barrier to sustained reading, he began a project that would occupy the next fourteen years: assembling a team of more than one hundred people, including Dr. Brendan Kennedy (Ph.D., Old Testament, Midwestern Baptist Theological Seminary) and Dr. Colby Kinser (D.Min., Dallas Theological Seminary), to translate and typeset the Bible using the structural conventions of modern nonfiction publishing rather than the continuous-paragraph format nearly every English Bible has used for over a century.

An earlier, partial edition appeared in 2022; the complete Bible was published March 19, 2024, by Iron Stream Media (New Hope Publishers), running to 2,224 pages.

## Distinctive Features

TRB's translation philosophy is conventional by modern evangelical standards — reviewers have placed it alongside the CSB and NASB 2020 in translation character, and its own publisher describes it as "primarily a word-for-word translation" that supplies a thought-for-thought rendering (with the literal wording footnoted) only where a strictly literal translation would confuse a modern reader. What sets it apart is entirely presentational:

- Genealogies are laid out as family trees, with the line leading to Jesus specifically highlighted.
- Census and other numerical data appear in tables rather than as continuous prose.
- Legal material (e.g., much of Leviticus) is set in an outline format resembling a modern legal code.
- Poetic and theological passages use a "cascading text" layout intended to visually trace the movement of thought within a passage.
- Digressions within the text are set off with callout boxes.
- Maps, a glossary, a subject index, tables of key persons, and tables addressing the more complex prophetic passages are built directly into every edition.

## Textual Basis

The Old Testament translation is based primarily on the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (BHS), 5th edition, with passages modified, where the BHS text is judged questionable or more recent manuscript evidence warrants it, drawing on the Septuagint, Dead Sea Scrolls, Samaritan Pentateuch, Targums, and Syriac text. The New Testament is based primarily on the Nestle-Aland *Novum Testamentum Graece*, 28th edition, with modifications from more recent manuscript and textual discoveries footnoted individually — a textual basis consistent with most other modern evangelical translations profiled in this book.

## Reception and Influence

TRB remains a considerably newer and smaller-circulation work than the major translations elsewhere in this book, produced by an independent, multi-denominational evangelical team rather than a single established publisher's in-house translation committee. Early reader response has focused heavily on its formatting innovation specifically — reviewers and readers alike have generally found the translation itself unremarkable in the most literal sense of that word (accurate, conventional, comparable to its contemporaries), while treating the presentational format as the genuinely novel contribution. Some reader feedback has also raised practical concerns common to a first-generation reference redesign, such as the absence of running page headers in early printings, making it harder to locate a specific book quickly compared to a conventionally formatted Bible.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

TRB is a useful reminder that not every meaningful development in English Bible history is a translation-philosophy question. Every other entry in this section represents a choice about how to render the Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek into English; TRB instead holds a fairly mainstream translation approach constant and asks a different question entirely — whether the physical presentation of that text, largely unchanged since the 19th century, is itself an obstacle to reading comprehension. Whether this particular formatting experiment endures the way the paragraph-and-verse-number convention it challenges has for over 150 years remains, as of this writing, an open question — but it stands as a distinct, well-documented data point in the ongoing history of how English readers are given access to the biblical text, not merely another entry in the formal-versus-dynamic-equivalence spectrum the rest of this section largely tracks.
