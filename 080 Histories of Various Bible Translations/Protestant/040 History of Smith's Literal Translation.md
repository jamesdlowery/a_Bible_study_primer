# History of Smith's Literal Translation

**Smith’s Literal Translation (often abbreviated SLT) is the complete English Bible translated by Julia Evelina Smith (1792–1886) of Glastonbury, Connecticut, and published in 1876.** Formally titled *The Holy Bible: Containing the Old and New Testaments; Translated Literally from the Original Tongues*, it is widely recognized as the first complete translation of the Bible into English by a woman, and remains the only such work accomplished entirely by one woman without co-translators or a formal committee. The translation is characterized by its extreme literalness, preserving the tenses, word order, and consistent vocabulary of the Hebrew and Greek as closely as English would allow.

What follows traces Julia Smith’s background, the origins and method of her work, its publication, and its distinctive place in the history of English Bibles.

## Julia Evelina Smith

Julia Evelina Smith was born on 27 May 1792 in Glastonbury, Connecticut, into a family of independent religious and intellectual interests. Her father, Zephaniah Smith, had studied theology at Yale and briefly served as a Congregationalist minister before turning to law. Julia and her sisters received an unusually strong education for women of their time; she acquired working knowledge of Latin, Greek, and later Hebrew.

The Smith sisters were active in the reform movements of the nineteenth century, including abolition, temperance, and especially women’s suffrage. Julia and her sister Abby became well known for their tax-resistance campaign against taxation without representation, chronicled in the pamphlet *Abby Smith and Her Cows*. Their public advocacy for women’s rights formed part of the later motivation for publishing the Bible translation.

## Origins of the Translation

Julia Smith’s intensive study of the original languages began in the 1840s amid the excitement surrounding William Miller’s predictions of Christ’s return. After the failure of those predictions, she and a small circle of sisters and a friend continued meeting to examine the Scriptures closely. Dissatisfied with what she regarded as the insufficient literalness of the King James Version, she determined to produce her own rendering directly from the Hebrew and Greek.

Between approximately 1847 and 1855 she translated the entire Bible five times: twice from the Hebrew, twice from the Greek, and once from the Latin Vulgate. She worked alone, without consulting other scholars, later remarking, “I do not see that anybody can know more about it than I do.” The project was originally a private spiritual and intellectual exercise rather than a work intended for publication. She wrote out the text in multiple handwritten drafts totaling thousands of pages.

## Translation Method

Smith’s guiding principle was strict literalism. She aimed to render each Hebrew or Greek word by the same English word wherever possible and to follow the tenses and grammatical structures of the original even when the resulting English was awkward or unconventional. In the preface she explained that altering the tenses would make the translator the judge of the text, whereas “the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.” The translation therefore often reads more like an interlinear gloss expanded into continuous English than a polished literary version.

Notable features include the consistent use of “Jehovah” for the Tetragrammaton in the Old Testament and an arrangement of the Old Testament books that follows the traditional Hebrew order (with Chronicles at the end). The English is frequently rugged and non-idiomatic, a direct consequence of her refusal to smooth the text for readability.

## Publication

Although the translation was essentially complete by 1855, Smith did not seek publication for more than two decades. In 1876, at the age of eighty-four and in the year of the American centennial, she and her sister Abby decided to issue it at their own expense. The cost was approximately \$4,000; one thousand copies were printed by the American Publishing Company of Hartford, Connecticut, and offered for sale at \$2.50 each. The sisters’ purpose was not commercial profit but to demonstrate that a woman was capable of the highest scholarly achievement and to make the literal rendering available.

Sales were limited. The awkward English style restricted popular appeal, and remaining copies were later sold at the family’s household auction. Nevertheless, the work attracted notice among scholars for its consistency and literal accuracy, and it stood for a brief period as one of the few contemporary English translations made directly from the original languages before the appearance of the English Revised Version (1881–1885).

## Reception and Legacy

Smith’s Bible never achieved wide popular use, largely because of its stiff, non-literary English. It has, however, been recognized by historians of the Bible and of women’s history as a landmark: the first complete English Bible translated by a woman, and a remarkable example of independent nineteenth-century scholarship. In later years it has been reprinted in facsimile and studied as both a linguistic curiosity and a testament to one woman’s determination and linguistic skill.

Julia Smith married the elderly Amos Andrew Parker in 1879; both were in their late eighties. She died in 1886 at the age of ninety-four.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Smith’s Literal Translation occupies a unique niche in the history of English Bibles. Produced outside any institutional or denominational framework, by a single self-taught woman motivated first by personal study and later by a desire to prove women’s intellectual capacity, it stands as an extreme expression of the literalist impulse. While most later translations have sought a balance between accuracy and readability, Smith deliberately subordinated English idiom to the forms of the original languages. Her work remains a singular monument to solitary dedication and to the conviction that the plainest possible rendering of the sacred text is the most faithful.
