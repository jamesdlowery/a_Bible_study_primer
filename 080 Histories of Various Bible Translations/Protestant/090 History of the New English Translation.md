# History of the New English Translation

**The New English Translation (NET Bible) is a completely new English Bible produced by a multi-denominational team of more than twenty-five biblical scholars under the sponsorship of the Biblical Studies Foundation (Bible.org) and published by Biblical Studies Press.** Conceived in the mid-1990s as a free digital translation for internet distribution, it is distinguished by its extensive translators’ notes—more than 60,000 in the full-notes editions—and by an unusually open and transparent translation process. The first complete edition appeared in the mid-2000s, with significant updates continuing thereafter.

What follows traces its origins, distinctive method, publication history, and place among modern English Bibles.

## Origins and Vision

The NET Bible project was conceived at the annual meeting of the Society of Biblical Literature in Philadelphia in November 1995. A group of biblical scholars recognized the potential of the internet to make a high-quality, modern English translation freely available worldwide. The goal was not merely another English Bible, but a translation that would document its own decisions with unprecedented thoroughness, allowing readers to see the reasoning behind every major choice.

The project was sponsored by the Biblical Studies Foundation (associated with Bible.org) and published by Biblical Studies Press. More than twenty-five scholars—most of them professors of Old or New Testament exegesis with specialized expertise in particular books—worked directly from the best available Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek texts. W. Hall Harris III served as a key project director; Robert B. Chisholm Jr. and others held major editorial roles. The team was inter-denominational and evangelical in orientation, describing the translation as non-sectarian.

## The Open Translation Process

A defining feature of the NET Bible was its public beta-testing. Working drafts were posted online for review and comment by scholars, pastors, and lay readers around the world. Millions of page views and extensive feedback shaped successive revisions. The translators did not aim for a consensus or “crowd-sourced” text; rather, they filtered all suggestions through rigorous linguistic, textual, and exegetical standards. The process made the translation unusually accountable and transparent.

This openness is crystallized in the notes. The full-notes editions contain over 60,000 translators’ notes of several types: - **Translators’ notes (tn)** explaining the rationale for a rendering and listing alternatives
- **Text-critical notes (tc)** discussing manuscript variants
- **Study notes (sn)** providing background and interpretive help
- Occasional map and other specialized notes

These notes allow the main text to prioritize the strongest scholarly option while documenting alternatives, difficulties, and the translators’ reasoning.

## Publication Timeline

- **2001**: First beta edition released
- **2003**: Second beta
- **2005–2006**: First complete edition published (often dated 2005 or 2006)
- **2017**: Second edition with updated Strong’s mappings and other refinements
- **2019 onward**: Further major updates; full-notes print editions issued in partnership with Thomas Nelson

The text and notes have remained freely available for download and non-commercial ministry use under a liberal copyright policy, while print editions (including full-notes volumes) have been commercially published.

## Translation Philosophy and Textual Basis

The NET Bible seeks accuracy within grammatical, historical, and theological context, with primary weight given to the grammatical sense of the original languages. It aims for clear, contemporary English without sacrificing fidelity. Where a more interpretive rendering best conveys the probable meaning, the translators often adopt it and explain the decision in the notes; where a more literal rendering is both accurate and readable, they prefer that course.

For the Old Testament the primary base is the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (Masoretic Text), with attention to the Dead Sea Scrolls and other evidence. For the New Testament the translators worked from an eclectic text informed by the Nestle-Aland and United Bible Societies editions, occasionally departing from the standard critical text where they judged the evidence warranted and documenting those decisions.

## Reception and Distinctive Contribution

The NET Bible has been valued especially by students, pastors, and translators who appreciate the depth of its documentation. Its free digital availability and extensive notes make it a unique resource for serious study. Critics sometimes find the main text more interpretive than strictly formal-equivalence versions, or note that the sheer volume of notes can be overwhelming for casual reading. Supporters regard the notes as the translation’s greatest strength—an honest window into the complexities of rendering ancient texts into modern English.

Print editions with the full set of notes have extended its usefulness beyond the screen, while the ongoing digital presence continues the original vision of free global access.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The New English Translation occupies a distinctive niche among twenty-first-century English Bibles. It is neither a revision of an earlier version nor a committee product shaped primarily by denominational or publishing-house priorities. Instead, it is a scholar-driven, digitally native translation whose hallmark is radical transparency. By pairing a carefully considered English text with tens of thousands of explanatory notes, the NET Bible invites readers not only to receive a translation but to understand the decisions that produced it—fulfilling in a new medium the long-standing scholarly ideal of accountable, well-documented work on the Scriptures.
