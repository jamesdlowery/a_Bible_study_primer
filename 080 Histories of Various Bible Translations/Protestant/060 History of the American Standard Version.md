# History of the American Standard Version

**The American Standard Version (ASV) of 1901 is a landmark American revision of the English Revised Version (1881–1885), itself a scholarly update of the King James Version of 1611.** Officially titled the *Revised Version, Standard American Edition*, it quickly became known simply as the American Standard Version. Produced by a committee of American scholars under the leadership of Philip Schaff, the ASV is noted for its formal, literal approach, its use of the then-newest critical Greek texts for the New Testament, and its consistent rendering of the divine name as “Jehovah.” Though its archaic English limited its popular longevity, it became the foundation for several major twentieth-century translations and remains valued for its precision.

What follows traces its origins in the Anglo-American revision project, its distinctive features, publication, and enduring influence.

## Origins in the Anglo-American Revision

In 1870 the Convocation of Canterbury in England authorized a revision of the Authorized (King James) Version. American scholars were invited to participate. In December 1871 an American Revision Committee was organized under the chairmanship of the church historian Philip Schaff; work began in October 1872. The American committee included representatives from Baptist, Congregationalist, Dutch Reformed, Friends, Methodist, Episcopal, Presbyterian, and Unitarian traditions—approximately thirty scholars in all.

The British and American teams worked cooperatively but not identically. American suggestions were considered by the British revisers; only those approved by a two-thirds majority of the British committee were incorporated into the main text of the English Revised Version (RV). Remaining American preferences were relegated to an appendix. By agreement, the Americans deferred independent publication of their preferred text for fourteen (later effectively fifteen) years after the British RV appeared, so that the English edition could establish itself.

The RV New Testament was published in 1881 and the Old Testament in 1885 (Apocrypha 1894). After the waiting period expired, the American committee published its own edition.

## Publication of the American Standard Version

The complete *Revised Version, Standard American Edition* was published in 1901 by Thomas Nelson & Sons. A New Testament edition had appeared the previous year. The text incorporated a larger number of the American preferences that had not been accepted into the British RV, along with other adjustments reflecting American English usage and scholarly judgment.

By the time the copyright was renewed in 1929, the version was universally known as the American Standard Version. In 1928 the copyright passed to the International Council of Religious Education (a forerunner of the National Council of Churches), which later authorized the Revised Standard Version as a further revision of the ASV.

## Textual Basis and Distinctive Features

For the Old Testament the ASV followed the Masoretic Text, with limited attention to the Septuagint and other ancient versions. For the New Testament it relied primarily on the critical Greek texts of Westcott and Hort (1881) and Tregelles, rather than the Textus Receptus that underlay the King James Version. This was one of the earliest widely circulated English Bibles to adopt the newer critical text in a thoroughgoing way.

Among its most noticeable features:

- Consistent use of “Jehovah” for the Tetragrammaton (YHWH) throughout the Old Testament, rather than the traditional “LORD” (small capitals). This choice made the ASV especially attractive to groups that preferred the divine name rendered as a proper name.
- Replacement of “which” with “who” or “that” when referring to persons, and “Holy Spirit” in place of “Holy Ghost.”
- A generally more literal, sometimes stiff, English style that closely followed the word order and grammatical structure of the original languages—leading some later critics to describe it as “strong in Greek, weak in English.”
- Retention of many archaic pronouns and verb endings (“thee,” “thou,” “-est,” “-eth”), which soon dated the translation for ordinary readers.

## Reception and Influence

The ASV was well received in American seminaries and among scholars who valued its accuracy and its break with the Textus Receptus tradition. It never displaced the King James Version in popular or liturgical use, partly because of its formal, sometimes awkward English. Nevertheless, it became a standard reference text and the direct ancestor of several important later versions:

- The **Revised Standard Version** (NT 1946, complete 1952), authorized as a revision of the ASV.
- The **New American Standard Bible** (1971 and subsequent updates), which sought to update the ASV’s literal approach into more contemporary English.
- The **Amplified Bible** (1965), which took the ASV as its base and expanded word meanings in brackets.
- Indirectly, through the RSV, the **New Revised Standard Version**, the **English Standard Version**, and others.
- It also served as the starting point for Kenneth Taylor’s paraphrase *The Living Bible* (1971).

Because the copyright eventually expired, the ASV entered the public domain and remains freely available in digital form. Occasional modern “refreshed” or lightly updated editions have appeared that seek to retain its wording while removing the most archaic pronouns and verb forms.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The American Standard Version occupies a pivotal place in the English Bible tradition. It stands as the American counterpart to the English Revised Version—the first major, officially sponsored revision of the King James Bible that systematically incorporated the results of nineteenth-century textual criticism. By adopting a more literal style and a critical Greek text, it helped shift scholarly and eventually popular English Bibles away from exclusive reliance on the Textus Receptus. At the same time, its formal English and distinctive rendering of the divine name mark it as a product of its era.

Though largely superseded for everyday reading by its twentieth-century descendants, the ASV remains a monument of careful, collaborative scholarship and a vital link between the King James tradition and the modern formal-equivalence translations that continue to serve the church.
