# The King James Tradition: The King James Version and Its Direct Descendants

**The King James Version of 1611 is the fountainhead of a translation lineage that continues directly into three later works profiled together here — Webster's Bible (1833), the New King James Version (1982), and the American King James Version (1999) — each a different kind of attempt to keep the same underlying text alive for a new generation of readers, ranging from a full scholarly re-examination of the manuscript evidence to a single translator's light modernization of spelling and vocabulary.**

## Before 1611: The Road to the King James Version

The King James Version was not a wholly new creation but a careful revision drawing heavily on nearly a century of prior English translation, produced by approximately fifty scholars working under rules set at the Hampton Court Conference of 1604. Roughly 80-90% of its New Testament wording, and a substantial portion of its Old Testament, traces ultimately to William Tyndale.

**John Wycliffe** (c. 1320s-1384) oversaw the first complete English Bible (c. 1382-1388), translated from the Latin Vulgate and circulated only in manuscript among the Lollards; the Church later declared Wycliffe a heretic, exhumed and burned his bones in 1428, and made possession of his Bible punishable by death.

**William Tyndale** (c. 1494-1536), working directly from the Greek and Hebrew, produced the New Testament (1526) and part of the Old Testament that would supply most of the KJV's eventual wording. Betrayed and imprisoned near Brussels, he was strangled and burned in 1536; his reported last prayer was "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."

**Miles Coverdale** (1488-1569) produced the first complete printed English Bible (1535), relying on Tyndale where available and on Luther's German and the Vulgate elsewhere. **John Rogers**, publishing under the pseudonym "Thomas Matthew," combined Tyndale's and Coverdale's work into Matthew's Bible (1537) and became the first Protestant martyr burned under Mary I, in 1555.

The **Great Bible** (1539), Coverdale's revision of Matthew's Bible commissioned under Thomas Cromwell, became the official Bible of Henry VIII's church — a king whose own break with Rome, driven by his need for an annulment from Catherine of Aragon, had opened the political door that earlier reformers had died trying to force. The **Geneva Bible** (1560), produced by English Protestant exiles under William Whittingham, became the household Bible of English Protestants and the Pilgrims, but its strongly Reformed marginal notes troubled the English crown. The **Bishops' Bible** (1568), commissioned by Archbishop Matthew Parker specifically to counter Geneva's popularity, supplied the official base text for the King James translators.

## The King James Version (1611)

At the 1604 Hampton Court Conference, King James I authorized a new translation but on his own terms: he despised the Geneva Bible's marginal notes as "partial, untrue, seditious," particularly objecting to annotations that seemed to justify disobedience to kings. Rule 6 for the translators forbade marginal notes except to explain a Hebrew or Greek word — the KJV was deliberately a text without the interpretive apparatus that had made Geneva a "people's Bible" with political edge. (Historical sources also record James's intense attachments to male favorites and contemporary accusations of sodomy; whether or how these matters affected the translation is speculative, since the explicit, documented motive for excluding Geneva-style notes was the defense of monarchical and episcopal authority, not a stated personal agenda.)

The translators were instructed to follow the Bishops' Bible "as little altered as the truth of the original will permit," consulting Tyndale, Coverdale, Matthew's, the Great Bible, and Geneva wherever these agreed better with the Hebrew and Greek.

The 1611 text was not static. Cambridge issued corrective editions in 1629 and 1638 (the latter involving two scholars who had served on the original 1611 committees); Cambridge's 1762 edition (Dr. F. S. Parris) and Oxford's 1769 edition (Dr. Benjamin Blayney) corrected italicization and modernized spelling and punctuation. The 1769 Oxford text became, by wide consensus, the standard King James Version still printed today. None of these editions introduced a different underlying Hebrew or Greek text or constituted a new act of translation — they are properly understood as successive standardizations of one continuous 1611 translation, unlike the three genuinely distinct revision projects that follow.

## Webster's Bible (1833): The First American Revision

**Noah Webster** (1758-1843) — the lexicographer behind *An American Dictionary of the English Language* — undertook the first significant English Bible revision after 1611, published in New Haven as *The Holy Bible... with Amendments of the Language*. Webster's own stated purpose was narrow: update obsolete or "indelicate" words, correct minor grammatical irregularities, and adapt spelling for American readers, while leaving the KJV's "general character of style" untouched. Typical changes were modest — "why" for "wherefore," "Holy Spirit" for "Holy Ghost," softened renderings of words he judged coarse. The underlying text remained the KJV's own Masoretic Text and Textus Receptus. Sales were limited; traditionalists preferred the unaltered KJV, and those wanting real modernization found Webster's changes too slight. The edition's linguistic judgments nonetheless quietly influenced the American scholars who later produced the American Standard Version (1901) — connecting this branch of the King James tradition to the ASV lineage covered in a separate entry in this section.

## The New King James Version (1982): A Full Scholarly Update

By the 1970s the KJV's archaic vocabulary and pronouns had become a genuine barrier for many readers, while a market of Christians still valued its literary heritage and doctrinal reputation over the newer dynamic-equivalence translations. Thomas Nelson Publishers convened roughly 130 scholars, pastors, and laypeople in 1975 under **Dr. Arthur L. Farstad** (Executive Editor) and **Dr. James D. Price** (Old Testament committee chair) to produce a "complete equivalence" update — modernizing archaic pronouns and vocabulary while retaining as much of the KJV's sentence structure, rhythm, and theological vocabulary as modern English would allow. The New Testament appeared in 1979, Psalms in 1980, and the complete Bible in 1982.

For the Old Testament the translators used the Stuttgart *Biblia Hebraica* (Leningrad Codex), the same Hebrew tradition underlying the original KJV; for the New Testament they deliberately retained the *Textus Receptus* for continuity with the Authorized Version, even though Farstad himself preferred the broader Byzantine Majority Text — a preference reflected in the NKJV's own footnotes, which mark significant differences among the Textus Receptus (TR) reading in the main text, the Nestle-Aland/UBS critical text (NU), and the Majority Text (M) without declaring any one "best." The NKJV drew criticism from both directions — strict King-James-Only advocates who objected to the critical-text footnotes at all, and critical-text scholars who felt the TR itself was outdated — but became one of the most widely used formal-equivalence translations in conservative evangelical and independent circles.

## The American King James Version (1999): A Public-Domain Modernization

Far more modest in scope, the AKJV is the work of a single primary translator, **Michael Peter (Stone) Engelbrite**, assisted by a small circle of helpers, released into the public domain on 8 November 1999. Unlike the NKJV's large committee and its engagement with the critical-text/Majority-Text debate, the AKJV makes no attempt to revise the underlying textual base at all — it follows the traditional KJV readings throughout, updating only archaic spelling and vocabulary ("shew" to "show," and similar) while deliberately leaving the grammar and sentence structure unchanged, in Engelbrite's own words, "to prevent any alteration of the doctrine." Its chief legacy has been practical rather than scholarly: a freely distributable, lightly modernized King James text, widely incorporated into digital Bible software, without the institutional review or doctrinal engagement that shaped the NKJV.

## Comparing the Three Descendants

The three post-1611 revisions profiled here differ chiefly in how much they were willing to change, and on what authority. Webster's (1833) made the lightest touch of the three, adjusting only vocabulary judged obsolete or indelicate. The AKJV (1999) is comparably light in scope but a century and a half later, produced by one individual rather than any institution, with an explicit goal of leaving grammar untouched. The NKJV (1982) alone represents a full scholarly re-examination — a large interdenominational committee, an explicit textual-basis debate carried out in its own footnotes, and a level of institutional review the other two never received.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

Across four centuries, this family traces a single throughline: the conviction that the same essential text — Tyndale's language, the Masoretic Old Testament, the Textus Receptus New Testament — could and should be kept intelligible for each new generation without abandoning it for something else entirely. Webster's, the NKJV, and the AKJV each answer the question of how much updating is enough differently, but none of them severs the line running back through the 1611 translators to Tyndale's prison cell and Wycliffe's exhumed bones. It is a lineage defined as much by what each reviser chose to preserve as by what they changed.
