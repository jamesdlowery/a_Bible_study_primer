# History of the Christian Standard Bible

**The Christian Standard Bible (CSB) is a modern English translation that seeks to combine careful fidelity to the original Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek texts with clear, natural readability for contemporary readers.** Published in full in 2017 by Holman Bible Publishers, it is a thorough revision of the earlier Holman Christian Standard Bible (HCSB). Its stated aim is “optimal equivalence”—a deliberate balance between formal (word-for-word) and dynamic (thought-for-thought) approaches—so that the text remains both accurate for study and accessible for preaching, teaching, and personal reading.

What follows traces its origins in the HCSB project, the revision process that produced the CSB, its textual foundations, distinctive features, and place among recent English translations.

## Origins: The Holman Christian Standard Bible

The roots of the CSB lie in work begun in the 1980s by Arthur Farstad, general editor of the New King James Version. Farstad envisioned a fresh translation that would draw on the best available original-language texts while remaining readable and theologically conservative. In 1998 an agreement was reached with LifeWay Christian Resources (the publishing arm of the Southern Baptist Convention) through its Holman Bible Publishers imprint to fund and publish the project.

Farstad died shortly thereafter, and editorial leadership passed to Dr. Edwin Blum. An international team of more than one hundred scholars, editors, stylists, and proofreaders from seventeen denominations—all affirming the inerrancy of Scripture—completed the work. The New Testament appeared in 1999/2001, and the full Holman Christian Standard Bible was published in 2004. A second edition followed in 2009–2010.

The HCSB employed an “optimal equivalence” philosophy and used the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* for the Old Testament and the Nestle-Aland critical Greek text for the New Testament. Distinctive features included a relatively frequent rendering of the divine name (the Tetragrammaton) as “Yahweh” and occasional use of “Messiah” for *Christos* when the context was considered especially Jewish.

## The Revision Process: From HCSB to CSB

After more than a decade of use, Holman Bible Publishers determined that the HCSB could be strengthened further in both accuracy and clarity. A Translation Oversight Committee, co-chaired by scholars such as Thomas Schreiner and David Allen and comprising roughly twenty evangelical scholars from Baptist, Presbyterian, Anglican, Lutheran, and other backgrounds, undertook a comprehensive review. Input was solicited from pastors, seminaries, and readers.

Work on the revision was completed in June 2016. The updated text was published in March 2017 under the simplified name **Christian Standard Bible**, dropping the word “Holman.” The change of name reflected a desire for broader recognition beyond the publisher’s brand while retaining continuity with the earlier work.

## Key Changes in the CSB

Several notable adjustments distinguish the CSB from the HCSB:

- **The divine name**: The HCSB’s selective use of “Yahweh” (hundreds of occurrences) was largely abandoned in favor of the traditional English rendering “LORD” (small capitals), bringing the CSB into line with most other English translations and removing an obstacle some readers found unfamiliar or distracting.
- **Christ / Messiah**: The HCSB had sometimes rendered *Christos* as “Messiah” in Jewish contexts. The CSB returns to the more consistent traditional rendering “Christ.”
- “**Slave**” **and** “**servant**”: In many places the HCSB’s “slave” was changed to “servant” (*doulos*), reflecting concern that modern American connotations of race-based slavery could obscure the theological meaning.
- **Pronouns referring to God**: Capitalization of divine pronouns (He, Him, His when referring to God) was discontinued, following the practice of the original languages and of most historic English translations, including the King James Version.
- Numerous smaller refinements in wording, style, and consistency were made throughout the text on the basis of advances in scholarship and reader feedback.

A further revision of the CSB appeared around 2020, incorporating additional refinements.

## Textual Basis and Translation Philosophy

For the Old Testament the CSB uses the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (primarily the 4th or 5th edition). For the New Testament it follows the Nestle-Aland *Novum Testamentum Graece*, 28th edition, and the United Bible Societies’ *Greek New Testament*, 5th corrected edition. Where significant textual variants exist, the translators generally follow what they judge to be the original reading and note major alternatives in footnotes.

The guiding philosophy remains optimal equivalence: the translators aim for linguistic precision to the source texts while ensuring that the English is clear and natural for modern readers. Where a strictly literal rendering would obscure the meaning, a more dynamic phrasing is chosen; where the original is already clear, a more formal rendering is preferred. The result is a translation widely regarded as balancing accuracy and readability particularly well.

## Reception and Use

The CSB has been well received in evangelical circles, especially among Southern Baptists and other conservative denominations. It is used for preaching, teaching, personal study, and church ministry. Endorsements have come from a range of pastors and scholars who appreciate its combination of scholarly care and accessible English. Because it is published by a major denominational publisher, it has also been integrated into a wide array of study Bibles, children’s editions, and ministry resources.

Like every translation, it has its critics. Some prefer a more consistently formal approach; others favor translations that retain distinctive features of the HCSB (such as “Yahweh”). Yet the CSB has established itself as a mainstream, reliable option among recent English Bibles.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The Christian Standard Bible continues the long effort to render the Scriptures faithfully and intelligibly for each generation of English readers. Rooted in the scholarly and confessional commitments of its HCSB predecessor, refined through careful revision, and grounded in the best available critical texts, it seeks to serve both the church’s need for accurate study and the believer’s need for a Bible that can be read with understanding and joy.

In an age of many translations, the CSB stands as a thoughtful attempt to hold accuracy and clarity together without unnecessary novelty or undue concession to passing cultural pressures.
