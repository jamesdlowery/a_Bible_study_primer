# History of the American King James Version

**The American King James Version (AKJV) is a modest but deliberate effort to bring the language of the classic King James Bible into clearer contemporary English while remaining as close as possible to the traditional text.** Unlike the large committee projects that produced the New King James Version or other modern translations, the AKJV is the work of a single primary translator, Michael Peter (Stone) Engelbrite, assisted by a small circle of helpers. It was released into the public domain on 8 November 1999 and has since circulated freely in digital and print forms.

What follows is an account of its origin, method, textual character, and place among the many revisions of the Authorized Version.

## Origins and Purpose

By the late twentieth century the King James Version, last substantially standardized in the 1769 Oxford (Blayney) edition, continued to hold a place of honor among many English-speaking Christians. Its literary beauty and perceived doctrinal stability remained highly valued. At the same time, its archaic vocabulary and spelling created a genuine barrier for ordinary readers, especially younger ones and those for whom English was a second language.

Michael Peter (Stone) Engelbrite set out to address this difficulty in a restrained way. His goal was not to produce a new translation from the Hebrew and Greek, nor to follow the more thorough linguistic updating of the New King James Version (1982). Instead, he aimed at a simple, word-for-word modernization of the existing King James text: replace outdated spelling and vocabulary with modern equivalents while leaving the grammar, sentence structure, and doctrinal content untouched. In his own words, care was taken “to change nothing doctrinally, but to simply update the spelling and vocabulary.” The grammar was deliberately left unchanged “to prevent any alteration of the doctrine.”

The project was completed and placed in the public domain on 8 November 1999. Special acknowledgment was given to Tye Rausch and Eve Engelbrite for their substantial help.

## Method of Revision

The AKJV is best described as a conservative, mechanical modernization rather than a scholarly retranslation.

- Archaic spellings (e.g., “shew,” “spake,” “believeth”) are updated to modern forms.
- Obsolete or obscure vocabulary is replaced with clearer contemporary words of equivalent meaning.
- Pronouns and verb endings that no longer function in ordinary English are adjusted where necessary for readability, yet the overall syntactic framework of the KJV is retained.
- No attempt is made to revise the underlying Hebrew or Greek textual base; the AKJV follows the traditional King James readings throughout.
- Italicized words that indicate additions for English sense in the original KJV tradition are generally handled consistently with that practice.

Because the changes are primarily lexical and orthographic, the AKJV remains extremely close to the classic text. Readers familiar with the King James Version will recognize almost every verse at once, yet will find fewer obstacles of obsolete language.

## Publication and Availability

Unlike the commercially published NKJV, the AKJV was released without copyright restriction. Engelbrite explicitly stated that anyone may copy, sell, modify, or distribute it freely, provided they do not claim original authorship of the work itself.

As a result, the text has been widely distributed in digital Bible software modules (including The SWORD Project and e-Sword formats), online reading sites, and various print-on-demand editions. Parallel editions pairing the AKJV with the 1611 KJV or other historic versions have also appeared. Later printings, including full-Bible editions and New Testament–Psalms–Proverbs volumes, have continued into the 2020s under Engelbrite’s name or associated ministries.

## Character and Reception

The AKJV occupies a distinctive niche among modernizations of the King James tradition. It is more conservative than the New King James Version in the extent of its changes and in its refusal to introduce marginal notes from the critical or Majority texts. At the same time, it is more readable than an unrevised 1769 or Pure Cambridge text for many contemporary readers.

Because it is the product of an individual rather than a large academic committee, it has not attracted the same level of scholarly scrutiny or institutional endorsement as the NKJV. Some users appreciate its straightforward approach and public-domain status; others prefer the more thoroughly reviewed language of the NKJV or continue to use traditional KJV editions. Its primary contribution has been practical: providing a free, lightly modernized King James text that removes many of the most obvious linguistic barriers while remaining recognizably within the historic Authorized Version tradition.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The American King James Version stands in the long line of efforts to keep the English Bible both faithful and intelligible. From Tyndale’s pioneering work, through the successive revisions that culminated in the 1611 Authorized Version, the Cambridge and Oxford standardizations of the eighteenth century, and the twentieth-century updates such as the NKJV, the impulse has remained the same: to let the Word of God speak clearly to each generation without needlessly abandoning the language that has already shaped the faith of millions.

In its modest scope and public-domain generosity, the AKJV continues that impulse in a quiet, practical way.
