# History of the King James Version

**The history of the King James Version (Authorized Version) of 1611 stands as a culminating labor in a long chain of English Bible translation, built upon the sacrificial foundations laid by earlier reformers and scholars.** It was not a wholly new creation but a careful revision drawing heavily from its predecessors, produced by approximately fifty learned men divided into companies, working under rules set at the Hampton Court Conference of 1604. The translators were instructed to follow the Bishops’ Bible as their base text “as little altered as the truth of the original will permit,” while consulting Tyndale, Coverdale, Matthew’s, the Great Bible (Whitchurch), and the Geneva Bible where they agreed better with the Hebrew and Greek. Roughly 80–90% of the New Testament wording, and a substantial portion of the Old, traces back ultimately to William Tyndale.

What follows is an account of that lineage, the revisions, the costs borne by those who labored, and the political and personal controversies that shaped the process.

## The Wycliffe Bible (c. 1382–1388)

John Wycliffe (c. 1320s–1384), often called the “Morning Star of the Reformation,” oversaw the first complete English Bible. Working from the Latin Vulgate (not the original languages), he and associates such as Nicholas of Hereford produced an initial version around 1382; John Purvey and others revised it into a more readable form by about 1388. These were manuscript copies only, circulated among the Lollards.

Wycliffe faced repeated trials, papal bulls condemning his teachings as heretical, and forced retirement from Oxford. After his death from a stroke in 1384, the Church intensified persecution of his followers. The Constitutions of Oxford (1408) forbade unauthorized English translations under pain of excommunication. In 1415 the Council of Constance declared Wycliffe a heretic; in 1428 his bones were exhumed, burned, and the ashes scattered in the River Swift. Possession of a Wycliffe Bible could mean imprisonment or death for Lollards. Yet the work endured in hundreds of surviving manuscripts and prepared the ground for the printed English Bible.

## William Tyndale’s Bible (New Testament 1526; portions of the Old Testament)

William Tyndale (c. 1494–1536) is rightly regarded as the father of the English Bible. Working from the Greek (Erasmus) and Hebrew, he produced a New Testament of remarkable clarity and force, printed at Worms in 1526 and smuggled into England. He completed the Pentateuch, Joshua–2 Chronicles, and Jonah before his arrest. Estimates suggest 80–90% of the KJV New Testament and a large share of the Old Testament sections he finished derive from his wording (“Let there be light,” “the powers that be,” “filthy lucre,” and countless other phrases).

Tyndale lived as a fugitive on the Continent after English authorities refused permission to translate. His work was publicly burned; associates such as John Frith were executed. Betrayed by Henry Phillips in Antwerp, he was imprisoned for over a year in Vilvoorde Castle near Brussels, tried for heresy, degraded from the priesthood, strangled, and his body burned in October 1536. His reported last prayer was “Lord, open the King of England’s eyes.” That prayer was answered within years.

## Coverdale Bible (1535)

Miles Coverdale (1488–1569), once Tyndale’s assistant, produced the first complete printed English Bible. He relied on Tyndale where available, supplemented by Luther’s German, the Vulgate, and other sources (he was not a strong Hebraist). Dedicated tactfully to Henry VIII, it contained no controversial notes. Coverdale’s Psalter later shaped the Book of Common Prayer.

Coverdale fled England multiple times for his Protestant preaching. He assisted Tyndale, returned under Cromwell’s protection, revised for the Great Bible, and was made Bishop of Exeter under Edward VI. Under Mary I he was deprived, briefly under house arrest, and escaped execution only through the intercession of the King of Denmark; he endured a third exile in Geneva, where he assisted with the Geneva Bible, before returning under Elizabeth.

## Matthew’s Bible (1537)

Published under the pseudonym “Thomas Matthew,” this was the work of John Rogers (c. 1505–1555), Tyndale’s friend and literary executor. It combined Tyndale’s New Testament and unfinished Old Testament work with Coverdale’s completion, added extensive Protestant notes and apparatus, and received Henry VIII’s license—the first English Bible to do so.

Rogers became the first Protestant martyr under Mary I. Arrested, examined by Stephen Gardiner, and refused a pardon if he would recant, he was burned at Smithfield on 4 February 1555. His wife and children (including a nursing infant) were present; he went to the stake reciting Psalm 51 and washed his hands in the flames.

## The Great Bible (1539)

Commissioned under Thomas Cromwell’s 1538 injunctions requiring a large English Bible in every parish church, this was Coverdale’s revision of Matthew’s Bible (itself largely Tyndale). Printed in Paris then London, it was a massive “pulpit” volume. A second edition (1540) carried Cranmer’s preface. It became the official Bible of Henry’s church and supplied the Psalter still used in the Book of Common Prayer for centuries. Its text was a direct ancestor of the Bishops’ and, through them, the KJV.

## Henry VIII’s Annulment/Divorce and Influence on the Translations

The relevant monarch is Henry VIII (r. 1509–1547). His desire for an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon (to marry Anne Boleyn and secure a male heir) led to the break with Rome. When Pope Clement VII refused, Henry had himself declared Supreme Head of the Church of England (Act of Supremacy, 1534). This political and personal crisis created the opening for an authorized English Bible.

Henry remained theologically conservative in many respects (the Six Articles of 1539 reaffirmed transubstantiation and clerical celibacy), yet he authorized Coverdale’s work, licensed Matthew’s Bible, and ordered the Great Bible into churches. Scripture itself (especially Leviticus 20:21) was pressed into service to justify the annulment on the grounds of affinity. The royal need for independence from papal authority thus accelerated the availability of Scripture in English, even while Henry continued to burn some “heretics.” The translators of his day worked under this uneasy patronage: the king’s marital and dynastic interests opened the door that earlier reformers had died trying to force.

## The Geneva Bible (1560)

Produced by English Protestant exiles in Calvin’s Geneva during the Marian persecutions (Mary I, 1553–1558), the Geneva Bible was the first English version translated entirely from the original languages as a unified scholarly project. William Whittingham led the work (with Anthony Gilby and others; Coverdale assisted). It introduced verse divisions, Roman type, and extensive marginal notes that were strongly Reformed, anti-papal, and occasionally political—commenting, for example, that the Hebrew midwives’ disobedience to Pharaoh was lawful (Exodus 1:19).

It became the household Bible of English Protestants, Shakespeare, the Puritans, and the Pilgrims, going through scores of editions. Its notes, however, troubled monarchs.

## The Bishops’ Bible (1568; revised 1572; 1602 edition)

Commissioned by Archbishop Matthew Parker to counter the popularity and Calvinist notes of the Geneva Bible, this revision of the Great Bible was produced largely by bishops. It was authorized for church use but never rivaled the Geneva in private popularity; its language was often stiffer. The 1602 edition was the official base text handed to the KJV companies.

## King James, the Marginal Notes, and the Controversies Surrounding Him

At the Hampton Court Conference (1604), Puritan leader John Rainolds proposed a new translation. James I agreed, but on terms that reflected his own concerns. He despised the Geneva Bible’s notes as “partial, untrue, seditious, and savouring too much of dangerous and traitorous conceits.” He particularly objected to annotations that appeared to justify disobedience to kings (e.g., the midwives in Exodus) or that challenged the divine right of kings—a doctrine James vigorously defended in works such as *The True Law of Free Monarchies* and *Basilikon Doron*. Rule 6 for the translators therefore forbade marginal notes except for the explanation of Hebrew or Greek words. The KJV was deliberately a text without the interpretive apparatus that had made the Geneva a “people’s Bible” with political edge.

Regarding James’s personal life and “spiritual shortcomings”: historical sources record his intense emotional attachments to male favorites (notably Robert Carr, Earl of Somerset, and George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham), public displays of affection, and contemporary criticism that included accusations of sodomy or unnatural vice. Some modern scholars find the evidence for homosexual relationships compelling; others regard the relationships as intense but not necessarily sexual, or note the difficulty of applying modern categories to the period. James publicly condemned sodomy as a grave sin while privately conducting himself in ways that invited scandal. Whether or to what degree these matters influenced the translation itself is speculative; the explicit, documented motive for excluding Geneva-style notes was political and ecclesiastical—the defense of monarchical and episcopal authority—rather than a personal theological agenda on sexual ethics. The translators worked under rules that prioritized a majestic, ecclesiastical English text free of partisan commentary.

## Subsequent Standardization: 1629–1873

The 1611 King James Version was not a static, one-time production; it went through several rounds of standardization over the following two and a half centuries, none of which involved new translation work or a change of underlying textual authority, but which together account for the differences between the original 1611 printing and the text found in virtually every King James Bible printed today.

Early printings of the 1611 KJV, produced under licenses split among several printers (Robert Barker, and later Bonham Norton and John Bill), accumulated a substantial number of typographical errors as competing print shops rushed editions to market. Cambridge, printing under its 1534 royal charter, issued the first serious corrective edition in 1629, removing many misprints and minor translational inconsistencies -- Oxford did not begin printing Bibles of its own until 1675, decades later, so this earliest corrective phase was a Cambridge undertaking alone. A further Cambridge revision followed in 1638, this one notable for the direct involvement of John Bois and Samuel Ward, two scholars who had actually served on the original 1611 translation committees, lending it particular authority in restoring the intended text.

Printing errors nonetheless continued to accumulate over the following century. Two further standardizations, prepared independently by the two university presses, proved decisive. In 1762 Cambridge published an edition overseen by Dr. F. S. Parris, which corrected italicized words (used throughout the KJV to mark words supplied by the translators with no direct equivalent in the Hebrew or Greek, but inconsistently marked in earlier printings), added marginal cross-references, and modernized spelling and punctuation that had grown increasingly archaic after a century and a half of linguistic change. Seven years later, in 1769, Oxford issued a rival standardization prepared by Dr. Benjamin Blayney, who is recorded as having cross-checked the original 1611 text, the 1701 Oxford (Bishop Lloyd) edition descending from the 1638 Cambridge revision, and Parris's 1762 Cambridge edition. Blayney's edition further expanded and corrected the italicization of supplied words (considerably more heavily italicized than the 1611 original), corrected a number of minor wording slips accumulated through generations of printing (for example, Matthew 13:6's "had not root" became "had no root"), and thoroughly modernized spelling, capitalization, and punctuation into forms recognizable to modern readers.

The 1769 Oxford edition became, by wide consensus, the standard form of the King James Version from that point forward -- it was the text carried to America during the Revolutionary era, printed throughout the Victorian period, and remains, in all but a handful of minor details, the text found in the great majority of King James Bibles sold and read today. A further, more narrowly scholarly standardization was undertaken by Dr. F. H. A. Scrivener between 1866 and 1873, producing the Cambridge Paragraph Bible of 1873; though highly regarded for its meticulous accuracy in reconstructing the translators' original intent, it has generally served as a reference standard for textual scholars rather than displacing the 1769 Oxford text in ordinary use.

Across all of these standardizations, the essential character of the 1611 translation was preserved. None introduced a different underlying Hebrew or Greek text, and none constituted a new act of translation; the changes were confined to correcting printing errors, adjusting italicization, and modernizing spelling and punctuation to keep pace with the ordinary development of the English language. For this reason, the 1629, 1638, 1762, 1769, and 1873 editions are all properly described simply as the King James Version -- successive standardizations of a single, continuous translation rather than a series of distinct revisions in the manner of the Webster's Bible, the Revised Version, or the New King James Version that would later follow it.

## The Fruit of the Labor

The King James Version of 1611 thus stands as the polished synthesis of nearly a century of translation, revision, blood, exile, and royal politics. Wycliffe’s vision, Tyndale’s linguistic genius and martyrdom, Coverdale’s completion and endurance, Rogers’s compilation and death at the stake, the official Great Bible, the scholarly and annotated Geneva, and the churchly Bishops’ Bible all contributed. Human sin, ambition, persecution, and compromise marked the process; yet through it the Word of God was made available in the common tongue with a dignity and power that has shaped the English-speaking world for four centuries. As the translators themselves wrote in their preface, their aim was not to make a new translation, but “to make a good one better.” The sacrifices of those who went before them made that betterment possible.
