# History of the New Living Translation

**The New Living Translation (NLT) is a dynamic-equivalence English Bible first published in 1996 by Tyndale House Publishers.** It grew out of the immensely popular *Living Bible* paraphrase created by Kenneth N. Taylor, yet it is a fully independent translation produced by a large committee of scholars working directly from the Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek texts. Designed for clarity and readability in contemporary English, the NLT has become one of the most widely used modern translations, particularly valued for personal reading, teaching, and outreach.

What follows traces its roots in the Living Bible, the development of the scholarly translation, subsequent revisions, and its place among contemporary English Bibles.

## Roots in the Living Bible

Kenneth N. Taylor (1917–2005), founder of Tyndale House Publishers, began paraphrasing Scripture in the 1950s so that his children could better understand the family Bible readings from the King James Version. Working on the Chicago commuter train and in his spare time, he produced *Living Letters* (the New Testament epistles) in 1962. After commercial publishers declined the manuscript, Taylor published it himself under the newly formed Tyndale House, named after the Reformation translator William Tyndale.

Subsequent portions followed, and the complete *Living Bible* appeared in 1971. Its warm, accessible style and the endorsement of figures such as Billy Graham led to extraordinary success—tens of millions of copies sold. The Living Bible was a paraphrase based primarily on the American Standard Version, not a translation from the original languages. While it opened Scripture to countless new readers, scholars and many pastors noted its interpretive freedom and occasional inaccuracies.

## From Revision to New Translation

In the late 1980s Tyndale House initiated a project to revise the Living Bible for greater accuracy. Ninety evangelical scholars—experts in the biblical languages and in the books they were assigned—were enlisted, along with English stylists. Mark Taylor (Kenneth’s son) served as Chief Stylist and a member of the central Bible Translation Committee.

As the work progressed, the committee realized they were producing far more than a revision. They were creating an entirely new translation from the original texts that retained the Living Bible’s clarity and readability while grounding every rendering in careful scholarship. The project was renamed the New Living Translation. The complete NLT was published in July 1996, twenty-five years after the Living Bible.

## Translation Philosophy and Textual Basis

The NLT follows a dynamic-equivalence (or functional-equivalence) approach. The translators sought to convey the meaning of the original texts in clear, natural, contemporary English, aiming for a reading level accessible to a broad audience (approximately sixth grade). Where a more literal rendering would obscure the sense for modern readers, they favored clarity of meaning; where a formal rendering was both accurate and readable, they retained it.

For the Old Testament the primary base was the Masoretic Text as printed in *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, with consultation of the Dead Sea Scrolls, Septuagint, and other ancient versions. For the New Testament the translators used the standard critical editions (UBS Greek New Testament and Nestle-Aland).

## Subsequent Editions

- **2004 Second Edition (NLTse)**: A thorough review increased precision while preserving readability. Many consider this the mature form of the translation.
- **2007, 2013, and 2015**: Further rounds of mostly minor refinements, including clarifications for international English readers and adjustments such as emphasizing personhood in references to people with leprosy.
- A Catholic Edition incorporating the Deuterocanonical books received ecclesiastical approval in India and influenced later standard editions.

The NLT text continues under the stewardship of the Tyndale House Foundation’s Bible Translation Committee.

## Reception and Influence

The NLT quickly gained wide acceptance among evangelical churches, individuals, and ministries that prioritize clear communication of Scripture. It has consistently ranked among the top-selling English Bibles. Readers praise its natural flow and usefulness for public reading, personal devotion, and introducing the Bible to new believers. Critics who prefer formal-equivalence translations sometimes regard its renderings as too interpretive. Supporters see it as a responsible, committee-produced meaning-based translation that successfully carries forward the Living Bible’s concern for accessibility without remaining a paraphrase.

## Place in the Larger Tradition

The New Living Translation stands in the lineage of efforts—stretching back to William Tyndale—to make the Bible speak clearly in the language of ordinary people. It transforms the insight of a single paraphraser’s work into a scholarly, multi-translator project grounded in the original languages. In an era of many English versions, the NLT occupies a prominent place among those that emphasize readability and contemporary expression while seeking fidelity to the meaning of the biblical text. Its enduring popularity testifies to the continuing need for translations that open the Scriptures to every reader.
