# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 Peter

This document catalogs 4 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 2 Peter, either
entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Romans, 1 Timothy,
Ezekiel, and 2 Timothy documents, and Jude. For each item, this document states the claim as
it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim
appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses 2 Peter's own
authorship -- widely regarded, by explicit scholarly consensus on the question's difficulty
even among defenders of traditional authorship, as the single most disputed authorship
question in the entire New Testament canon, considerably exceeding the disputes addressed
regarding the Pastoral Epistles and Hebrews in their own companion documents. Entry 4 addresses
a genuine, independently acknowledged textual variant at 3:10, where the earth's ultimate fate
is described as either being "burned up" or "found/exposed" depending on which ancient Greek
manuscript tradition is followed -- a variant the respected textual scholar Daniel Wallace has
himself called one of the most difficult textual problems in the New Testament.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 2, and 4 are all presented with genuine scholarly and
theological diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 4 were both checked in detail against multiple sources,
including Carson and Moo's standard evangelical New Testament introduction and specific
manuscript-level textual-critical sources documenting the exact Greek variants and their
attestation. The remaining entries are addressed from standard harmonization approaches,
several of which build on treatments already established in the companion Romans, 1 Timothy,
Ezekiel, and 2 Timothy documents.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly or
theological diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. 2 Peter's authorship -- widely regarded as the single most disputed authorship question in the New Testament canon

**References:** 2 Peter 1:1, 16-18; 2 Peter 3:1; 2 Peter 3:15-16

**The claim:** 2 Peter explicitly claims apostolic authorship, opening 'Simeon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ' (1:1) and claiming direct eyewitness testimony to the Transfiguration (1:16-18) and to having written an earlier letter to the same readers (3:1, apparently referring to 1 Peter). Despite these explicit claims, 2 Peter is widely regarded, by a wider range of scholars than for any other New Testament book, as the most difficult case for defending traditional apostolic authorship -- D. A. Carson and Douglas J. Moo state plainly, in a standard evangelical New Testament introduction, that 'for no other letter in the New Testament is there a greater consensus that the person who is named as the author could not, in fact, be the author' (An Introduction to the New Testament, 2nd ed. (Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 2005)).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the entirety of 2 Peter as a continuous text attributed to Peter, without marking any portion as disputed in the text itself; the authorship question addressed here is a matter of historical-critical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is the most substantively and widely debated authorship question in the entire New Testament canon, and this entry presents the case in full detail rather than a single confident answer. The evidence against traditional authorship includes this letter's markedly different Greek style and vocabulary from 1 Peter, considerably more dense and uneven; its unusually late and hesitant attestation among the early church fathers -- Origen already noted disputes over its authenticity in the third century, and the fourth-century historian Eusebius explicitly listed it among the New Testament's 'disputed' books, alongside James, Jude, 2 and 3 John; and its extensive, close literary relationship with the book of Jude. Chapter 2 of 2 Peter overlaps so substantially with Jude in content, structure, and specific Old Testament illustrations that some scholars have suggested it could accurately be called '2 Jude,' with the scholarly consensus holding that 2 Peter drew on Jude, rather than the reverse or both drawing on a common source, as one of its sources. The evidence for traditional authorship includes the letter's own use of the distinctively Palestinian, Aramaic-influenced form 'Simeon Peter,' rather than the more common Greek 'Simon,' the form a later forger might more predictably have used to match 1 Peter's own opening, at 1:1. It also includes its specific, personal claims to eyewitness experience of the Transfiguration and a close, personal, affectionate relationship with Paul, 'our beloved brother Paul' (3:15). Further, ancient pseudonymous letters, as opposed to pseudonymous apocalypses, a different and more common ancient genre, are only rarely attested at all in either Jewish or early Christian literature, making the practice this reading requires less well-established as a recognized, tolerated genre convention than sometimes assumed. And the earliest church, when it did eventually accept 2 Peter into the canon, affirmed by figures including Athanasius and Jerome by the fourth century, after this documented, extended hesitation, did so specifically because it judged the letter's own claim to Petrine authorship credible, having weighed the very stylistic and historical difficulties modern scholars still raise. The church's own historical uncertainty about the letter is not, on this view, independent, decisive evidence against authenticity in itself. This project does not adjudicate this substantial and ongoing scholarly debate, noting only that it remains, by a wide and explicit scholarly consensus on the question's difficulty even among those who ultimately affirm traditional authorship, the single most contested authorship question addressed anywhere in the New Testament canon.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. 'Not wishing that any should perish' -- God's universal saving will, and its relationship to particular election

**References:** 2 Peter 3:9; Romans 9:10-24

**The claim:** 2 Peter 3:9 states, regarding the seeming delay of Christ's return, 'The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance' -- language some read as a clear statement of God's universal saving will toward literally every individual person, connecting to the same broader question addressed at length in the companion Romans document regarding divine election and God's sovereign purposes.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Peter 3:9 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most substantively and sincerely debated theological questions across major Christian traditions, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Romans document, which addresses the genuine, long-running theological division between Reformed/Calvinist and Arminian and other traditions regarding the nature of divine election and the scope of God's saving will, without adjudicating between them. Those holding a more particular view of election generally read this verse's own immediate context ('patient toward you') as addressing God's patience specifically toward the letter's own believing recipients (or, more broadly, toward the elect as a class) rather than making an unqualified universal statement about every individual person without exception -- on this reading, 'any' and 'all' refer to all of God's own chosen people, not to literally every human being regardless of their ultimate response. Those holding a more universal view of God's saving will and desire (compatible with a range of positions on how that will is ultimately realized) read the verse as a straightforward, general statement of God's genuine desire for all people's repentance and salvation, consistent with similar statements elsewhere in Scripture (1 Timothy 2:4, addressed in the companion 1 Timothy document; Ezekiel 18:23, 32, addressed in the companion Ezekiel document, both affirming God's stated preference for the wicked's repentance over their judgment). This project does not adjudicate between these readings, consistent with its treatment of the same underlying question elsewhere in this series.

### 3. 'Carried along by the Holy Spirit' -- a further inspiration text, addressed alongside the companion 2 Timothy document

**References:** 2 Peter 1:20-21; 2 Timothy 3:16

**The claim:** 2 Peter 1:20-21 states, 'no prophecy of Scripture comes from someone's own interpretation. For no prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit' -- a further significant text regarding the doctrine of biblical inspiration, connecting to and complementing 2 Timothy 3:16's statement addressed in its own companion document, but describing the specific mechanism or process of prophetic inspiration rather than the nature or scope of 'Scripture' as a completed collection.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 Peter 1:20-21 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion 2 Timothy document for the fuller discussion of the doctrine of biblical inspiration.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a complementary text addressing a related but distinct aspect of the same broader doctrine addressed in the companion 2 Timothy document. Where 2 Timothy 3:16 makes a claim about the divine origin and resulting profitability of 'Scripture' as a body of texts, 2 Peter 1:20-21 addresses the process by which prophetic Scripture in particular came to be written -- prophets did not originate their own message through personal interpretation or initiative ('no prophecy... comes from someone's own interpretation'), but were 'carried along' (a nautical image, elsewhere used of a ship driven by the wind, suggesting the prophets' own active cooperation and faculties were genuinely engaged even while the Spirit supplied the actual direction and force of what was communicated) by the Holy Spirit's own superintending activity. This is generally read as a complementary, rather than competing, description of the same broader process of divine inspiration addressed in the companion 2 Timothy document, with each text illuminating a different specific dimension of how Scripture's divine and human authorship are understood to work together.

## Numbers and Text

### 4. 'The earth and the works done on it will be burned up' -- or 'will be found' or 'exposed'? A genuine, widely discussed textual variant

**References:** 2 Peter 3:10

**The claim:** 2 Peter 3:10 describes the day of the Lord's arrival, including the statement that 'the earth and the works that are done on it will be' -- and here the underlying Greek manuscripts genuinely diverge, with different textual traditions reading either 'will be burned up' (katakaesetai, the reading behind the KJV's traditional rendering) or 'will be found' or 'exposed' / 'laid bare' (heurethesetai, the reading favored by most modern critical Greek editions and reflected in most contemporary translations). This is independently confirmed as one of the most difficult individual textual problems in the entire New Testament, with the respected textual scholar Daniel Wallace himself directly acknowledging its genuine difficulty.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, LSB, RSV2CE:** Most modern translations, following the earlier manuscripts favored by contemporary textual criticism, render some form of 'will be found,' 'exposed,' 'laid bare,' or 'disclosed,' while KJV and NKJV, following the Byzantine textual tradition, render 'will be burned up' -- a genuine, well-documented textual variant with real interpretive consequences for how this verse describes the earth's ultimate fate, not merely a stylistic translation choice.
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'the earth and the works on it will be disclosed,' also following this reading.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'the earth and the works that are done on it will be exposed,' also following the 'found/exposed' reading.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'the earth also and the works that are therein shall be burned up,' following the Byzantine textual tradition (katakaesetai).
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders 'the earth and the works that are in it will be burned up,' following the same tradition.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'the earth and its works will be found,' following the reading favored by most contemporary critical editions (heurethesetai), with a footnote acknowledging the manuscript variation.
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'the earth and everything done in it will be laid bare,' also following this reading.
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'the earth and everything on it will be found to deserve judgment,' an interpretive expansion of the same underlying reading.

**Reconciliation:** This is a well-documented, genuine textual variant, and this entry presents the manuscript evidence and its interpretive consequences directly. The reading 'will be found' or 'exposed' (heurethesetai) is attested in several of the oldest and most highly regarded manuscripts (including Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus) and is favored by the majority of contemporary textual critics as the more likely original reading, specifically because it is the more difficult and unusual reading (a general principle of textual criticism holding that scribes are more likely to have altered a strange, difficult original reading into a smoother, more expected one -- 'will be burned up' -- than the reverse), and because the several minor variant readings that exist alongside it are more easily explained as later scribal attempts to clarify this same original, more difficult text. On this reading, the passage's meaning shifts from describing the earth's destruction by fire to describing the earth and human deeds being 'laid bare' or exposed before God's judgment -- a meaning some scholars connect to the passage's own broader theological point about human accountability and moral exposure at the final judgment, rather than a claim about the physical mode of the earth's own destruction. The alternative reading, 'will be burned up' (katakaesetai), remains attested in the majority of surviving Greek manuscripts overall (including the Byzantine textual tradition underlying the King James Version) and continues to be defended by some scholars and reflected in several English translations, including some produced after modern textual-critical methods became standard. This project does not adjudicate between these two textual readings, noting that this represents one of the most substantively debated textual questions in the entire New Testament, independently acknowledged as such by textual scholars across a range of theological positions.
