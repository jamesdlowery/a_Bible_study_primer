# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Nehemiah

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Nehemiah, either entirely internal to Nehemiah or between Nehemiah and its close companion
Ezra (the two originally circulated as a single book), plus a smaller number of connections to
Genesis, Deuteronomy, and Ruth. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Nehemiah is closely tied to Ezra and, like that book, is relatively
compact. This document has 7 entries rather than the 12 used for longer books in this series,
consistent with the approach already taken for Ruth and Ezra.

**A note on entry 1:** This is the one entry across this entire series that compares Scripture
to a non-canonical source (the first-century historian Josephus) rather than to another
biblical text. It is included because the comparison is so frequently raised in discussions of
this book, but it is flagged plainly as outside this project's normal scope of internal
biblical harmonization.

**Verification status:** Entry 3 (the Jaddua genealogy and the Sanballat son-in-law episode)
was freshly researched against a wide range of sources -- conservative, Catholic, and
mainstream -- for this document, and is presented with appropriate nuance about what different
scholars conclude. Entries 2 and 5 cross-reference fuller treatments already published in the
companion Ezra, Deuteronomy, and Ruth documents rather than repeating them. The remaining
entries are narrative and legal questions where all ten translations render the same
underlying text and the harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 1. The wall completed in 52 days -- a later account gives a very different timeframe

**References:** Nehemiah 6:15

**The claim:** Nehemiah 6:15 states the wall of Jerusalem, a massive rebuilding project, was completed in just 52 days. The first-century Jewish historian Josephus, writing centuries later (Antiquities 11.5.8), instead states the project took two years and four months -- a starkly different timeframe for what both authors present as the same undertaking.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '52 days' at Nehemiah 6:15 consistently; this figure is not disputed among any of the translations, and the comparison addressed here is with an extra-biblical source, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry differs from every other item in this project's Nehemiah-Ezra series in one respect worth stating plainly: Josephus's Antiquities is not Scripture, and a discrepancy between the Bible and a later, non-canonical historian is not, strictly speaking, an internal biblical contradiction of the kind this project otherwise addresses. It is included here only because the comparison is raised so frequently in discussions of Nehemiah's historical reliability that omitting it would leave a real gap in this document's usefulness as a reference. On the substance: most commentators who address the discrepancy conclude that Josephus, writing centuries after the events and known elsewhere in his history of this period to compress, expand, or otherwise handle chronological details loosely (a pattern also touched on regarding the Sanballat/Jaddua question addressed elsewhere in this document), is the less reliable source at this specific point, particularly given Nehemiah's own account reads as a detailed, first-person memoir with specific, internally consistent dates throughout the surrounding chapters -- a genre and level of contemporaneous detail Josephus's much later secondary history does not match. A rapid, 52-day completion is also not inherently implausible given the scale of prior preparation described in the text (stone, materials, and a workforce already organized by families and sections before construction began, per chapter 3) and the urgency created by active, ongoing opposition (chapters 4 and 6) that gave the workforce strong incentive to finish quickly.

## Numbers and Text

### 2. The returnees' census, appearing again in this book

**References:** Nehemiah 7:6-69; Ezra 2:1-67

**The claim:** Nehemiah 7 records what its own text (7:5) explicitly presents as a copy of the same list of returnees found in Ezra 2 -- yet the two chapters differ in numerous individual figures, and neither chapter's own family-by-family sum matches its own stated grand total of 42,360.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this book's own figures and total exactly as given, without adjusting them to match Ezra 2; see the companion Ezra document for the fuller, candid discussion of this puzzle.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Ezra document, which addresses it as a genuinely difficult, not fully resolved puzzle -- Ezra 2's individual figures sum to roughly 29,818 and Nehemiah 7's to roughly 31,089, with about 14 of some 33 matching family entries showing numeric differences between the two chapters, while both state the identical total of 42,360. Several partial explanations are offered there (different points in a months-long process, uncounted smaller family groups, ordinary scribal transmission variation), none of which fully accounts for the discrepancy with mathematical precision -- a difficulty openly acknowledged even by scholars committed to the text's reliability.

## Chronology and Composition

### 3. The high priestly genealogy reaching Jaddua -- does this point to material added after Nehemiah's own lifetime?

**References:** Nehemiah 12:10-11, 22; Nehemiah 13:28

**The claim:** Nehemiah 12:10-11 and 22 trace the succession of high priests from Jeshua (contemporary with Zerubbabel, generations before Nehemiah) down through Joiakim, Eliashib (high priest during Nehemiah's own governorship), Joiada, Jonathan/Johanan, and finally Jaddua. According to the later historian Josephus, Jaddua was the high priest who met Alexander the Great -- an event dated to roughly 332 BC, nearly a full century after Nehemiah's own attested activity in the mid-400s BC. Separately, Nehemiah 13:28 records Nehemiah personally expelling an unnamed son of Joiada (Eliashib's grandson) for marrying a daughter of Sanballat, Nehemiah's chief opponent -- an episode Josephus also narrates, but assigns to a man named Manasseh, brother of the high priest Jaddua, occurring generations later, at the time of Alexander's arrival, rather than during Nehemiah's own lifetime.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the genealogical list at 12:10-11, 22 and the Sanballat son-in-law episode at 13:28 exactly as given; the question addressed here concerns composition history and identification with figures in Josephus's later, non-canonical history, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Two distinct questions are often merged in popular discussion of this passage, and this entry treats them separately. On the genealogy reaching Jaddua: most scholars, including many who hold a high view of the book's historical reliability, acknowledge a real possibility worth stating plainly. If Nehemiah 12:22's Jaddua is indeed the same high priest who later met Alexander -- as several conservative and mainstream sources consulted for this entry accept as probable, while others hold the reference is instead to an earlier, same-named ancestor of Josephus's Jaddua, given the well-documented ancient Jewish practice of grandsons and great-grandsons reusing a grandfather's name -- this specific verse's information most likely reflects a note added by a later editor continuing the priestly record after Nehemiah's own writing. Several conservative commentaries, including a widely used Catholic study-Bible commentary consulted for this entry, accept this without difficulty, treating it as evidence of a brief scribal supplement to an otherwise contemporaneous memoir. It is comparable to the appended notice of Moses' death at the end of Deuteronomy, addressed at greater length in this project's companion Deuteronomy document, rather than as undermining the book's substantial claim to derive from Nehemiah's own eyewitness account. On the Sanballat son-in-law episode specifically: most scholars conclude Josephus has conflated or duplicated a single family scandal, transplanting the marriage Nehemiah personally addressed in his own lifetime (13:28) into a second, later generation of the same family line and assigning it to a different individual (Manasseh) at a different, much later point in history, rather than concluding two entirely separate, coincidentally similar incidents occurred a century apart. Since Josephus's account is not Scripture, this project treats Nehemiah's own contemporaneous record as the more reliable source for the incident it directly narrates, while acknowledging the later editorial note at 12:22 as a reasonable, minor addition to the book's own record-keeping.

## Ethical Tensions

### 4. Nehemiah's harsh personal enforcement against intermarriage -- a different method than Ezra's

**References:** Nehemiah 13:23-27; Ezra 10:1-17

**The claim:** Nehemiah, discovering continued intermarriage with foreign women years after Ezra's earlier reforms, responds personally and forcefully: he 'confronted them and cursed them and beat some of them and pulled out their hair' (13:25), in addition to making them swear an oath. Ezra's earlier response to the same underlying problem (Ezra 9-10) proceeds instead through a more measured, communal process -- public mourning and confession, a formal assembly, and an orderly, case-by-case review over several months (Ezra 10:9-17) -- raising a question about whether these represent contradictory models of appropriate religious leadership, or simply two leaders' different personal styles addressing a recurring problem.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both accounts plainly and consistently as given, without softening either leader's specific method; the harmonization concerns recognizing differing circumstances and roles, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as compatible responses to somewhat different circumstances rather than as competing models Scripture endorses inconsistently: Ezra's reform addressed a problem being confronted and corrected for evidently the first time, in a formal, deliberative setting appropriate to establishing a new communal covenant and legal process (Ezra 10:3, 'let us make a covenant'); Nehemiah's confrontation, by contrast, addresses a relapse into the same sin roughly a generation later, discovered by Nehemiah personally and immediately, including specific evidence that the community's own children could no longer speak Hebrew as a result (13:24) -- a visible, generation-spanning covenant failure that Nehemiah, in his specific role as civil governor with direct disciplinary authority (rather than Ezra's more priestly, teaching-oriented role), addressed with personal, on-the-spot severity. Nehemiah's own prayers in the same passage (13:14, 22, 29, 31) reflect a leader keenly aware of standing at what he perceived as a critical, potentially final moment for the community's covenant fidelity, which most commentators read as contextualizing, without necessarily fully justifying to modern sensibilities, the harshness of his specific response -- a question some readers continue to find genuinely uncomfortable even where they accept the underlying concern (intermarriage leading to religious and cultural assimilation) as legitimate.

## Law and Practice

### 5. The renewed exclusion of Ammonites and Moabites

**References:** Nehemiah 13:1-3; Deuteronomy 23:3-6; Ruth 4:13-17

**The claim:** Nehemiah 13:1-3 records that, upon reading 'the Book of Moses... it was found written there that no Ammonite or Moabite should ever enter the assembly of God,' the community responded by separating out 'all those of foreign descent' -- applying a law (Deuteronomy 23:3-6) that this project's companion Deuteronomy and Ruth documents address at length, including the tension this exclusion creates with Ruth the Moabite's celebrated inclusion in Israel generations earlier.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Nehemiah 13:1-3 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Deuteronomy and Ruth documents for the fuller discussion of the underlying law's scope.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Deuteronomy and Ruth documents, which address the scope of the original exclusion (most likely concerning formal, corporate covenant status rather than barring any individual convert who personally renounced foreign gods and allegiance, as Ruth explicitly does) and its grammatical particulars. This book's own application of the law in Nehemiah's time is generally read by commentators as a straightforward, good-faith re-application of Deuteronomy's stated principle to the specific concern then at hand (guarding against renewed religious assimilation, the same concern driving the intermarriage crisis addressed elsewhere in this chapter), rather than as evidence of any tension within this book itself.

## Genealogy and Textual History

### 6. How many nations did God dispossess for Israel -- do the lists match?

**References:** Nehemiah 9:8; Genesis 15:19-21; Deuteronomy 7:1

**The claim:** Nehemiah's great prayer of confession recounts God's covenant with Abraham, including the promise to give the land 'of the Canaanite, the Hittite, the Amorite, the Perizzite, the Jebusite, and the Girgashite' to his descendants (9:8) -- a list of six nations. Genesis 15:19-21, recording the original covenant promise itself, lists ten nations (adding the Kenite, Kenizzite, Kadmonite, and Rephaim to the six named in Nehemiah). Deuteronomy 7:1, in yet another restatement, lists seven nations (the same six as Nehemiah, plus the Hivite).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each list's specific nations exactly as given in their respective passages, without harmonizing the count between them; the explanation concerns recognizing summary lists of varying length and detail rather than a strict, competing enumeration, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these varying lists as different-length summaries of the same broader reality rather than as competing, mutually exclusive claims about exactly how many distinct peoples occupied Canaan -- ancient Near Eastern king lists and territorial descriptions regularly varied in how many named subgroups they included depending on the occasion and level of detail required, without this being read as internally inconsistent by the original audience. The six or seven nations named in Deuteronomy 7:1 and echoed in Nehemiah 9:8 represent the more commonly repeated, likely more prominent or better-known peoples across the historical books generally, while Genesis 15's longer list of ten includes several additional, more obscure or smaller groups (the Kenites and Kadmonites, for instance, appear rarely elsewhere in the text) appropriate to the more expansive, foundational covenant-promise context in which that list appears. No text claims its own list is total and exhaustive to the exclusion of any other group not named.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. Eliashib the high priest: wall-builder, then enabler of Nehemiah's chief opponent

**References:** Nehemiah 3:1; Nehemiah 13:4-9

**The claim:** Nehemiah 3:1 opens the list of wall-builders with Eliashib the high priest and his fellow priests constructing the Sheep Gate -- a cooperative, faithful contribution to the reconstruction project. Later in the book, the same Eliashib is described as having provided Tobiah -- Nehemiah's persistent opponent throughout the wall-building narrative -- with a large chamber inside the Temple precincts during Nehemiah's absence, which Nehemiah discovers and furiously reverses upon his return (13:4-9).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given, without softening or explaining away Eliashib's later conduct; the harmonization is a matter of recognizing believable, unflattering character complexity across the narrative, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction between two competing portraits requiring reconciliation, but as a believable account of a real person's inconsistency or moral decline over time, a pattern this project has already documented for several other biblical figures (Gideon's ephod following his earlier faithful obedience; Jehu's commendation followed by later prophetic condemnation for excess). The text itself supplies no explicit motive for Eliashib's later accommodation of Tobiah (who was, per Nehemiah 13:4, 'related to' Eliashib by marriage, which most commentators read as the likely explanation for his willingness to grant Tobiah access), and does not attempt to soften or excuse the inconsistency -- a straightforward instance of Scripture's characteristic willingness to narrate its principal figures, including a high priest, with unflattering honesty rather than uniform, idealized consistency.
