# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Song of Solomon

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual and
interpretive questions involving the book of Song of Solomon, either entirely internal to the
book or in comparison with 1 Kings, Ephesians, and Galatians. For each item, this document
states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Song of Solomon is a short (8-chapter) book of unified love poetry. This
document has 6 entries rather than the full 12 used for longer books in this series,
consistent with the approach already taken for the similarly compact books of Ruth, Ezra,
Nehemiah, and Esther.

**A note on candor:** Entry 1 (the book's central interpretive question -- literal celebration
of human love, or allegory of divine love) and entry 3 (Solomonic authorship) are both
presented with genuine scholarly and theological diversity of opinion rather than a single
forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entry 5 (the "black, but lovely" / "black and beautiful" translation
choice at 1:5) was checked directly against eight of the ten published translations, confirming
a genuine and culturally significant split: the current NASB alone renders the connecting
conjunction additively ("and"), while the other translations checked render it contrastively
("but" or "yet"). The remaining entries are theological, historical, and literary questions
where all ten translations render the same underlying text and the harmonization is exegetical
rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine diversity of serious
opinion remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult
the primary texts directly.

## Theological Interpretation

### 1. Human romantic love poetry, or an allegory of divine love? -- and a book that never mentions God

**References:** Song of Solomon (entire book)

**The claim:** Song of Solomon consists entirely of frank, sensuous love poetry exchanged between a bride and groom (or, in some readings, a woman and two suitors), celebrating physical desire and romantic attraction in vivid, sometimes erotic detail. Like Esther (addressed at length in the companion Esther document), the book never once mentions God by name or explicit title anywhere in its text -- raising, in an even sharper form than Esther, the question of how a book of secular-seeming romantic and physical love poetry came to be included in the canon of Scripture at all, and whether it should be read literally (as a celebration of human marital love) or allegorically (as a figure for God's love for Israel, or Christ's love for the Church).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the entire book's love poetry plainly and consistently as given, without adding or removing any explicit reference to God; the interpretive question addressed here is a matter of hermeneutics and theological tradition, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is the central interpretive question surrounding this book, on which serious, faithful interpreters across many centuries have genuinely differed, and this entry does not force a single resolution. The dominant tradition for much of Jewish and Christian history (reflected in extensive rabbinic commentary reading the book as depicting God's covenant love for Israel, and in a long line of Christian commentators from Origen onward reading it as depicting Christ's love for the Church, or the individual soul's love for God) treated the book as primarily or exclusively allegorical, a reading that made its canonical inclusion straightforwardly explicable despite the absence of any explicit divine reference. A substantial and, in modern scholarship, now-dominant alternative reading holds the book is primarily a celebration of human marital and romantic love as a genuinely good gift of God in its own right (consistent with Genesis 1-2's own positive theology of marriage, sexuality, and physical union, addressed in this project's companion Genesis-adjacent material), needing no allegorical translation to justify its inclusion any more than Proverbs' or Ecclesiastes' this-worldly wisdom needs allegorizing -- on this reading, the book's implicit theology is that romantic and physical love between a husband and wife is itself a proper subject of inspired reflection and a legitimate part of a full biblical anthropology, without requiring every image to correspond to a specific point of divine-human relationship. A significant number of contemporary and historic interpreters hold both readings are legitimate and complementary rather than exclusive -- the human love genuinely celebrated in its own right, while also serving, as human marriage does elsewhere in Scripture (Ephesians 5:31-32, explicitly calling marriage 'a profound mystery' pointing to Christ and the Church), as a fitting analogy for divine love without this requiring every specific verse to carry a coded, non-literal meaning.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 2. Celebrating exclusive, singular love -- in a book attributed to a king famous for 700 wives and 300 concubines

**References:** Song of Solomon 1:1; Song of Solomon 2:16; Song of Solomon 6:3; Song of Solomon 6:8-9; 1 Kings 11:3

**The claim:** Song of Solomon is traditionally attributed to Solomon (1:1) and repeatedly celebrates exclusive, singular, mutual devotion between the two lovers: 'My beloved is mine, and I am his' (2:16), 'I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine' (6:3). Yet 1 Kings 11:3 records that Solomon himself had 700 wives and 300 concubines -- among the most extreme cases of royal polygamy recorded in Scripture, and one this project's companion 1 Kings document addresses as a direct violation of the king's law in Deuteronomy 17:17. The book itself even explicitly acknowledges a royal harem within its own text: 'There are sixty queens and eighty concubines, and virgins without number. My dove, my perfect one, is the only one' (6:8-9) -- naming the very reality the poem's celebration of singular devotion seems to stand against.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2:16, 6:3, and 6:8-9 plainly and consistently as given, without softening the harem's explicit mention or the surrounding declarations of singular devotion; the harmonization rests on reading 6:8-9 as the poem's own superlative comparison rather than a denial of the wider historical context, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read the book's own explicit acknowledgment of the harem in 6:8-9 as the key to resolving rather than deepening this tension: the poem does not deny or hide the existence of many other women in the king's court, but explicitly and deliberately elevates the beloved above all of them by comparison -- 'my dove, my perfect one, is the only one' functions as a superlative declaration of uniqueness and preeminence within a real polygamous context, not a factual claim that no other women existed. Some read this as the text's own honest, unresolved tension between the ideal of exclusive marital devotion the poetry itself celebrates and idealizes, and the real, compromised practice of Solomon's court that 1 Kings records without approval -- the Song articulating what genuine romantic love aspires to and feels like from within the relationship, even in a historical and cultural setting (royal polygamy) the book does not otherwise address or defend. Others hold that if the book is substantially the product of a later compiler working with earlier Solomonic material (addressed further in entry 3 below) rather than a strictly autobiographical composition, the tension may reflect an idealized poetic voice not strictly bound to a full accounting of Solomon's own actual domestic arrangements, in the same way ancient love poetry more generally often idealizes a single beloved without needing to address a poet's full biography.

## Literary Composition

### 3. Solomonic authorship claimed -- yet questioned on similar grounds to Ecclesiastes

**References:** Song of Solomon 1:1

**The claim:** Song of Solomon 1:1 identifies the book as 'The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's,' traditionally read as a straightforward authorship claim. As with Ecclesiastes (addressed at greater length in the companion Ecclesiastes document), a number of scholars -- including some who otherwise hold a high view of the book's inspiration -- have questioned strict Solomonic authorship on linguistic grounds (some of the same late Hebrew and apparent Persian/Aramaic features found in Ecclesiastes appear here as well) and on the observation that the book's superscription, in Hebrew, is itself somewhat ambiguous between 'the Song of Songs, which is Solomon's [i.e., by Solomon],' 'which is about Solomon,' or 'which is dedicated to Solomon.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the book's superscription (1:1) exactly as given, without resolving the authorship question through their wording; this is a matter of historical and linguistic scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the parallel question addressed at greater length for Ecclesiastes, this remains a genuinely debated point among careful scholars, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Defenders of Solomonic authorship note the book's extensive references to flora, fauna, and geography from across the wider ancient Near East (consistent with Solomon's own famous breadth of knowledge and international connections, 1 Kings 4:33-34) and its natural fit with Jewish tradition placing all three Solomonic wisdom books (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and this book) at different stages of Solomon's own life. Those questioning strict authorship note the same linguistic evidence raised for Ecclesiastes, and some further note that the book's romantic idealization of a single beloved sits in some tension with what is known of Solomon's own extensive harem (addressed in entry 2 above), which some read as more consistent with a poem written about an idealized Solomon by a later admirer than as Solomon's own autobiographical composition. Many conservative scholars hold a mediating position similar to the one offered for Ecclesiastes: that the book preserves genuinely Solomonic content, later given its final compiled and edited form, without this undermining its canonical authority or substantial connection to Solomon.

## Names and Identity

### 4. The 'Shulammite' -- Abishag the Shunammite, or an unrelated figure?

**References:** Song of Solomon 6:13; 1 Kings 1:1-4; 1 Kings 2:17-22

**The claim:** Song of Solomon 6:13 refers to the beloved woman by a specific title, 'the Shulammite' (or, in some translations, 'the Shulamite') -- a term that closely resembles 'the Shunammite,' the description given to Abishag, the young woman brought to care for the aging King David in 1 Kings 1:1-4, and later the subject of a request by Adonijah that nearly cost him his life (1 Kings 2:17-22). Some readers, noting the similarity, have identified the beloved of this book with Abishag specifically; others regard the resemblance as coincidental or the result of a scribal or dialectal variation rather than evidence of the same historical individual.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders 'the Shulamite' at 6:13, with a single 'm.'

**Reconciliation:** Most scholars regard the identification with Abishag as unlikely and probably unintended, for several reasons: 'Shulammite' most likely derives from 'Shulem' or 'Shunem,' a town in the territory of Issachar, functioning as a general place-name designation ('the woman from Shunem/Shulem') rather than a personal identifier necessarily pointing to one specific, previously named biblical figure -- a common pattern for identifying women by their hometown elsewhere in the Old Testament (compare 'the Shunammite woman' who hosts Elisha in 2 Kings 4:8-37, a different individual from Abishag, showing the town-name designation was applied to more than one woman). Given that Abishag's own story (1 Kings 1-2) presents her specifically as David's aged-care companion rather than as anyone romantically linked to Solomon, and the Song's own beloved is nowhere else connected to that specific narrative, most commentators conclude the resemblance in terms is likely coincidental, or at most reflects a shared regional origin (the same town) rather than the same individual woman.

## Numbers and Text

### 5. 'I am black, but lovely' -- or 'and lovely'? A translation choice with real cultural and historical weight

**References:** Song of Solomon 1:5

**The claim:** Song of Solomon 1:5 has the beloved woman describe herself, in the most common traditional English rendering, as 'black, but comely' (or 'dark, but lovely') -- language read by some as implying a contrast, as though her dark complexion were a deficiency her beauty overcomes or compensates for despite it. The underlying Hebrew conjunction connecting the two descriptions (waw) is genuinely ambiguous and can be rendered either adversatively ('but') or simply additively ('and'), and translators have made different choices at this specific point, with real implications for how the verse reads.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'I am deeply tanned but lovely,' contrastive (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'I am dark like the tents of Kedar, yet lovely like the curtains of Solomon,' restructuring the verse but preserving a contrastive relationship via 'yet' (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'I am very dark, but lovely,' also contrastive (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'I am black, but comely,' the traditional contrastive rendering (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'I am dark, but lovely,' also contrastive (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** The current NASB text renders 'I am black and beautiful,' the additive rendering -- a documented departure from most other translations surveyed here (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'I am black but lovely,' contrastive, differing from NASB's current additive choice despite LSB's general pattern of tracking NASB closely (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'Dark am I, yet lovely,' contrastive (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'I am dark but beautiful,' contrastive (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'I am very dark, but comely,' contrastive (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction between two biblical texts, but a genuine, actively discussed translation choice at a single point of real cultural and historical significance -- the Hebrew waw conjunction is highly flexible and context-dependent throughout the Old Testament, and the choice between 'but' and 'and' at this specific verse is not dictated by grammar alone but by the translator's interpretive judgment about the intended relationship between darkness and beauty in the woman's own self-description. Scholars favoring 'and' (an additive, non-contrastive rendering, e.g., 'I am black and beautiful') argue this better reflects a straightforward, non-apologetic self-description untainted by any assumption that dark skin requires justification alongside beauty, and note the word order in the Hebrew places 'black' emphatically first. Scholars favoring 'but' note the immediately following verses (1:6) have the woman explain that her complexion resulted from being forced to work outdoors in the sun ('do not gaze at me because I am dark; the sun has looked upon me'), which several read as implying some degree of self-consciousness or social stigma attached to sun-darkened skin in the poem's own ancient context (where pale, unweathered skin often signaled higher social status, since it indicated a life not spent in outdoor labor), making a contrastive 'but' arguably more contextually apt even without any negative judgment about dark skin's intrinsic beauty. Both readings are defended by serious scholars, and this entry does not resolve between them.

## Ethical Tensions

### 6. 'Jealousy is fierce as the grave' -- a vice condemned elsewhere, or a mark of committed love?

**References:** Song of Solomon 8:6; Galatians 5:19-21; Exodus 20:5

**The claim:** Song of Solomon 8:6 describes love and its accompanying jealousy in powerful, almost reverent terms: 'love is strong as death, jealousy is fierce as the grave' (or, in some translations, 'as cruel as the grave') -- language that treats jealousy as a natural, even admirable, feature of passionate, committed love. Yet jealousy is elsewhere listed among the sinful 'works of the flesh' Paul explicitly warns against (Galatians 5:19-21, 'jealousy... and things like these'), raising a question about whether Scripture is consistent in its evaluation of jealousy as a vice or a virtue.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Song of Solomon 8:6 and Galatians 5:19-21 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing righteous, protective jealousy proper to an exclusive covenant bond from sinful, resentful envy, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators resolve this the same way this project has addressed God's own repeatedly described 'jealousy' for his covenant people (Exodus 20:5, 'I the LORD your God am a jealous God') -- by distinguishing a righteous, protective jealousy appropriate to an exclusive covenant relationship (whether marital or divine-covenantal) from a sinful, self-centered envy or possessive suspicion directed at another's unrelated success or possessions, which is the specific vice Galatians 5 and similar New Testament passages condemn. Song of Solomon 8:6's jealousy is presented specifically in the context of exclusive marital devotion and the fierce, protective intensity appropriate to a covenant bond that has been given and received exclusively -- the same kind of jealousy God himself is described as having for his own covenant people's exclusive devotion, treated approvingly throughout Scripture, rather than the resentful envy of what belongs to another that Paul's list of vices addresses. Both texts describe genuinely different attitudes sharing the same English word, not two competing evaluations of a single, univocal concept.
