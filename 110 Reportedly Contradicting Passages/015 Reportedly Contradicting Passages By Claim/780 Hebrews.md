# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Hebrews

This document catalogs 5 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Hebrews, either
entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion 2 Timothy, Daniel, and
Tobit documents, Genesis, 2 Kings, and John. For each item, this document states the claim as
it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim
appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses Hebrews' own anonymous
authorship -- a genuine mystery acknowledged since antiquity, with Origen's own third-century
verdict ("God knows") still regarded by most scholars as the most honest available assessment.
Entry 2 addresses the most severely worded apostasy warnings in the entire New Testament,
considerably intensifying the related question addressed in the companion 2 Timothy document.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 2, and 3 are presented with genuine scholarly and theological
diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 3 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing the full range of authorship candidates and Melchizedek interpretations,
including citation to Origen's original statement (preserved by Eusebius) and the Dead Sea
Scrolls document 11QMelchizedek. The remaining entries are addressed from standard
harmonization approaches, several of which build on treatments already established in the
companion 2 Timothy, Daniel, and Tobit documents.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly or
theological diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. Hebrews' own author -- a genuine mystery acknowledged since antiquity

**References:** Hebrews 1:1; Hebrews 13:22-25

**The claim:** Unlike every one of Paul's thirteen letters, each of which explicitly names Paul as author, Hebrews never identifies its own author anywhere in the text, despite the writer clearly being known personally to the letter's original recipients (13:19, 22-23 reflect a close, direct personal relationship, including a reference to 'our brother Timothy'). This anonymity was already a recognized puzzle in the earliest centuries of the church, not merely a modern critical discovery.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hebrews as a continuous, unsigned text, without supplying an author's name anywhere in the text itself; the authorship question addressed here is a matter of historical scholarship dating back to antiquity, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most genuinely open authorship questions addressed anywhere in the New Testament, and this entry presents the range of proposed candidates rather than a single certain answer. The third-century theologian Origen already candidly acknowledged the difficulty, stating, as preserved by the church historian Eusebius, that 'who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows' -- an assessment most contemporary scholars still regard as the most honest one available, nearly eighteen centuries later. The traditional attribution to Paul himself, which gained currency particularly in the Eastern church from the late second century onward, faces substantial internal difficulties. The letter's sophisticated, polished Greek style differs markedly from Paul's own writing elsewhere. Hebrews 2:3 describes the gospel message as having been confirmed to the author by those who personally heard the Lord, a description Paul, who insisted elsewhere on having received his own gospel directly (Galatians 1:11-12), would be unlikely to apply to himself. And the letter's own extended argument and theological emphases, particularly its sustained focus on Christ's priesthood, largely absent from Paul's other letters, differ from Paul's typical style and concerns. The Western church resisted the Pauline attribution for roughly three centuries before eventually accepting the letter's canonicity on other grounds. Other candidates seriously proposed across church history and modern scholarship include Barnabas, Luke, Apollos, and Priscilla. Barnabas was already proposed by the early theologian Tertullian, and is supported by Barnabas's own identification as a Levite in Acts 4:36, which would explain the author's detailed familiarity with the Levitical priesthood. Luke was proposed by Clement of Alexandria as at least a possible translator, and revived in modern scholarship by David Allen based on stylistic affinities with Luke-Acts. Apollos was proposed by Martin Luther in the sixteenth century and is favored by a considerable number of contemporary scholars, given Apollos's description in Acts 18:24 as an eloquent, Alexandrian-trained expert in the Scriptures, matching the letter's polished style and scriptural mastery. And Priscilla was proposed by the scholar Adolf von Harnack in 1900, a theory that would also help explain the letter's anonymity, given the more limited public teaching role available to women in that specific era, though this proposal has not attracted majority scholarly support. This project does not adjudicate between these candidates, noting that the question remains genuinely unresolved and has been honestly acknowledged as such since the earliest generations of the church.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. 'It is impossible... to restore them again to repentance' -- one of the most severely worded apostasy warnings in the New Testament

**References:** Hebrews 6:4-6; Hebrews 10:26-31; Hebrews 12:16-17

**The claim:** Hebrews 6:4-6 delivers an unusually severe warning: 'it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit... and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm.' Similarly severe warnings recur later in the letter (10:26-31's warning that 'there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins' for those who 'go on sinning deliberately,' and 12:16-17's account of Esau finding 'no chance to repent' despite seeking it 'with tears'). These passages, taken at face value, appear to describe genuine believers permanently and irrecoverably losing their salvation, connecting to and considerably intensifying the question addressed in the companion 2 Timothy document regarding 2 Timothy 2:11-13's more measured language.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hebrews 6:4-6, 10:26-31, and 12:16-17 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most substantively and sincerely debated theological questions in the New Testament, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most substantively and sincerely debated passages in the entire New Testament regarding the possibility of apostasy, and this project does not adjudicate the underlying question, consistent with its treatment of the related, less severe passage in the companion 2 Timothy document. Traditions holding to the perseverance of genuine believers (broadly, Reformed and some other traditions) generally read this passage as describing people who had extensive, genuine exposure to the blessings and experience of the Christian community and its teaching -- 'enlightened,' having 'tasted' the heavenly gift, having 'shared in' the Holy Spirit's presence and work among the community -- without this description necessarily implying they had themselves become truly, savingly regenerate believers in the first place (a distinction some draw between genuine participation in the visible community's blessings and genuine, invisible saving faith, with the warning describing a decisive, final rejection by those who had experienced much but never possessed saving faith internally, rather than a true believer losing salvation once genuinely possessed). Traditions holding that genuine believers can commit final, decisive apostasy read the passage's own specific, cumulative language (particularly the multiple descriptive terms in 6:4-5, each individually used elsewhere in the New Testament to describe authentic Christian experience) as describing real, true believers, and read the passage as a genuine, serious warning against the real possibility of a decisive, final falling away that forfeits salvation once genuinely held. Both traditions agree the passage functions pastorally as a serious warning intended to promote genuine perseverance and discourage complacency, rather than as an abstract theological puzzle to be resolved apart from its own intended pastoral function -- the letter's own immediately following words (6:9, 'we feel sure of better things for you') are widely read, regardless of one's position on the underlying question, as expressing the author's own confidence that his actual audience would not, in fact, prove to be the kind of person this severe warning describes.

### 3. Melchizedek -- 'without father or mother' -- a supernatural being, or an argument from scriptural silence?

**References:** Hebrews 7:1-3; Genesis 14:18-20; Psalm 110:4

**The claim:** Hebrews 7:1-3 describes Melchizedek, the mysterious priest-king who blessed Abraham in Genesis 14, as being 'without father or mother or genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but resembling the Son of God he continues a priest forever' -- language some read as describing an actual supernatural, superhuman, or even divine being (proposals have included an angel, a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ himself, or a heavenly, eschatological figure), while others read it as a rhetorical argument drawn from the striking absence of any recorded genealogy, birth, or death for Melchizedek within the Genesis narrative itself, rather than a claim about his actual nature.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hebrews 7:1-3 plainly and consistently as given; this remains a genuinely debated interpretive question with ancient roots, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated interpretive question with a documented ancient background, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. The most common reading among contemporary commentators holds that the author of Hebrews is making a deliberate argument from scriptural silence -- unlike the Levitical priests, whose entire legitimacy depended on a verifiable, recorded genealogy establishing their descent from Aaron (a requirement the same letter's own surrounding argument addresses at length), Melchizedek simply appears in the Genesis narrative without any of the usual genealogical framing ancient Hebrew narrative typically supplies for significant figures, and without any recorded death -- a literary silence the author of Hebrews reads typologically as fittingly prefiguring the eternal, underived nature of Christ's own priesthood, 'resembling the Son of God' specifically in this respect (the absence of the usual markers), rather than asserting Melchizedek was not, in fact, an ordinary human being who was actually born and actually died like anyone else. This reading is supported by the passage's own explicit language of resemblance and typology ('resembling the Son of God') rather than direct identification. A minority position holds Melchizedek was some kind of actual supernatural being, a reading with some background support from Second Temple Jewish tradition -- the Dead Sea Scrolls include a document (commonly called 11QMelchizedek) that independently develops Melchizedek into an exalted, heavenly, eschatological redeemer and judge figure, considerably beyond his brief, human appearance in Genesis, showing that elevated, non-ordinary readings of Melchizedek were already circulating in Jewish thought before Hebrews was written, and some scholars propose the author of Hebrews may be aware of, and drawing on, this broader interpretive tradition even without fully adopting its most elevated claims. This project does not adjudicate between these readings, noting that both draw on genuine textual and historical evidence.

### 4. Christ's superiority to angels (1:4-14) -- and the developing New Testament portrait of angelic beings

**References:** Hebrews 1:4-14; Hebrews 2:2-3

**The claim:** Hebrews opens with an extended argument for Christ's superiority to angels, quoting a catena of Old Testament texts (1:5-13) to establish that Christ, unlike any angel, is called 'Son,' is worshiped by angels, and reigns on an eternal throne -- part of a broader concern, addressed further at 2:2-3, that treats angels as significant mediating agents of the old covenant ('the message declared by angels proved to be reliable') whose authority Christ's own new covenant now decisively surpasses.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hebrews 1:4-14 and 2:2-3 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a matter of theological argument building on already-established premises, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a further point in the same developing New Testament portrait of angelic beings addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (regarding the increasing specificity of angelic revelation across the Old Testament and into texts such as Daniel and Tobit, addressed in their own companion documents). Hebrews' own extended argument here does not introduce any new or unprecedented claim about angels' general nature or role, but draws on an already well-established Jewish tradition (reflected in Deuteronomy 33:2 and Acts 7:53, both associating the giving of the Mosaic law with angelic mediation) to construct a deliberate argument from lesser to greater -- if the old covenant, mediated through angels, carried binding authority, then the new covenant, mediated directly by the Son himself (who is greater than any angel), carries even greater, more binding authority and consequence for those who neglect it (2:2-3's own explicit point). This is generally read as a rhetorical and theological argument building on already-accepted premises about angelic mediation, not as introducing a novel or inconsistent claim about angels' own nature or status elsewhere in Scripture.

## Theological Tensions

### 5. 'It is appointed for man to die once' -- compatible with biblical accounts of resuscitation?

**References:** Hebrews 9:27; 2 Kings 13:20-21; John 11:1-44

**The claim:** Hebrews 9:27 states, 'it is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment' -- a statement of the normal, universal pattern of human mortality. Scripture elsewhere records several instances of individuals dying and then being restored to physical life, at least temporarily, including a dead man reviving upon contact with the bones of the already-deceased prophet Elisha (2 Kings 13:20-21) and Lazarus's raising after four days in the tomb (John 11:1-44), raising a question of whether these accounts are compatible with a strict reading of 'die once' for every human being without exception.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Hebrews 9:27 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the passage's own general, rhetorical scope rather than a strict, exceptionless metaphysical claim excluding temporary miraculous resuscitation, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a genuine contradiction, but as a matter of recognizing the passage's own intended scope and purpose. Hebrews 9:27 is stating the ordinary, universal pattern governing human mortality and its consequence (one death, followed by final judgment) as the basis for the passage's own immediately following theological argument about Christ's own singular, sufficient sacrifice (9:28, 'so Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time... to save those who are eagerly waiting for him') -- the author's rhetorical point depends on the general, normal pattern of a single, final death followed by judgment, not on a strict, exceptionless metaphysical claim that categorically excludes the small number of documented biblical instances in which God miraculously and temporarily restored someone to ordinary earthly life before their own final, ultimate death. Those individuals who were resuscitated (the man restored by contact with Elisha's bones, Lazarus, and others) still underwent an ordinary, final, once-for-all death at a later point in their lives, consistent with the passage's own general rule -- their temporary resuscitation does not multiply the number of times they ultimately, finally died and faced judgment, meaning these accounts do not actually contradict the passage's own stated principle once its rhetorical scope and purpose are properly understood.
