# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Revelation

**A note on this document's place in the series: this is the final document in this entire
project.** With Revelation, this series completes its coverage of the full 66-book Protestant
canon, the seven additional Catholic deuterocanonical books, and the Additions to Esther and
Daniel -- the complete scope originally envisioned for this Bible study reference library.

This document catalogs 9 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Revelation,
either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with the companion Daniel, 1 and 2
Thessalonians, Romans, 2 John, Philippians, and 1 Timothy documents, and Deuteronomy, 1 Kings,
and 2 Kings. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes
how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's
own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and
gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's foundational interpretive question:** Entry 1 addresses the four major
interpretive frameworks (preterist, historicist, futurist, idealist) that govern how nearly
every other specific question in this book is approached -- the single most foundational
methodological question in Revelation studies, and one this project does not resolve, any
more than it resolves the comparably foundational questions addressed regarding Daniel's
seventy weeks or 1 Thessalonians' rapture timing in their own companion documents.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 2, 6, 5, 7, and 8 are all presented with genuine, often
centuries-old scholarly and denominational diversity of opinion rather than a single forced
resolution. Entry 6 in particular addresses a historically fraught identification with real,
documented consequences for Catholic-Protestant relations, addressed directly rather than
passed over.

**Verification status:** Entries 4 and 5 were checked in unusual depth given their genuinely
checkable textual and historical content -- the 666/616 manuscript variant and its connection
to Nero Caesar via gematria, and the missing tribe of Dan's connection to documented idolatry
traditions and the Antichrist speculation already attested in Irenaeus and Hippolytus. The
remaining entries are addressed from standard scholarly positions, several of which build
directly on treatments already established throughout this series.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly or
denominational diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary text and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Interpretation

### 1. Four major interpretive frameworks for the entire book -- the most foundational question in Revelation studies

**References:** Revelation 1:1-3; Revelation 1:19

**The claim:** Before any single passage in Revelation can be addressed, readers face a foundational interpretive choice regarding the book's own overall structure and referent, since nearly every other specific question addressed elsewhere in this document is downstream of this choice. Revelation 1:19 instructs John to write 'the things that you have seen, those that are and those that are to take place after this' -- language itself open to different readings of scope and timing.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Revelation's opening verses plainly and consistently as given; the choice among these four interpretive frameworks is a matter of hermeneutical approach to the entire book, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Four major interpretive frameworks have been seriously defended throughout church history, and this project does not adjudicate between them, consistent with its treatment of comparably foundational, actively contested eschatological questions elsewhere in this series (Daniel's seventy weeks, 1 Thessalonians' rapture timing). The preterist framework holds that most or all of Revelation's specific prophecies were fulfilled in the first century, primarily in the fall of Jerusalem (AD 70) and/or the fall of Rome, understanding the book as addressing John's own immediate historical context and near-future rather than the distant future. The historicist framework, historically prominent among Reformation-era Protestants, reads Revelation as a symbolic panorama of the entire church age from John's own time to the second coming, with specific symbols corresponding to specific later historical events and figures (this framework historically produced the identification of the papacy with the beast or Babylon, addressed in entry 6 below). The futurist framework, dominant within contemporary evangelicalism and especially dispensationalism, holds that most of Revelation from chapter 4 onward describes events still future even to modern readers, clustered around a final period of tribulation preceding Christ's visible return. The idealist (or symbolic) framework reads Revelation's imagery as representing timeless spiritual truths and the ongoing cosmic conflict between good and evil throughout the entire church age, without requiring identification with any specific historical persons or events at all, whether past, present, or future. Serious, careful biblical scholars occupy all four positions, and many contemporary interpreters hold blended or eclectic positions drawing on more than one framework for different sections of the book. This project takes no position on which framework, or combination of frameworks, is correct.

### 2. The Millennium -- premillennial, postmillennial, or amillennial?

**References:** Revelation 20:1-6

**The claim:** Revelation 20:1-6 describes Satan being bound for 'a thousand years,' the martyred saints reigning with Christ for this same period, and a subsequent, final rebellion -- the primary New Testament text for the doctrine of the millennium, one of the most substantively and actively debated eschatological questions across contemporary Christian denominations.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Revelation 20:1-6 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most substantively debated eschatological questions across major Christian denominations, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This project does not adjudicate this actively and sincerely debated question, consistent with its treatment of the underlying interpretive-framework question in entry 1 above, to which this question is closely connected. Premillennialism holds that Christ returns before this thousand-year period, personally and visibly reigning on earth for its duration prior to the final judgment (the position generally associated with a futurist reading of the book as a whole). Postmillennialism holds that Christ returns after an extended period of the gospel's own successful, transformative spread throughout the world (understood as, or leading into, the 'thousand years,' itself often read symbolically rather than as a literal count), with the millennium representing an era of increasing gospel influence and societal flourishing preceding Christ's return. Amillennialism holds that the 'thousand years' symbolically describes the entire present church age between Christ's first and second comings, with Satan's binding referring to a real, present restriction on his ability to deceive the nations (rather than a specific future period), and Christ's own reign understood as his present, spiritual reign from heaven rather than a future, earthly one -- a position generally associated with an idealist or amillennial reading of Revelation as a whole. This project takes no position on which of these three major views, each held by serious, biblically engaged denominations and theologians, is correct.

### 3. 'Do not add to... or take away' -- a warning about the whole Bible, or specifically about Revelation itself?

**References:** Revelation 22:18-19; Deuteronomy 4:2

**The claim:** Revelation 22:18-19 delivers a severe warning: 'I warn everyone who hears the words of the prophecy of this book: if anyone adds to them, God will add to him the plagues described in this book, and if anyone takes away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God will take away his share in the tree of life...' This verse is frequently invoked in popular usage as a warning against altering the entire Bible, or as a proof-text establishing the closure of the whole biblical canon -- yet the verse's own explicit, repeated wording refers specifically to 'this book' and 'this prophecy,' language most naturally referring to Revelation itself rather than to the Bible as a complete sixty-six-book (or larger) collection, which did not yet exist in its final canonical form at the time Revelation was written.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Revelation 22:18-19 plainly and consistently as given, preserving its own specific reference to 'this book' and 'this prophecy'; this entry addresses a matter of popular application extending beyond the verse's own immediate scope, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable case, similar to a few other instances addressed elsewhere in this project (regarding popular application of a specific verse extending beyond its own original, immediate scope, such as Philippians 4:13 and 1 Timothy 6:10, addressed in their own companion documents), of a memorable warning being popularly applied more broadly than its own text most directly supports. Most careful commentators read Revelation 22:18-19 as specifically protecting the integrity of John's own prophetic vision as recorded in this particular book -- a formula with a recognized ancient precedent in Deuteronomy 4:2's own comparable warning against adding to or subtracting from the Torah's specific commands, a formula ancient authors used to protect the integrity of a specific completed document rather than necessarily to make a claim about the future closure of an entire, still-developing scriptural collection. This is not to say the verse has no broader relevance to how later Christian tradition has understood the seriousness of tampering with any inspired scriptural text (a principle with genuine application beyond Revelation specifically), but its own explicit, repeated wording ('this book,' 'this prophecy') most directly and immediately protects Revelation's own text, and its popular use as a specific proof-text establishing the entire Bible's own canonical closure represents an extension beyond what the verse's own plain wording most directly supports.

## Numbers and Text

### 4. 'His number is 666' -- or 616? A genuine textual variant, most likely both pointing to the same historical figure

**References:** Revelation 13:16-18

**The claim:** Revelation 13:18 states, 'let the one who has understanding calculate the number of the beast, for it is the number of a man, and his number is 666' -- among the most famous and endlessly speculated-about numbers in the entire Bible. A small number of ancient manuscripts, however, including the early Papyrus 115 (dated to roughly AD 250) and the fifth-century Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus, read '616' instead of '666,' a genuine, independently documented textual variant already known to and discussed by the second-century church father Irenaeus.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** All ten translations render Revelation 13:18 as '666'; none of the ten translations prints '616' in the main text, though some include a footnote acknowledging this genuine, independently documented manuscript variant.
- **NASB:** Some editions of the NASB include a footnote noting the 616 variant reading found in some ancient manuscripts.
- **NIV:** Some editions of the NIV include a similar footnote regarding the 616 variant.

**Reconciliation:** This is a well-documented textual variant, and this entry presents the manuscript evidence and its most widely accepted explanation directly. The great majority of textual scholars regard 666 as the more likely original reading, both because it is more strongly attested across the wider range of surviving manuscripts and because Irenaeus, writing in the late second century, treated 666 as the reading requiring explanation (rather than 616), suggesting 666 was already the dominant, received reading in his own time. Most scholars connect the number, using the ancient practice of gematria (assigning numerical values to letters), to the Roman emperor Nero: the Hebrew transliteration of 'Nero Caesar' (Neron Caesar) yields exactly 666 when its letters are added together, a calculation independently attested in a Dead Sea Scroll fragment from Nero's own reign showing this same Hebrew spelling in contemporary use. The 616 variant is generally explained the same way, as pointing to the identical historical figure through a slightly different route -- either a Latin spelling of 'Nero Caesar' calculated by gematria, or a simplified Hebrew spelling of the same name dropping its final letter (which would also reduce the total by exactly fifty, precisely accounting for the numerical difference between 666 and 616). On this widely held reading, both numbers converge on the same historical identification (Nero, whose persecution of Christians provides a plausible immediate context for Revelation's own concerns) via two documented variant spellings of his name, rather than representing two unrelated theories. A minority of commentators read the number symbolically rather than as a coded historical name at all (most notably G.K. Beale, who reads 666 as emphasizing the beast's utter, threefold imperfection relative to the number seven's own completeness), a position other scholars find less satisfying given John's own explicit instruction to 'calculate' the number, a term specifically associated with numerical computation rather than purely symbolic reflection. This project presents both readings, favoring neither definitively, while noting the gematria-based Nero identification enjoys unusually wide scholarly support relative to some of the more genuinely open questions addressed elsewhere in this series.

### 5. The 144,000 -- literal ethnic Israel, or symbolic of the whole church? And why is the tribe of Dan missing?

**References:** Revelation 7:4-8; Revelation 14:1-5

**The claim:** Revelation 7:4-8 lists 144,000 sealed servants of God, 12,000 from each of twelve tribes of Israel -- yet the specific list of tribes differs from every Old Testament tribal listing: the tribe of Dan is entirely absent, with Manasseh included alongside Joseph to restore the number to twelve. This substitution has generated centuries of interpretive discussion regarding both the identity of the 144,000 as a whole (literal ethnic Israelites, or symbolic of the complete church) and the specific, otherwise unexplained omission of Dan.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Revelation 7:4-8 plainly and consistently as given, preserving the same tribal list (with Dan absent and Manasseh present); the identity of the 144,000 and the reason for Dan's specific omission remain genuinely debated regardless of translation, and are not resolved by any of the ten translations' specific wording.

**Reconciliation:** On the identity of the 144,000: this connects to the same broader interpretive-framework question addressed in entry 1, and this project does not adjudicate it -- futurist interpreters generally read the 144,000 as a literal, specific number of ethnic Jewish believers sealed for protection and service during a future tribulation period; idealist and amillennial interpreters (a position held by Augustine, among others, per one contemporary source) generally read the number symbolically, representing the complete, full number of God's people (the church) under a deliberately idealized, symbolic Israelite tribal structure, rather than a literal ethnic count. On the specific omission of Dan: this is a genuinely unresolved historical puzzle, and this entry presents the leading proposals rather than a single certain answer. The most commonly proposed explanation connects Dan's omission to that tribe's own documented, persistent association with idolatry throughout Israelite history -- the tribe of Dan established its own unauthorized cultic center with a graven image after failing to fully possess its own allotted territory (Judges 18), and later became one of two sites (alongside Bethel) where King Jeroboam I deliberately established golden calf worship to draw the northern kingdom away from Jerusalem (1 Kings 12:28-30), giving Dan an unusually persistent, documented association with idolatrous worship among the twelve tribes. A separate, historically significant tradition, already attested in the second-century church fathers Irenaeus and Hippolytus, held that the Antichrist himself would arise specifically from the tribe of Dan, connecting Jacob's own ambiguous blessing over Dan in Genesis 49:17 ('Dan shall be a serpent in the way... that bites the horse's heels') with Jeremiah 8:16's mention of the 'snorting of [enemy] horses' being heard 'from Dan' -- though most contemporary commentators regard this specific tradition as considerably less likely than the idolatry-based explanation, and note plainly that nothing in the New Testament itself states or requires this specific reading. This project does not adjudicate between these proposals, noting this remains a genuinely debated question without a single certain answer.

## Ethical Tensions

### 6. 'Babylon the Great' -- Rome, a future empire, or a specific later church? A historically fraught identification

**References:** Revelation 17:1-18; Revelation 18:1-24

**The claim:** Revelation 17-18 describes the judgment of 'Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes,' a symbolic figure whose specific identity has been the subject of considerable historical and denominational dispute -- most scholars identify her with imperial Rome (supported by the explicit reference to 'seven mountains' in 17:9, widely known as a description of Rome's own seven hills, and by the figure's description as 'the great city that has dominion over the kings of the earth,' 17:18, fitting Rome's own first-century imperial status), but this identification has historically been extended, particularly by Reformation-era Protestant interpreters working from a historicist framework (addressed in entry 1 above), to the Roman Catholic Church specifically -- an identification with real, documented historical consequences for Catholic-Protestant relations that this project addresses candidly rather than passing over.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Revelation 17-18 plainly and consistently as given, without resolving 'Babylon's' specific identity in the text itself; this remains a historically significant and, in some of its specific applications, genuinely fraught interpretive question, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely significant question with real historical weight, and this entry presents the range of positions directly. The majority scholarly position, held broadly across preterist, idealist, and many futurist interpreters alike, identifies 'Babylon' in its original, first-century context with imperial Rome as the persecuting political and economic power of John's own day -- a reading with substantial textual support (the seven hills, and Babylon's own historic Old Testament role as the paradigmatic oppressor and destroyer of Jerusalem's Temple, a role Rome had itself just repeated in AD 70) and broad support across the four major interpretive frameworks addressed in entry 1, even though preterist, historicist, futurist, and idealist readers differ on whether this reference is exhausted by first-century Rome specifically, extends further into history, or points to a future, end-times power modeled on Rome's own pattern. The specific identification of 'Babylon' with the Roman Catholic Church, historically prominent among Reformation-era Protestants (reflected, for example, in some early Protestant confessional documents) and still held by some Protestant traditions today, arose within a specific, polemical historical context of Reformation-era conflict between Protestant reformers and the papacy, and has been widely criticized, including by many Protestant scholars themselves, as reading a specific institution of a much later era into a text whose own explicit, immediate historical markers (the seven hills, the persecuting political power of John's own day) point most directly to first-century imperial Rome rather than to any specific later Christian institution. This project does not adjudicate the underlying question of Revelation's fullest scope and application, but notes candidly, consistent with its treatment of comparably historically fraught interpretive questions elsewhere in this series, that this specific identification carries real, documented historical baggage from Reformation-era polemics that responsible contemporary interpretation should acknowledge directly rather than pass over in silence.

## Literary Composition

### 7. 'John' -- the apostle, or a distinct 'John the Elder'? The same authorship complexity already addressed regarding 2 and 3 John

**References:** Revelation 1:1, 4, 9

**The claim:** Revelation identifies its own author simply as 'John' (1:1, 4, 9), a servant of God who received this vision while exiled 'on the island called Patmos.' Tradition has long identified this John with the apostle, author of the fourth Gospel and the three Johannine letters, but this identification connects directly to the same 'two Johns' question addressed at length in the companion 2 John document -- notably, the fourth-century historian Eusebius, who explicitly distinguished a separate 'John the Elder' from the apostle John based on his reading of the second-century bishop Papias, specifically credited this distinct 'Elder' (not the apostle) with authorship of Revelation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Revelation 1:1, 4, and 9 exactly as given, identifying the author simply as 'John' without further specification; this authorship question is a matter of historical and literary scholarship extending beyond the text itself, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at length in the companion 2 John document, which addresses the same underlying 'two Johns' puzzle and its dependence on Eusebius's own interpretation of Papias's fragmentary, now-lost original statement. This project notes an additional wrinkle specific to Revelation: ancient opinion on this book's authorship was itself notably divided beyond just the 'two Johns' question -- some ancient critics (including, prominently, Dionysius of Alexandria in the third century, as reported by Eusebius) argued from the book's own distinctive Greek style and vocabulary, markedly different from the fourth Gospel and 1 John, that Revelation's author could not be the same person who wrote those other Johannine texts, whoever that author (apostle or elder) may have been -- a stylistic argument textual and literary scholars continue to discuss today, independent of and in addition to the specific patristic testimony regarding two distinct historical Johns. This project does not adjudicate either dimension of this authorship question, consistent with its treatment of the comparable puzzle in the companion 2 John document.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 8. The two witnesses (11:1-13) -- Moses and Elijah, Enoch and Elijah, or symbolic figures?

**References:** Revelation 11:3-13

**The claim:** Revelation 11:3-13 describes two unnamed 'witnesses' who prophesy for 1,260 days, are killed by 'the beast,' and are then miraculously resurrected and taken up to heaven before onlooking crowds -- figures whose specific identity Revelation itself never names, generating several distinct proposed identifications.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Revelation 11:3-13 plainly and consistently as given, without naming the two witnesses in the text itself; their specific identity remains genuinely debated regardless of translation, and is not resolved by any of the ten translations' specific wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated identification question, and this entry presents the leading proposals rather than a single confident answer. Some commentators identify the two witnesses as Moses and Elijah specifically, noting the witnesses' own described powers directly parallel each figure's own biblical ministry (11:6's power to 'shut the sky, that no rain may fall,' matching Elijah's own miracle in 1 Kings 17, and power to 'turn [waters] into blood and to strike the earth with every kind of plague,' matching Moses' own miracles in Exodus) -- a reading with some further support from both figures' appearance together with Jesus at the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:3). Others propose Enoch and Elijah specifically, based on a documented Jewish and early Christian tradition (reflected in some patristic sources) holding that these two figures, alone among all humanity, had not experienced ordinary death (Enoch's translation in Genesis 5:24; Elijah's ascent in 2 Kings 2:11), making them uniquely appropriate candidates to return and experience a genuine, visible death as martyred witnesses. Still other commentators, particularly those working from an idealist or amillennial framework (addressed in entry 1 above), read the 'two witnesses' symbolically, representing the church's own faithful prophetic testimony throughout the church age (the number two reflecting the biblical requirement of at least two witnesses to establish truth, Deuteronomy 19:15) rather than two specific individual historical figures at all. This project does not adjudicate between these proposals, noting this remains a genuinely open interpretive question.

### 9. 'His share in the tree of life' -- or 'the book of life'? A textual variant traceable to a single historical event

**References:** Revelation 22:19

**The claim:** Revelation 22:19 warns that anyone who takes away from the words of this book's prophecy will have their 'share' or 'part' removed from something -- but the ancient manuscript evidence genuinely diverges on what that something is. The overwhelming majority of Greek manuscripts, including all the earliest and most reliable witnesses, read 'the tree of life' (apo tou xylou tes zoes), directly echoing the tree of life imagery running throughout Revelation's closing chapters (22:2, 14). The Textus Receptus -- and, in turn, the KJV and NKJV -- instead reads 'the book of life' (apo biblou tes zoes). This specific reading traces to a documented historical circumstance: when Desiderius Erasmus compiled his 1516 Greek New Testament, the single manuscript available to him for Revelation was missing its final leaf, lacking the last six verses of the book entirely. Erasmus supplied this missing text by translating backward from the Latin Vulgate into Greek, and the particular Vulgate copy he used read 'liber vitae' ('book of life') at this point -- a reading that had itself entered the Latin textual tradition centuries earlier, most likely through a scribal confusion between similar Latin words for 'book' and 'tree.' This makes Revelation 22:19 one of the clearest documented cases in the entire New Testament of a specific reading's origin being traceable to a single, identifiable historical event in the history of textual transmission.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads 'God will take away his share in the tree of life' (verified against the published text), following the modern critical Greek text.
- **CSB:** CSB reads 'God will take away his share of the tree of life' (verified against the published text), following the modern critical Greek text.
- **ESV:** ESV reads 'God will take away his share in the tree of life and in the holy city' (verified against the published text), following the modern critical Greek text.
- **KJV:** KJV reads 'God shall take away his part out of the book of life' (verified against the published text), following the Textus Receptus. Erasmus lacked a Greek manuscript for the last six verses of Revelation when preparing his 1516 edition and back-translated this portion from the Latin Vulgate into Greek; his Vulgate copy read 'book of life' rather than 'tree of life,' likely due to an early intra-Latin confusion between similar-looking words for 'book' and 'tree.' (A minority of KJV defenders dispute that Erasmus relied purely on the Vulgate here, noting a small number of late Greek minuscules -- such as 2049 and 296 -- that also carry this reading, though the overwhelming majority of Greek manuscript evidence, including all the earliest witnesses, reads 'tree of life.')
- **NKJV:** NKJV likewise reads 'God shall take away his part from the Book of Life' (verified against the published text), following the same Textus Receptus tradition as KJV, typically with a footnote noting the manuscript variant.
- **NASB:** NASB reads 'God will take away his part from the tree of life' (verified against the published text), following the modern critical Greek text.
- **LSB:** LSB reads 'God will take away his part from the tree of life' (verified against the published text); LSB's New Testament is based on the modern critical Greek text (Novum Testamentum Graece), not the Textus Receptus, so it follows the same reading as NASB, ESV, NIV, CSB, and NLT here.
- **NIV:** NIV reads 'God will take away that person's share in the tree of life and in the holy city' (verified against the published text), following the modern critical Greek text.
- **NLT:** NLT reads 'God will remove that person's share in the tree of life and in the holy city' (verified against the published text), following the modern critical Greek text.
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV reads 'God will take away his share in the tree of life and in the holy city' (verified against the published RSV text), following the modern critical Greek text; RSV2CE's New Testament preserves this reading.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated in this project not as a contradiction between translations but as a significant, well-documented manuscript-based textual variant, comparable in kind to Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11, and 1 John 5:7-8 (all addressed in their own companion documents or entries), and, like the Comma Johanneum in 1 John, directly traceable to Erasmus's editorial choices in producing the first printed Greek New Testament. KJV and NKJV, translating from the Textus Receptus, read 'book of life'; ESV, NASB, NIV, CSB, NLT, LSB, AMP, and RSV2CE all follow the modern critical Greek text (reflecting the earliest and most numerous manuscript witnesses, none of which support the Vulgate-derived reading) and read 'tree of life.' This project takes no position on which reading modern readers should prefer, noting only that the underlying facts of the variant's origin are unusually well documented compared to most textual questions addressed in this project, since Erasmus's own working method at this specific point in his text is independently attested rather than merely inferred.
