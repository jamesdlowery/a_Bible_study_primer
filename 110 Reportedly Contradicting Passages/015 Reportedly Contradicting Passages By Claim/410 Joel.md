# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Joel

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable interpretive
questions involving the book of Joel, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison
with Acts, Romans, and the companion Isaiah document's treatment of the shared "swords into
plowshares" oracle. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised,
notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the
translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in
English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Joel is a very short book (3-4 chapters depending on versification). This
document has 6 entries rather than the full 12 used for longer books in this series,
consistent with the approach already taken for Ruth, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, Song of Solomon,
and Lamentations.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1 and 6 (the literal-versus-symbolic locust plague, and the
book's dating) are both presented with genuine scholarly diversity of opinion rather than a
single forced resolution -- Joel's dating in particular spans one of the widest ranges of any
book in the Old Testament, a genuine and openly acknowledged uncertainty rather than a settled
matter.

**Verification status:** Entry 6's claims about the range and basis of scholarly dating
proposals were checked against a deliberately wide range of sources for this document. The
remaining entries are theological and literary questions where all ten translations render the
same underlying text and the harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Interpretation

### 1. The locust plague: a literal insect infestation, a symbolic military invasion, or both?

**References:** Joel 1:2-2:11

**The claim:** Joel opens with an extended, vivid description of a devastating locust plague (1:2-20), described in agricultural and ecological terms that read as a literal natural disaster. Chapter 2, however, describes what appears to be the same or a related event in unmistakably military language: an invading force 'like a mighty army... they run like mighty men; they climb the wall like soldiers... like war horses they run... with the rumbling of chariots' (2:2-9), raising a question about whether Joel is describing a literal locust plague, a literal military invasion described through locust imagery, or a literal locust plague functioning as a springboard for prophesying a future, greater military 'day of the LORD.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both chapters plainly and consistently as given, without resolving the literal-versus-symbolic question or explicitly identifying the locusts with a human army in the text itself; this is a matter of literary and prophetic interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and openly debated interpretive question, and this entry does not force a single resolution, since serious commentators hold several different positions. Some read the entire book as describing a single, literal, historical locust plague (a well-attested and devastating natural phenomenon in the ancient Near East, with locust swarms sometimes described by observers, including in early twentieth-century accounts from the same region, using strikingly similar military metaphors purely because of their overwhelming, disciplined-seeming, unstoppable advance) -- on this reading, the military language throughout chapter 2 is the prophet's own vivid poetic description of the locusts themselves, not a coded reference to a separate human army. Others read the locust plague of chapter 1 as a real, recent historical disaster that Joel then uses as a springboard and pattern for prophesying a future, far worse day of the LORD involving an actual human military invasion (some identifying this future army as Assyrian, Babylonian, or a more distant eschatological force), with chapter 2's military imagery describing that future army directly rather than continuing to describe locusts. A third position holds the entire book operates at a symbolic or apocalyptic level throughout, using the locust plague primarily as a literary and theological image for divine judgment in general, without requiring either a specific historical locust swarm or a specific historical army to be identified as the passage's exclusive, literal referent.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. 'I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh' -- and Peter's citation at Pentecost

**References:** Joel 2:28-32; Acts 2:16-21

**The claim:** Joel 2:28-32 promises a future outpouring of God's Spirit 'on all flesh,' accompanied by prophecy, dreams, and visions, alongside cosmic signs -- 'blood and fire and columns of smoke... the sun shall be turned to darkness, and the moon to blood.' Peter, in his Pentecost sermon (Acts 2:16-21), quotes this passage at length and declares, 'this is what was uttered through the prophet Joel,' applying it directly to the events of Pentecost -- yet the specific cosmic signs Joel describes (the sun darkened, the moon turned to blood) were not literally, visibly manifest at Pentecost itself, raising a question about the nature and completeness of Peter's claimed fulfillment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Joel 2:28-32 and Acts 2:16-21 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing an inaugurated, 'already but not yet' pattern of fulfillment, addressed at several points elsewhere in this project, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Peter's citation as identifying Pentecost as the genuine beginning of the fulfillment Joel's prophecy anticipates, rather than claiming every specific detail of the prophecy (including its cosmic, apocalyptic imagery) was completed in that single event -- a pattern of inaugurated, 'already but not yet' fulfillment addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding prophecy whose complete realization unfolds across an extended period rather than in one instant. On this reading, the outpouring of the Spirit on all people (not merely priests, prophets, or kings, as in the Old Testament's more restricted pattern of Spirit-empowerment) genuinely began at Pentecost and continues through the church age Peter's sermon inaugurates, while the passage's more explicitly apocalyptic cosmic imagery (widely recognized elsewhere in prophetic literature as conventional language for describing climactic divine judgment and the created order's dramatic response to it, rather than requiring strictly literal astronomical events) is generally understood to await a fuller, final realization at a later point still associated with 'the day of the LORD' Joel's own passage names -- Peter's sermon itself continues, in the same speech, to discuss Jesus' resurrection and coming judgment, suggesting his own understanding of the citation as inaugurating rather than completely exhausting the prophecy's full scope.

### 3. 'Everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved' -- YHWH's name applied to Jesus

**References:** Joel 2:32; Romans 10:9-13

**The claim:** Joel 2:32 promises, 'everyone who calls on the name of the LORD [YHWH] shall be saved.' Paul quotes this verse directly in Romans 10:13, applying it in a context explicitly about confessing 'Jesus is Lord' and believing in his resurrection for salvation (10:9) -- directly identifying Jesus with the object of the Old Testament's own exclusive covenant name for God, a significant Christological application of an Old Testament text originally addressed to calling on YHWH specifically.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Joel 2:32 and Romans 10:9-13 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing a deliberate and theologically significant New Testament pattern of applying YHWH-language to Jesus, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as one of the New Testament's clearest and most direct examples of applying Old Testament language specifically reserved for YHWH to Jesus, functioning as a significant piece of the biblical basis for later, more developed Trinitarian theology -- Paul's argument in Romans 10 explicitly moves from 'confess... that Jesus is Lord' (10:9, using the Greek kyrios, the standard Septuagint rendering of YHWH throughout the Old Testament) directly into quoting Joel's promise about calling on 'the name of the LORD,' treating the two as describing the same act and the same divine object of address without any indication that Paul understood this as a strained or merely analogical application. This pattern -- New Testament writers directly and deliberately applying language and titles exclusively used of YHWH in the Old Testament to Jesus -- appears at several other points in the New Testament (compare the discussion of Isaiah 6 and John 12:41 in the companion Isaiah document, and Philippians 2:9-11's application of Isaiah 45:23's 'every knee shall bow' language to Jesus) and is generally read by conservative and mainstream scholars as reflecting the New Testament's own developed conviction of Jesus' full deity, rather than an isolated or idiosyncratic reinterpretation of a single verse.

### 4. The Valley of Jehoshaphat -- a literal geographic location, or a symbolic name for judgment?

**References:** Joel 3:2, 12, 14

**The claim:** Joel 3:2 and 3:12 both describe God gathering all the nations for judgment in 'the Valley of Jehoshaphat,' with 3:14 renaming the same location 'the valley of decision' -- raising a question about whether this refers to an actual, identifiable geographic valley (some later tradition identifies it with the Kidron Valley just outside Jerusalem, and the name has been used for a specific valley since at least the fourth century AD) or is a symbolic name invented for this prophecy specifically, since 'Jehoshaphat' itself means 'the LORD judges' in Hebrew -- making the location's very name a description of its prophesied function.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'the Valley of Jehoshaphat' and 'the valley of decision' consistently at each of these verses, without resolving the literal-versus-symbolic question in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and literary interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators favor reading 'the Valley of Jehoshaphat' as a symbolic name coined for this specific prophecy rather than a reference to an already-existing, previously named geographic location -- no valley by this specific name is independently attested prior to Joel's own use of it, and the name's transparent meaning ('the LORD judges'), combined with the passage's own explicit renaming of the same location as 'the valley of decision' just two verses later (3:14), strongly suggests the name functions primarily as a theological description of the event (a place where the LORD will judge, and where a decisive verdict will be rendered) rather than as a specific, pre-existing geographic identifier the original audience would already have recognized. The later Jewish and Christian tradition identifying this valley with the Kidron Valley (a tradition attested from at least the early Byzantine period onward) is generally read by scholars as a subsequent, devotionally motivated geographic application of Joel's symbolic name to an actual location near Jerusalem, rather than as evidence that Joel himself was referring to an already-known valley by that name.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 5. 'Beat your plowshares into swords' -- the direct inversion of Isaiah's and Micah's famous vision

**References:** Joel 3:9-10; Isaiah 2:2-4; Micah 4:1-3

**The claim:** Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3 (addressed together in the companion Isaiah document as a shared oracle appearing in both books) famously envision a future era of universal peace in which the nations 'shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks.' Joel 3:9-10 issues the precisely opposite instruction: 'Beat your plowshares into swords, and your pruning hooks into spears' -- an apparently direct, deliberate inversion of the more famous vision, raising a question about whether the prophets are offering contradictory visions of the future.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Joel 3:9-10, Isaiah 2:4, and Micah 4:3 plainly and consistently as given, preserving the deliberate verbal inversion between the passages; the harmonization rests on recognizing Joel's ironic address to a specific, doomed audience rather than a competing vision of history's final outcome, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as a genuine contradiction between the prophets' visions of history's ultimate outcome, but as Joel's own deliberate, ironic literary allusion addressed to a different moment and a different audience within the same broader prophetic framework: Isaiah's and Micah's vision describes a final, ultimate era of universal peace among the nations under God's own reign, while Joel 3:9-10 is addressed specifically to the surrounding pagan nations, summoning them (in a tone of grim, ironic mockery, 'proclaim this among the nations: consecrate for war... let the weak say, I am a warrior') to muster for a specific, climactic battle of judgment in which God himself will defeat them (the 'valley of decision,' 3:14, addressed further in entry 4 below) -- a summons to a losing battle, not a genuine, sincerely intended call to arms that competes with or reverses Isaiah's ultimate vision of peace. On this reading, Joel's inversion of the famous phrase is a deliberate rhetorical device, ironically calling doomed nations to their own destruction using language that pointedly echoes, rather than genuinely competes with, the prophets' shared ultimate hope for peace under God's reign.

## Literary Composition

### 6. Joel's dating: one of the widest, most genuinely uncertain ranges of any book in the Old Testament

**References:** Joel 1:1

**The claim:** Joel 1:1 identifies the prophet only as 'Joel, the son of Pethuel,' supplying no reference to any reigning king or other clear historical anchor point -- a marked contrast with most other prophetic books, which typically date themselves by naming the kings under whom the prophet ministered (compare Hosea 1:1, Amos 1:1). As a result, scholarly proposals for the book's date of composition span an extraordinarily wide range, from the ninth century BC (making Joel among the very earliest of the recorded prophets) to the fourth century BC or even later (making it among the very latest).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Joel 1:1 exactly as given, without adding any dating information not present in the Hebrew text itself; the dating question addressed here is a matter of historical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction but a genuinely and widely acknowledged case of historical uncertainty, and this entry does not force a single resolution, since the arguments on each side are seriously debated among careful scholars. Arguments for an early, pre-exilic date (commonly associated with the early reign of the boy-king Joash, guided by the priest Jehoiada, roughly 835 BC) include: the book's classical Hebrew style, more consistent with pre-exilic rather than later Hebrew; the specific list of foreign enemies named (Phoenicia, Philistia, Egypt, Edom, and the Sabeans, 3:4-8), which are all peoples prominent in earlier periods rather than the Assyrians or Babylonians who dominate later pre-exilic prophecy; and the fact that several other prophets (Amos, and possibly Isaiah) appear to directly quote specific verses from Joel, which would require Joel to have been written first. Arguments for a later, post-exilic date include: the absence of the northern kingdom of Israel as a separate political entity (with 'Israel' used as a synonym for Judah, 2:27, 3:2, 16); the absence of any mention of a king, more naturally explained if the monarchy had already ended; the book's strong emphasis on Temple ritual, the priesthood, and communal fasting, characteristic of post-exilic religious life; and the possibility that Joel, rather than being quoted by other prophets, was himself quoting them. Both sets of arguments have been defended at length by serious scholars, and the absence of decisive internal evidence means this question is likely to remain genuinely unresolved.
