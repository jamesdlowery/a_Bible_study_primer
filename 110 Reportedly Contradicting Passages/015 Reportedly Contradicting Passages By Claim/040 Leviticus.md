# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Leviticus

This document catalogs 20 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Leviticus, either entirely internal to Leviticus or between Leviticus and a directly related
passage elsewhere (Exodus, Genesis, Matthew, Luke, Mark, Acts, 1 Timothy, Hebrews, Galatians).
For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the
**KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own
wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives
the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Natural History and Dietary Law

### 1. Is the bat classified as a bird?

**References:** Leviticus 11:13-19

**The claim:** The dietary law lists the bat alongside various birds in a section headed (in some translations) by the word 'fowls' or 'birds,' which modern zoological classification would not do, since bats are mammals.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV heads the list, 'these are they which ye shall have in abomination among the fowls,' using the narrower English word 'fowls,' and includes 'the bat' at the end of the list (11:19).

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew word here (oph) does not correspond precisely to the modern biological taxonomic category 'bird' but more broadly denotes flying creatures generally -- a functional, observation-based grouping (things that fly) common to ancient classification systems worldwide, rather than a claim about modern biological classification by reproductive or skeletal criteria. Several modern translations render the heading with a broader term like 'flying creatures' or 'winged creatures' specifically to avoid the misleading impression that the text is making a strict zoological claim.

### 2. 'Four-footed' flying insects?

**References:** Leviticus 11:20-23

**The claim:** The KJV describes certain 'creeping things' (insects) as going 'upon all four,' which some read as a factual claim that insects have exactly four legs, when they have six.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'flying insect that walks on all fours,' with bracketed amplification clarifying this refers to insects that move along the ground.
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'all winged insects that walk on all fours.'
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'winged insects that go on all fours,' also retaining the phrase but applying it to insects specifically rather than 'fowls.'
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'all fowls that creep, going upon all four,' the wording most often cited in this claim.
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'flying insects that creep on all fours,' retaining similar language to the KJV.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'the swarming things...which walk on all fours,' similarly retained.
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'winged insects that walk on all fours,' tracking NASB/ESV.
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'all flying insects that walk on all fours,' retaining the phrase.
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'winged insects that walk along the ground,' removing the 'four' figure from the English text entirely.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders 'winged insects that go on all fours.'

**Reconciliation:** The phrase describes flying/swarming insects that also move by walking or hopping along the ground on jointed legs -- 'upon all four' (an idiom for going on all fours, i.e., not walking upright) versus the specific exception carved out immediately afterward for insects 'which have legs above their feet, to leap withal' (locusts, etc.). It is best read as a general, non-technical description of insects' characteristic ground-level, multi-legged locomotion (as opposed to flying or upright movement), not a scientific claim about an exact leg count -- and most modern translations render the passage in a way that removes the 'four' figure from English entirely.

### 3. Do rabbits and rock badgers actually 'chew the cud'?

**References:** Leviticus 11:5-6

**The claim:** Leviticus 11:5-6 lists the 'coney'/'rock badger'/'hyrax' and the 'hare'/'rabbit' among animals that 'chew the cud' but do not have a divided hoof, and are therefore unclean. Neither animal is a true ruminant in the modern zoological sense (ruminants have multi-chambered stomachs and regurgitate a genuine cud), which some read as a scientific error in the text.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV names both animals 'the coney' (11:5) and 'the hare' (11:6), and both are described as chewing 'the cud' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Both the rock hyrax and the rabbit/hare family practice behaviors that closely resemble cud-chewing to an observer without modern digestive physiology: hyraxes constantly move their jaws in a chewing-like motion (unrelated to digestion, but visually identical to chewing a cud), and rabbits and hares practice cecotrophy (re-ingesting a special soft fecal pellet produced in the cecum), which involves visible chewing and produces a functionally similar outcome to rumination -- extracting further nutrition from partially digested plant matter. The Hebrew phrase is best understood as a phenomenological, observation-based description (what the animal visibly appears to do) consistent with how ancient classification systems generally worked, rather than a claim keyed to the internal, multi-chambered ruminant digestive anatomy unknown to any ancient observer.

## Law and Ethics

### 4. 'Eye for eye, tooth for tooth' -- literal retaliation, or a legal principle of proportional compensation?

**References:** Leviticus 24:19-20; Exodus 21:23-25; Matthew 5:38-39

**The claim:** Leviticus 24:19-20 (echoing Exodus 21:23-25) prescribes 'fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth' -- read by some as mandating literal physical mutilation as punishment. Jesus in Matthew 5:38-39 tells his followers not to resist an evildoer, but to turn the other cheek, seemingly overturning this principle.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Leviticus 24:19-20 and Matthew 5:38-39 plainly as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing judicial law from personal ethic (and, for the literal/monetary question, on later legal-historical interpretation), not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The lex talionis in Leviticus and Exodus is widely understood by scholars, including within the later rabbinic legal tradition (which came to interpret these verses as establishing monetary compensation calculated according to the value of the harm done, rather than literal bodily mutilation, based on other Torah texts addressing personal injury), as a judicial principle limiting proportionality in a court setting -- preventing escalating private vengeance (a killing in response to a minor injury, for example) rather than mandating identical injury be inflicted in return. Jesus' teaching in Matthew 5 addresses personal conduct toward those who wrong an individual directly, not the operation of a legal system, so the two are addressing different spheres (courts of law versus personal ethics) rather than issuing contradictory commands for the same situation.

### 5. Levitical death penalties for many offenses vs. Jesus and the woman caught in adultery

**References:** Leviticus 20:10, 13, 18; Leviticus 24:16; John 8:1-11

**The claim:** Leviticus prescribes death for numerous offenses, including adultery (20:10) and blasphemy (24:16). In John 8, Jesus is presented with a woman caught in adultery -- a capital offense under this law -- and rather than affirming her execution, tells her accusers 'let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone,' and ultimately tells her, 'neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the Levitical penalty and the Gospel scene plainly as given; the harmonization is exegetical and procedural, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Several harmonizations are commonly offered, and are not mutually exclusive. First, the Mosaic penalty required due process, including the presence and testimony of the male participant -- Leviticus 20:10 explicitly condemns 'both' the adulterer and adulteress -- who is conspicuously absent from the accusers' account in John 8, raising a procedural irregularity in how the woman was brought forward. Second, under Roman occupation, Jewish courts did not have the independent authority to carry out capital sentences (compare John 18:31, where the leaders tell Pilate 'it is not lawful for us to put anyone to death'), making the scene as narrated more a test of Jesus -- explicitly stated in John 8:6, 'This they said to test him' -- than an actual judicial proceeding. Third, and more broadly, Jesus' statement 'neither do I condemn you' is read by most commentators as a decision not to add a further human accusation and death sentence in that specific, procedurally irregular moment, not a repeal of the Law's stated penalty as an abstract matter, since Jesus immediately adds 'go, and sin no more,' affirming that what she did was indeed sin. (This entry does not address the separate, well-documented textual question of whether John 7:53-8:11 was originally part of John's Gospel, which the general document set covers.)

### 6. Close-relative marriage prohibited by the Law -- yet practiced by the patriarchs before it

**References:** Leviticus 18:9; Leviticus 18:12; Genesis 20:12; Exodus 6:20

**The claim:** Leviticus 18:9 prohibits marrying a sister or half-sister; Leviticus 18:12 prohibits marrying a father's sister (a paternal aunt). Yet Abraham states his wife Sarah is 'the daughter of my father, though not the daughter of my mother' -- his half-sister (Genesis 20:12) -- and Moses' father Amram married his own paternal aunt, Jochebed (Exodus 6:20), both without any narrative censure.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render all three passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns the timing of the Law relative to the patriarchal and early Exodus narratives, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As addressed in more detail in the Exodus-specific companion document for the Amram/Jochebed case, the standard harmonization simply notes that the Levitical prohibitions were given generations after both marriages occurred, at Sinai -- the Law's provisions, like several others (its regulation of polygamy, for instance), applied prospectively from the point they were given and did not retroactively criminalize marriages already contracted under earlier, less restrictive ancient Near Eastern customs. Neither Genesis nor Exodus presents either marriage as violating a legal code that, narratively, does not yet exist at the time each marriage took place.

### 7. Different purification periods prescribed for a mother after the birth of a son versus a daughter

**References:** Leviticus 12:2-5

**The claim:** Leviticus 12 prescribes a period of ceremonial impurity and purification for a mother following childbirth: seven days of initial impurity plus thirty-three days of purification (forty days total) after a son's birth, but fourteen days of initial impurity plus sixty-six days of purification (eighty days total) after a daughter's birth -- a doubled period for a female child, which some read as reflecting or teaching a devaluation of female life.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the seven/thirty-three and fourteen/sixty-six day figures identically and consistently as given; there is no translation variance on the underlying numbers or terms in this passage.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators generally distinguish two different questions here: what the text's ritual-impurity provisions meant within Israel's own conceptual system, versus a modern ethical evaluation of any implied valuation of persons. On the first question, the majority scholarly view holds that Levitical ritual impurity is not a moral category (it does not indicate sin or lesser worth -- childbirth itself, an unambiguous blessing throughout the Old Testament, is what triggers the impurity in both cases) but a symbolic system tracking events involving blood, death, and the loss of life-force in the ancient Israelite conceptual world; several explanations for the specific doubling with a female child have been offered (a longer symbolic period tied to the daughter's own future reproductive capacity, ancient beliefs about gestational or vaginal blood-flow patterns after each type of birth, or a stylized doubling of the base seven/thirty-three unit without an explicit stated rationale in the text itself), but Scripture does not supply a definitive stated reason, and interpreters differ on how satisfying any of these explanations are.

### 8. 'Love your neighbor as yourself' (Leviticus 19:18) -- originally scoped to fellow Israelites, or universal?

**References:** Leviticus 19:18; Leviticus 19:33-34; Matthew 22:39; Luke 10:25-37

**The claim:** Leviticus 19:18 commands, 'you shall love your neighbor as yourself,' but its immediate context restricts the preceding prohibitions (against vengeance and grudge-bearing) to 'the sons of your own people' -- suggesting the command's original scope may have been limited to fellow Israelites. Jesus cites this verse as the second-greatest commandment (Matthew 22:39) and, when asked directly 'who is my neighbor?', answers with the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37), which extends the category to include even a member of a group (Samaritans) that many of Jesus' Jewish contemporaries did not consider covenant kin -- seemingly broadening the command's scope well beyond its original context.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders 'the children of thy people' at 19:18.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that Leviticus 19 itself, only fifteen verses later, extends the identical command to a non-Israelite: 'the stranger who sojourns with you... you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt' (19:33-34) -- meaning the chapter's own internal logic already moves outward from covenant-community solidarity toward a broader neighbor-love grounded in Israel's own experience as outsiders, rather than treating 'neighbor' as forever fixed to ethnic kin alone. On this reading, Jesus' parable in Luke 10 is best understood as drawing out and sharpening a trajectory already present within Leviticus 19's own structure (vv. 18 and 33-34 read together), rather than introducing a foreign, contradictory universalism the original text rules out.

### 9. Slavery regulated by the Law -- including provisions specific to Leviticus -- vs. 'neither slave nor free' in Christ

**References:** Leviticus 25:39-46; Exodus 21:2-11; Galatians 3:28

**The claim:** Leviticus 25:44-46 permits Israelites to acquire and hold as permanent property slaves purchased 'from the nations that are around you,' explicitly distinguishing this from the more limited, time-bound servitude permitted for fellow Israelites in the same chapter (25:39-43); Paul states there is 'neither slave nor free' in Christ (Galatians 3:28).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Leviticus 25:44-46's distinct provisions for foreign versus Israelite servants, and Galatians 3:28, plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is a matter of historical and ethical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Scholars broadly distinguish the Mosaic provisions -- which regulate and substantially limit an already-existing ancient Near Eastern institution, including mandatory release, protections against abuse, and (for fellow Israelites specifically) a required release in the Jubilee year (Leviticus 25:40-41) -- from either an endorsement of slavery as an ideal or a claim that it is compatible with the gospel's full outworking. Paul's Galatians 3:28 describes a spiritual and relational equality 'in Christ' that many historians and theologians read as planting a trajectory that, over time and considerable historical struggle, contributed to the eventual undermining of the institution altogether, even though the New Testament does not issue an immediate, blanket legal abolition within the Roman economic order of its own day.

### 10. Are people commanded to judge righteously, or told not to judge at all?

**References:** Leviticus 19:15; Matthew 7:1-5; John 7:24

**The claim:** Leviticus 19:15 commands, 'in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour,' explicitly requiring impartial judgment rather than prohibiting judgment as such. Jesus, in the Sermon on the Mount, says, 'Judge not, that ye be not judged' (Matthew 7:1) -- language some read as a flat prohibition on judging others at all, in apparent tension with the Old Testament's repeated commands to render righteous judgment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jesus' own later statement in John 7:24 -- 'judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment' -- directly echoes Leviticus 19:15's own language and shows that Jesus is not prohibiting judgment as such; read alongside Matthew 7:1's immediate context (verses 2-5 warn specifically against hypocritical, self-righteous, hair-splitting condemnation of others while ignoring one's own greater faults, 'the beam in thine own eye'), the standard harmonization holds that Matthew 7:1 targets a particular manner of judging -- harsh, hypocritical, and self-exalting -- not the exercise of moral discernment and righteous judgment that Leviticus 19:15, John 7:24, and numerous other New Testament texts (e.g., 1 Corinthians 5:12-13; John 8:15-16, where Jesus distinguishes 'judging after the flesh' from his own true judgment) continue to require of God's people.

### 11. Should we love our brother, or are there brothers and enemies Scripture commands us to hate?

**References:** Leviticus 19:17-18; Psalm 139:21-22; Luke 14:26; Matthew 5:43-44

**The claim:** Leviticus 19:17-18 commands, 'thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart... thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.' Yet the Psalmist declares, 'Do not I hate them, O LORD, that hate thee?... I hate them with perfect hatred' (Psalm 139:21-22), and Jesus tells his followers that whoever comes to him and does not 'hate' father, mother, and even his own life 'cannot be my disciple' (Luke 14:26) -- language some read as commanding hatred in tension with Leviticus's prohibition.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization reads each 'hatred' text in its own specific context rather than as a blanket command toward persons as such: Psalm 139's hatred is directed at God's enemies precisely as enemies of God and of righteousness (a zeal for God's honor rather than personal animosity, and itself balanced elsewhere in the Psalms by love and prayer even for personal enemies, e.g. Psalm 35:13-14), a category distinct from the 'brother' and 'neighbour' Leviticus 19 has in view. Luke 14:26's 'hate' is widely recognized as a Semitic idiom of comparative preference (found elsewhere in Scripture, e.g. Genesis 29:31's 'Leah was hated' meaning simply loved less than Rachel, and clarified by the parallel account in Matthew 10:37, 'he that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy of me') -- meaning that loyalty to Christ must exceed even the strongest natural family loyalty, not that literal hatred of one's family is required, which would directly contradict Jesus' own repeated commands to love one's family and even one's enemies (Matthew 5:43-44).

### 12. Does God love everyone, given his stated abhorrence of certain nations and practices?

**References:** Leviticus 20:22-23; John 3:16; 1 John 4:8

**The claim:** Leviticus 20:22-23 has God telling Israel that the nations he is casting out are abhorred specifically 'because they committed all these things,' and warns Israel against following the same practices. John 3:16 states 'God so loved the world,' and 1 John 4:8 declares simply, 'God is love' -- language some read as describing an unconditional divine love in tension with the abhorrence expressed in Leviticus.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard theological distinction, held broadly across the conservative tradition, is between God's universal love of benevolence toward all his creatures (reflected in John 3:16's 'the world,' and in God's common care for all people, Matthew 5:45) and his response of abhorrence toward specific, entrenched sinful practices and the persistent, unrepentant societies built around them (the specific practices catalogued in the surrounding chapters of Leviticus, including child sacrifice and various sexual practices the text elsewhere details) -- a moral revulsion at evil that Scripture treats as fully compatible with, rather than opposed to, God's love, in the same way a loving parent can love a child while still abhorring the child's harmful and self-destructive behavior. Leviticus 20:22-23 itself frames the coming judgment as a consequence of specific practices rather than a comment on God's disposition toward the nations' inhabitants as such, and the same Pentateuch that records this warning also records God's compassion extended to non-Israelites throughout its narrative (e.g., Rahab in the conquest narrative, or the repeated instruction to show kindness to the 'stranger').

### 13. Should believers fear God, or has perfect love cast out fear?

**References:** Leviticus 25:17; 1 John 4:18; Romans 8:15

**The claim:** Leviticus 25:17 commands, 'ye shall not therefore oppress one another; but thou shalt fear thy God.' 1 John 4:18 states that 'perfect love casteth out fear... he that feareth is not made perfect in love,' and Romans 8:15 states believers 'have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators widely distinguish two different senses of 'fear' addressed by these texts, a distinction the Bible itself maintains elsewhere (e.g., Proverbs 1:7's 'the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge,' paired with the confident access of a child before a father, Psalm 103:13): a reverential, worshipful awe and recognition of God's holiness, majesty, and authority -- the sense intended in Leviticus 25:17 and throughout the wisdom literature, and never abolished for believers -- versus the anxious, servile dread of condemnation and punishment that 1 John 4:18 and Romans 8:15 describe as removed for those who belong to God through Christ. The 'spirit of bondage' Romans 8:15 sets aside is specifically a slave's terror before a master who might condemn him, replaced by 'the spirit of adoption,' a son or daughter's confident relationship with a father -- a change in the relational basis of the fear, not a claim that reverence, awe, and moral seriousness before a holy God are no longer appropriate, which the New Testament itself continues to commend (e.g., 2 Corinthians 7:1; Philippians 2:12; 1 Peter 1:17).

## Law and Covenant Structure

### 14. 'Perpetual'/'everlasting' priesthood and sacrifices vs. their obsolescence in Hebrews

**References:** Leviticus 6:18; Leviticus 7:34-36; Leviticus 16:29-34; Leviticus 24:8; Hebrews 7:11-19; Hebrews 8:13; Hebrews 10:1-14

**The claim:** Leviticus repeatedly describes the priestly portions, the Day of Atonement observance, and other cultic provisions as 'a perpetual statute' or 'an everlasting covenant' (terms appearing at 6:18, 7:34-36, 16:29-34, 24:8, and elsewhere). Hebrews explicitly teaches that the Levitical priesthood has been changed and its associated law set aside (7:11-19), that the first covenant is 'becoming obsolete' and 'ready to vanish away' (8:13), and that the sacrifices it prescribed have been replaced by Christ's single, sufficient sacrifice (10:1-14) -- seemingly contradicting the 'perpetual'/'everlasting' language.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP's expanded renderings at some of these verses include bracketed clarification tying the term to the duration of the Levitical order, reflecting the 'until Christ' understanding more explicitly in the translation itself.
- **CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE:** All ten translations render the relevant Leviticus passages with 'perpetual' or 'everlasting' (or a close synonym) and render the Hebrews passages plainly as given; the harmonization concerns the semantic range of the Hebrew term and Hebrews' own typological argument, not translation choice.
- **NASB:** NASB renders the Hebrew olam as 'perpetual' or 'everlasting' depending on context, consistent with most other translations; its footnotes do not typically flag the semantic-range nuance discussed above.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization rests on the semantic range of the underlying Hebrew word olam, most often translated 'everlasting' or 'perpetual': olam frequently means 'for a long, indefinite age' or 'for the duration of the relevant era/administration' rather than 'for all eternity without any possible end' -- the same word describes, for example, an 'everlasting' possession of the land (Genesis 17:8) or a slave serving 'forever' (Exodus 21:6), both understood by context to have real, bounded endpoints. Hebrews' own argument does not claim the Old Testament language was false; rather, it argues that the entire Levitical system was always understood (even within the Old Testament's own typology, Hebrews contends) as a temporary arrangement pointing toward a greater, final reality in Christ, which is why Hebrews 9:10 describes the old covenant's regulations as binding 'until the time of reformation' -- an in-text acknowledgment of the very transition the author is describing, not a claim that Leviticus's 'perpetual' language was simply mistaken.

### 15. Dietary laws (Leviticus 11) vs. the New Testament declaring all foods clean

**References:** Leviticus 11:1-47; Mark 7:18-19; Acts 10:9-16; 1 Timothy 4:3-5

**The claim:** Leviticus 11 lays out an extensive, binding list of clean and unclean animals for food. Mark's Gospel states that Jesus, in his teaching on what defiles a person, 'declared all foods clean' (Mark 7:19). Peter's vision in Acts 10 has a voice tell him, 'What God has made clean, do not call common,' regarding animals Leviticus would classify as unclean. Paul warns against those who forbid foods 'that God created to be received with thanksgiving' (1 Timothy 4:3-5).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Leviticus 11's dietary code, Mark 7:19's parenthetical comment, Acts 10's vision and its own explanation, and 1 Timothy 4:3-5 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is a matter of redemptive-historical and canonical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the priesthood/sacrifice question above, the standard harmonization treats the dietary code as part of the broader Mosaic covenant's ceremonial provisions, which the New Testament consistently describes as having reached their intended purpose in Christ rather than remaining independently binding. Mark's parenthetical comment ('thus he declared all foods clean') is explicitly the Gospel writer's own theological commentary on the significance of Jesus' teaching about internal versus external defilement, written for a later audience. Peter's vision in Acts 10 is itself explicitly interpreted within the same chapter as being about the acceptance of Gentiles rather than a strict, standalone statement about food regulations (Acts 10:28, 'God has shown me that I should not call any person common or unclean'), with the food imagery serving as an object lesson. And Paul's warning in 1 Timothy addresses false teachers imposing needless asceticism as a means of achieving spiritual merit -- a different question from whether a now-fulfilled ceremonial code remains obligatory.

### 16. Must all sacrifices be offered only at the tabernacle, given that patriarchs, judges, and prophets built altars and sacrificed elsewhere?

**References:** Leviticus 17:1-9; Genesis 12:7-8; Genesis 22:9; Judges 6:24-26; 1 Samuel 7:9-10; 1 Kings 18:30-38

**The claim:** Leviticus 17:1-9 commands that any sacrifice be brought specifically to the tabernacle's entrance, under penalty of being 'cut off' from the people -- language read by some as a blanket, permanent centralization requirement. Yet both before and after this law was given, the text records approved sacrifices offered elsewhere: Abraham builds altars across Canaan (Genesis 12:7-8) and offers Isaac on a mountain in Moriah (Genesis 22:9), Gideon sacrifices at Ophrah (Judges 6:24-26), Samuel sacrifices at Mizpah (1 Samuel 7:9-10), and Elijah famously sacrifices on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18:30-38), all without any narrative condemnation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization notes that Leviticus 17's centralization command is addressed to Israel specifically for the period of the wilderness wandering, when the tabernacle was mobile and located at the center of a compact, unified camp -- a situation explicitly different from both the patriarchal period before Sinai (Genesis) and the settlement period after Israel was dispersed across the land in Canaan, when Deuteronomy 12:8-11 itself anticipates and addresses a coming transition ('ye shall not do after all the things that we do here this day, every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes... but when ye go over Jordan... then there shall be a place which the LORD your God shall choose'), permitting local worship until the promised land was settled and a permanent central sanctuary established. Elijah's altar on Carmel (many centuries into that settled period, and during the divided kingdom's crisis of apostasy at the Jerusalem/Bethel/Dan cult centers) is widely read by commentators as an extraordinary, prophetically-authorized exception during a period of national covenant crisis, explicitly vindicated by God's own fire from heaven in the same passage (1 Kings 18:38), rather than a routine violation of the general principle.

### 17. Is Sabbath observance a permanent requirement, or was it fulfilled and set aside in Christ?

**References:** Leviticus 19:3, 30; Exodus 20:8-11; Romans 14:5-6; Colossians 2:16-17

**The claim:** Leviticus 19:3 commands, 'ye shall keep my sabbaths,' repeating the fourth commandment's requirement (Exodus 20:8-11) as a standing part of Israel's law. Paul, writing to New Testament believers, treats the observance of special days as a matter of individual conscience rather than binding obligation (Romans 14:5-6), and explicitly tells the Colossians not to let anyone judge them 'in respect of... the sabbath days,' calling these 'a shadow of things to come' whose substance is found 'in Christ' (Colossians 2:16-17).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is addressed at greater length in the Exodus companion document's treatment of the fourth commandment's place in the broader law; in brief, the standard reconciliation treats the weekly Sabbath, like the rest of the ceremonial and civil provisions given specifically to Israel at Sinai, as part of the Mosaic covenant's sign and shadow structure that the New Testament consistently teaches has reached its intended fulfillment in Christ (Colossians 2:16-17 explicitly uses this 'shadow'/'substance' language). Christian traditions differ on exactly how this bears on Sunday observance and rest in the New Testament era (some holding a moral, creation-based Sabbath principle continues in some form, others holding the entire ceremonial Sabbath law has been set aside), but this variety of positions concerns how to apply the New Testament's teaching, not a disagreement between what Leviticus 19:3 and Colossians 2:16-17 each plainly say about their own respective covenants.

### 18. May a eunuch, or a person with a physical blemish, belong to the assembly of the LORD?

**References:** Leviticus 21:17-20; Deuteronomy 23:1; Isaiah 56:3-5; Acts 8:26-39

**The claim:** Leviticus 21:17-20 disqualifies priests with various physical conditions, including one 'that hath his stones broken' (a eunuch), from approaching the altar to serve as a priest, and Deuteronomy 23:1 more broadly excludes anyone 'wounded in the stones' or otherwise emasculated from entering 'the congregation of the LORD.' Isaiah 56:3-5, by contrast, promises eunuchs who keep God's covenant 'a place and a name better than of sons and daughters... an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off,' explicitly within God's own house. Acts 8:26-39 then records Philip baptizing an Ethiopian eunuch without hesitation or any stated exclusion.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization distinguishes the specific, narrow scope of the Leviticus and Deuteronomy provisions from a later, explicitly-announced expansion rather than treating the later texts as contradicting the earlier ones: Leviticus 21:17-20 addresses qualification for the priestly office specifically (a restricted class even among Israelite men, most of whom were never eligible for priestly service regardless of this provision), and Deuteronomy 23:1's exclusion from 'the congregation' is generally understood as barring eunuchs from a specific participatory role or status within Israel's covenant assembly under the Mosaic administration, not from all contact with Israel's worship or community life. Isaiah 56:3-5 explicitly frames itself as describing a future expansion of covenant inclusion ('neither let the son of the stranger... speak, saying, The LORD hath utterly separated me from his people'), and this same prophetic trajectory toward wider Gentile and previously-excluded inclusion is what the New Testament, including the Ethiopian eunuch's baptism in Acts 8 (itself widely read as a deliberate narrative fulfillment of Isaiah 56, since the eunuch is depicted reading Isaiah just before his baptism, Acts 8:27-33), presents as now being realized in the new covenant community -- a documented, Scripture-announced widening of participation over redemptive history, not two competing claims about the same fixed law.

## Law and Text

### 19. Mixed fabrics forbidden for Israel in general -- yet required for the priesthood

**References:** Leviticus 19:19; Exodus 28:4-8; Exodus 39:27-29

**The claim:** Leviticus 19:19 prohibits wearing 'a garment of cloth made of two kinds of material' (commonly understood to include a wool/linen mixture, sha'atnez). Yet the priestly garments prescribed in Exodus 28 explicitly call for linen combined with wool (blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, alongside fine twisted linen) woven together with gold thread for the ephod, breastpiece, and other priestly vestments.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Leviticus 19:19's prohibition and Exodus 28's priestly garment specifications plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing a general-rule-versus-priestly-exception pattern documented elsewhere in the Law, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is generally treated not as a genuine conflict but as an expected feature of a law code that frequently sets apart priestly practice from that of the general population precisely as a marker of the priesthood's distinct, consecrated status -- other Levitical provisions likewise permit or require the priesthood to do things forbidden to ordinary Israelites (for example, priests alone may eat portions of the most holy offerings, Leviticus 6:16-18, 29). Traditional Jewish legal interpretation (reflected in later rabbinic sources) treats the priestly garments as a specifically authorized, one-time exception to the general sha'atnez prohibition, valid only for garments made according to the divine pattern for priestly service and worn only in that context -- consistent with a broader pattern in the Torah where a general rule for the nation coexists with a narrower, explicitly stated exception for the priesthood's sacred function.

### 20. The sabbatical year, its centuries of neglect, and the seventy-year exile: does the arithmetic connect?

**References:** Leviticus 25:1-7; Leviticus 26:34-35; 2 Chronicles 36:20-21; Jeremiah 25:11-12; Jeremiah 29:10; Daniel 9:2

**The claim:** Leviticus 25:1-7 commands a sabbatical year every seventh year, in which the land itself is to lie fallow and rest, just as the seventh day of the week is a sabbath for people. Centuries later, 2 Chronicles 36:20-21 describes the Babylonian exile in strikingly specific terms tied directly back to this command: the land lay desolate during the seventy years of exile 'to fulfill the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah, until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths... to fulfill seventy years,' a figure Jeremiah himself had prophesied (Jeremiah 25:11-12; 29:10). This raises a specific, checkable arithmetic and historical question: if the exile's seventy years correspond to unobserved sabbatical years at a rate of one per seven years, this implies approximately 490 years (70 x 7) during which Israel failed to keep the commanded rest -- and a question of whether this reflects an actual, sustained pattern of disobedience to Leviticus 25 across the nation's history in the land, or a more general theological statement not intended as precise historical accounting.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP follows the same pattern across all three passages, typically with bracketed explanatory language connecting the seventy years directly to the unkept sabbatical years (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB follows the same pattern across all three passages (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV follows the same pattern, with 2 Chronicles 36:21 reading 'to fulfill seventy years' and directly citing the land 'enjoying its Sabbaths' (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 2 Chronicles 36:21, 'until the land had enjoyed her sabbaths: for as long as she lay desolate she kept sabbath, to fulfil threescore and ten years,' directly echoing Leviticus 26:34-35's warning ('Then shall the land enjoy her sabbaths, as long as it lieth desolate') and Leviticus 25:1-7's original command (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV follows the same pattern, rendering 2 Chronicles 36:21's 'to fulfill seventy years' as a direct echo of the sabbatical-year command in Leviticus 25 and its warning in Leviticus 26:34-35 (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB follows the same pattern across all three passages (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB follows the same pattern across all three passages (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV follows the same pattern across all three passages (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT follows the same pattern across all three passages, rendering the connection explicitly: 'the land finally enjoyed its Sabbath rest' (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV follows the same pattern across all three passages (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated by conservative and mainstream commentary alike not as a contradiction but as a deliberate, coherent theological and numerical statement: 2 Chronicles 36:21 explicitly frames the seventy years of exile as the land's overdue sabbatical rest, finally taken all at once after having been withheld, cumulatively, across the nation's prior history in the land. Since a single sabbatical year was commanded every seven years, seventy such unobserved years implies roughly 490 years of neglect (70 x 7 = 490) -- a span commentators commonly trace from Israel's entry into Canaan (or, more narrowly, from the establishment of the monarchy) to the Babylonian exile in 586 BC. Scripture nowhere claims that Israel observed the sabbatical year with total fidelity at any point in this period, and 2 Chronicles 36:21 itself explicitly treats the exile's length as compensating for this specific, cumulative failure, so there is no internal inconsistency between the command in Leviticus and the historical note in Chronicles -- rather, the later text presents the former as having gone unfulfilled for centuries and being satisfied, in a single stroke, by the exile's duration. This numerical connection (whether or not the underlying 490-year span can be reconstructed with full historical precision) is also cited by many scholars alongside the appearance of the same '70' and multiples of seven in Daniel 9's subsequent prophecy of 'seventy weeks,' which explicitly builds on Jeremiah's seventy-year prophecy (Daniel 9:2).
