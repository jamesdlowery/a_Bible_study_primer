# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in the Wisdom of Solomon

**A note on this document's place in the series:** As with Tobit and Judith, the Wisdom of
Solomon is a deuterocanonical book included in only one of the ten translations surveyed in
this project: RSV2CE, a Catholic edition. The other nine (AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB,
NIV, NLT) follow the Protestant and Jewish canon, which excludes it. This document notes, for
each entry, that nine of the ten translations do not contain the book at all.

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable theological
and philosophical questions involving the Wisdom of Solomon, either entirely internal to the
book or in comparison with Genesis, Proverbs, Hebrews, Colossians, Romans, 2 Maccabees, and
Joshua. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised and gives the
standard scholarly and Catholic-tradition reconciliation.

**A note on this book's character:** Of the deuterocanonical books addressed so far in this
series, the Wisdom of Solomon is unusually philosophically sophisticated, composed originally
in Greek (unlike Tobit and Judith, likely composed in Semitic languages) and deeply engaged
with contemporary Hellenistic philosophy. Its pseudonymous Solomonic attribution is treated
with unusual scholarly unanimity, even among conservative and Catholic scholars, as a
recognized literary convention rather than a live authorship dispute -- a notably more settled
question than the comparable debates addressed in the companion Ecclesiastes and Song of
Solomon documents.

**Verification status:** Entry 4 (creation "out of formless matter") was checked against a
deliberately wide range of sources representing genuine, ongoing scholarly disagreement,
including specialized academic studies and a Catholic apologetics rebuttal. The remaining
entries are theological and philosophical questions addressed from standard scholarly
positions.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by relevant scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly disagreement remains, an honest account
of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary text directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. Attributed to Solomon in the first person -- yet almost universally recognized, including by Catholic scholarship, as a much later composition

**References:** Wisdom 7:1-14; Wisdom 8:17-21; Wisdom 9:7-8

**The claim:** The Wisdom of Solomon is written throughout in the first-person voice of an unnamed king who describes his own request for wisdom (9:7-8, closely echoing 1 Kings 3's account of Solomon's request), his own royal upbringing (7:1-6), and his own decision to seek wisdom as a bride (8:17-21) -- a clear literary self-presentation as Solomon. Yet virtually all scholars, including the great majority of Catholic scholarship that affirms the book's canonical and inspired status, agree the book was actually composed many centuries after Solomon's own tenth-century-BC lifetime, most likely in the first century BC in the Greek-speaking Jewish community of Alexandria, Egypt -- a dating with a notably stronger and more nearly unanimous scholarly basis than the more genuinely disputed authorship questions surrounding Ecclesiastes and Song of Solomon addressed elsewhere in this project.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the Wisdom of Solomon. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the Wisdom of Solomon in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Wisdom of Solomon is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the full text of the Wisdom of Solomon as canonical Scripture. The book is written throughout in Solomon's first-person voice exactly as the claim describes, including 9:7-8's echo of 1 Kings 3 and 8:17-21's account of seeking wisdom as a bride (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Unlike the more contested authorship questions this project has addressed regarding Ecclesiastes and Song of Solomon (where a live scholarly minority continues to defend at least substantial genuine Solomonic content later edited), the case for the Wisdom of Solomon's late, pseudonymous composition is unusually decisive and is accepted even by conservative and Catholic scholars who fully affirm the book's canonicity and inspiration. The book's sophisticated original Greek -- employing precise, technical vocabulary drawn from Stoic and Platonic philosophy (addressed further in entries 3 and 4 below) that did not exist in any form accessible to a tenth-century-BC Hebrew-speaking king -- makes authentic Solomonic authorship essentially impossible on purely linguistic grounds, a much more clear-cut case than the more ambiguous linguistic evidence debated regarding Ecclesiastes. This is generally treated, including within Catholic scholarship itself, not as a difficulty requiring explanation away, but as a recognized and unremarkable literary convention of the era -- writing in the voice and persona of a revered, wisdom-associated ancient figure (here, Solomon, already firmly established as the biblical exemplar of wisdom through Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Solomon) was a well-attested genre convention in the wisdom-literature tradition of the Hellenistic period, intended to invoke that figure's own authority and association with wisdom rather than to make a literal, historical claim of authorship the original audience would have mistaken for straightforward fact.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. 'Through the devil's envy death entered the world' -- a doctrinal development beyond Genesis's own explicit statement

**References:** Wisdom 2:23-24; Genesis 3:1-19; Romans 5:12

**The claim:** Wisdom 2:23-24 states, 'God created man for incorruption, and made him in the image of his own eternity, but through the devil's envy death entered the world, and those who belong to his party experience it.' This is among the earliest and clearest texts in the biblical and deuterocanonical corpus to explicitly identify the serpent of Genesis 3 with 'the devil' by name, and to attribute human mortality specifically to the devil's own motivating 'envy' -- neither detail stated explicitly in Genesis 3 itself, which describes 'the serpent' without further identification and does not specify envy as its motive.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the Wisdom of Solomon. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the Wisdom of Solomon in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Wisdom of Solomon is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (2:23-24): "for God created man for incorruption, and made him in the image of his own eternity, but through the devil's envy death entered the world, and those who belong to his party experience it" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a further, later stage in the same pattern of progressive doctrinal development addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding the developing biblical portrait of Satan (in the companion Job, Ezekiel, and Daniel documents) -- earlier texts (Genesis 3, and the more restrained 'satan' figure of Job 1-2 and Zechariah 3) establish the basic narrative and theological building blocks without yet supplying the more developed, explicit identification and motive later Jewish and Christian tradition would draw out and make explicit. Wisdom 2:24 represents an early witness to an interpretive tradition -- identifying Genesis 3's serpent specifically with a personal, envious devil, and connecting that identification directly to the origin of human death -- that would become standard in later Jewish and Christian theology (compare Revelation 12:9's explicit identification of 'the ancient serpent... the Devil and Satan,' and Romans 5:12's own, differently-framed statement that 'sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin'), representing a legitimate development and clarification of themes already present in seed form in Genesis 3 rather than an addition in tension with it.

### 3. 'A good soul fell to my lot' -- does the book teach the soul's pre-existence, in tension with typical Hebrew anthropology?

**References:** Wisdom 8:19-20; Wisdom 9:15; Genesis 2:7

**The claim:** Wisdom 8:19-20 has its speaker (writing in Solomon's voice) state, 'As a child I was by nature well endowed, and a good soul fell to my lot; or rather, being good, I entered an undefiled body' -- language many scholars read as reflecting the Platonic doctrine of the soul's pre-existence before entering a body, a concept in some tension with the more typical Hebrew anthropological picture found elsewhere in Scripture (Genesis 2:7's account of God forming man's body from the dust and then breathing life into it as a single, unified act of creation, without any suggestion of a soul existing independently beforehand and then being assigned to a body).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the Wisdom of Solomon. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the Wisdom of Solomon in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Wisdom of Solomon is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (8:19-20): "As a child I was by nature well endowed, and a good soul fell to my lot; or rather, being good, I entered an undefiled body" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most scholars agree this passage reflects genuine influence from Hellenistic Greek philosophy, particularly Platonic and Middle Platonic ideas about the soul's pre-existent, non-material nature (compare also Wisdom 9:15's separately Platonic-sounding statement that 'a perishable body weighs down the soul, and this earthy tent burdens the thoughtful mind,' echoing Plato's own body-soul dualism), representing the book's broader project of expressing Jewish theological content using the philosophical vocabulary and conceptual categories of its Hellenistic Alexandrian setting. This project does not adjudicate the theological question of whether this specific passage should be read as making a literal doctrinal claim about pre-existent souls (a minority, more speculative theological position within the wide range of Jewish and Christian thought on the soul's nature) or should be read more figuratively, as poetic language expressing the author's sense of having been specially graced with natural aptitude for wisdom from the very beginning of his life, without committing to a fully worked-out Platonic metaphysics of the soul -- both readings have been proposed by serious commentators, and the passage's precise philosophical commitments remain a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion.

### 4. Creation 'out of formless matter' -- creation from nothing, or from pre-existing material?

**References:** Wisdom 11:17; Genesis 1:1-2; 2 Maccabees 7:28

**The claim:** Wisdom 11:17 describes God's 'all-powerful hand, which created the world out of formless matter' (Greek, ex amorphou hyles) -- a phrase some scholars read as adopting a specifically Platonic and Stoic philosophical formula describing a divine craftsman shaping already-existing, eternal raw material, rather than the doctrine of creation entirely 'out of nothing' (creatio ex nihilo) that later became standard Christian and Jewish theology, and which some read 2 Maccabees 7:28 ('God did not make them out of things that existed') as more directly and explicitly teaching.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the Wisdom of Solomon. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the Wisdom of Solomon in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Wisdom of Solomon is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (11:17): God's "all-powerful hand, which created the world out of formless matter" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated question among scholars, and this entry does not resolve it. A substantial body of scholarship (including the influential Biblical scholar Bruce Metzger, and Jonathan Goldstein's specialized study of ancient Jewish creation theology) holds that Wisdom 11:17 does reflect the Hellenistic philosophical concept of eternal, uncreated matter shaped by a divine craftsman, noting that this specific Greek phrase was itself a recognized technical formula in contemporary Platonist philosophical writing, and that no clearly pre-rabbinic Jewish text can be shown with confidence to teach true creation ex nihilo prior to 2 Maccabees 7:28 (itself a matter of some scholarly dispute over its precise philosophical meaning). Other scholars and theologians, including a common line of Catholic apologetic response, read Wisdom 11:17's 'formless matter' as simply and directly echoing Genesis 1:2's own description of the earth as 'without form and void' -- itself, on this reading, a description of the earliest, still-unformed stage of God's own creative work, rather than a claim of independently pre-existing, uncreated material -- meaning Wisdom's phrase would describe the same initial created (not eternal, uncreated) state Genesis itself describes, consistent with rather than contradicting later ex nihilo doctrine. This project does not adjudicate between these two readings, both of which are defended by serious scholars.

### 5. Personified Wisdom as 'a reflection of eternal light' -- a precursor to New Testament Christology?

**References:** Wisdom 7:22-8:1; Wisdom 7:25-26; Proverbs 8:22-31; Hebrews 1:3; Colossians 1:15

**The claim:** Wisdom 7:22-8:1 offers an extended, sophisticated meditation on personified Wisdom, describing her with an extensive list of Hellenistic philosophical attributes and stating she is 'a breath of the power of God, and a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty... a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God, and an image of his goodness' (7:25-26) -- language strikingly similar to, and widely believed by scholars to have directly influenced, the New Testament's own Christological language describing the Son as 'the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature' (Hebrews 1:3) and 'the image of the invisible God' (Colossians 1:15), raising the same general question addressed in this project's companion Proverbs document regarding Proverbs 8's own personified Wisdom -- is this the same figure New Testament writers later identify with the divine Son, a literary precursor to that identification, or simply a shared but ultimately distinct wisdom tradition?

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the Wisdom of Solomon. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the Wisdom of Solomon in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Wisdom of Solomon is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (7:25-26): Wisdom is "a breath of the power of God, and a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty... a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God, and an image of his goodness" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This question parallels, and is generally treated by scholars in continuity with, the discussion of Proverbs 8:22-31 addressed in the companion Proverbs document. Most scholars agree that Wisdom of Solomon's extended, philosophically developed personification of Wisdom represents a further, more theologically and philosophically elaborate stage of the same wisdom tradition Proverbs 8 initiates, and that New Testament authors -- writing in a milieu where this book (or at least its ideas and vocabulary) was well known within Hellenistic Judaism -- drew directly and extensively on this specific language and conceptual framework when developing their own Christological descriptions of the Son's relationship to the Father. This is generally read not as a claim that the Wisdom of Solomon's author intended to describe the specific person of Jesus Christ centuries in advance, but as evidence that the New Testament's own high Christology developed by taking up and applying, to the person of Jesus specifically, imagery and vocabulary that Hellenistic Jewish wisdom literature had already developed to describe Wisdom's own intimate relationship with, and revelatory function toward, God -- a documented and substantial literary and theological influence, rather than a claim of direct predictive identity between the two figures.

### 6. God's gradual judgment on the Canaanites, 'to give them a chance to repent' -- an extended theological gloss on the conquest narratives

**References:** Wisdom 12:3-11; Genesis 15:16; Joshua 6-11

**The claim:** Wisdom 12:3-11 offers an extended theological reflection on God's judgment against the Canaanite peoples conquered in the book of Joshua, explicitly stating that although God 'hated' their 'detestable practices' and knew their 'wickedness was inborn' and their nature 'evil from the start' (12:10-11), God nonetheless judged them 'little by little' and 'gave them a chance to repent' (12:10) rather than destroying them all at once, despite having full power to do so -- a considerably more explicit and developed theological rationale than Genesis 15:16's own brief statement that Abraham's descendants would not yet possess Canaan 'for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete.'

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the Wisdom of Solomon. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the Wisdom of Solomon in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Wisdom of Solomon is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (12:10-11): God judged the Canaanites "little by little" and "gave them a chance to repent," despite their wickedness being "inborn" and their nature "evil from the start" (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a further, later theological reflection expanding on a principle already present, though much more briefly stated, in Genesis 15:16 itself -- both texts agree that divine judgment on the Canaanites was not immediate or arbitrary, but connected to the accumulation of persistent wrongdoing over an extended period, with Wisdom of Solomon's later reflection drawing out and making explicit an additional dimension (opportunity for repentance during the delay) that Genesis's own brief statement does not explicitly develop but is fully compatible with. This is generally read as a legitimate later theological meditation on, rather than a revision of, the conquest narratives' own moral logic, addressing directly the same question this project has addressed regarding the ethics of the conquest narratives in the companion Joshua document -- Wisdom of Solomon here functions as an early witness to later Jewish theological reflection specifically defending God's patience and justice in the conquest account against exactly the kind of ethical questions modern readers continue to raise about it.

### 7. An etiology of idolatry's origin -- grief for a dead child, or flattery of a distant ruler

**References:** Wisdom 14:12-21

**The claim:** Wisdom 14:12-21 offers two distinct, specific theories for how idol worship first began among humanity: one account traces it to a grieving father who made an image of his deceased child to console himself, which was later mistakenly venerated by others as though it held divine power (14:15); a second account traces it to subjects flattering a distant or absent ruler by crafting and honoring an image of him, a practice that gradually developed into a settled convention of image-worship (14:16-20) -- raising a question of whether these two distinct etiologies for the same phenomenon are compatible with each other or represent two competing theories.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the Wisdom of Solomon. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the Wisdom of Solomon in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Wisdom of Solomon is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes both etiological accounts of idolatry's origin (14:12-21) -- the grieving father's image of his dead child (14:15) and the flattering image made for a distant ruler (14:16-20) -- exactly as the claim describes (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as the author offering two separate, illustrative case studies of how idolatry might plausibly have originated in different specific circumstances, rather than proposing a single, universal historical account of idolatry's one true origin -- ancient etiological reflection of this kind (offering illustrative 'just-so' accounts of how a widespread practice might have begun) commonly supplied multiple plausible scenarios without requiring a single, exclusive causal history, especially given that idol worship, as a broad human phenomenon, plausibly arose independently in different specific circumstances across different cultures and periods rather than through one single historical point of origin. Both accounts share the same underlying diagnosis the author is chiefly concerned to establish -- idolatry began through a merely human, emotionally or socially motivated act (grief, or flattery) subsequently and mistakenly elevated to the status of divine reverence -- which is the passage's real theological point, rather than a strict historical claim requiring the two specific scenarios to be mutually exclusive alternatives.
