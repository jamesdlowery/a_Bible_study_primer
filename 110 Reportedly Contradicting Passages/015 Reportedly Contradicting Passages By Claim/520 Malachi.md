# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Malachi

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual and
theological questions involving the book of Malachi, the final book of the Old Testament in
canonical order, either entirely internal to the book or in comparison with Romans, Matthew,
Mark, Luke, John, and the companion documents addressing related recurring themes across this
project. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how
the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own
wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives
the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's most significant entry:** Entry 4 (Malachi 2:16, popularly known as
"I hate divorce") documents one of the most genuinely difficult and consequential translation
cruxes addressed anywhere in this project -- major translations genuinely disagree not merely
on shading of meaning but on the verse's actual grammatical subject, with real, documented
shifts in how translation families have rendered it over time (RSV to ESV; NIV 1984 to NIV
2011).

**A note on candor:** Entry 1 (Jacob and Esau, and Romans 9) addresses one of the most
substantively debated questions in the history of Christian theology and is presented without
taking a side between major theological traditions.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine diversity of serious
opinion remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult
the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated' -- individual predestination, or national destiny?

**References:** Malachi 1:2-3; Romans 9:10-13; Genesis 25:23

**The claim:** Malachi 1:2-3 opens with God's declaration, 'I have loved you... Yet I have loved Jacob but Esau I have hated. I have laid waste his hill country.' Paul quotes this directly in Romans 9:13, in the midst of an extended argument about divine election explicitly framed around the two individual patriarchs 'before they were born, and had done nothing either good or bad' (9:11) -- raising a major theological question about whether this describes God's sovereign choice regarding the two individual men's own eternal destinies, or the national destinies of the two peoples descended from them (Israel and Edom), given that Malachi's own immediate context (1:3-4, explicitly describing Edom's 'hill country' laid waste and its cities as ruins the Edomites will try, but fail, to rebuild) addresses the two nations' historical fates rather than the individual patriarchs' personal standing before God.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Malachi 1:2-3 and Romans 9:10-13 plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here is one of the most substantively debated in the whole of Christian theology, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most substantively debated theological questions addressed anywhere in this project, central to long-running disagreements between different Christian theological traditions regarding the nature of divine election, and this entry does not adjudicate between them. Most commentators agree that Malachi's own immediate, six-century-later context is unambiguously about the two nations' historical fates (Edom's national desolation, contrasted with Israel's God-given land), not a statement about either patriarch's personal, eternal salvation -- 'hated' in this reading reflects a common Hebrew idiom of comparative preference ('loved less' or 'set aside in favor of another,' a usage with some parallel elsewhere in Scripture, e.g., Genesis 29:31's description of Leah as 'hated' relative to Rachel, meaning simply less favored rather than despised). The genuinely debated question concerns how directly Paul's own argument in Romans 9 extends this national-historical statement to make a claim about individual, unconditional election to salvation -- some theological traditions (broadly, Reformed/Calvinist) read Paul's argument as directly applying Malachi's language to make a parallel point about God's sovereign, unconditional election of individuals apart from any foreseen merit, using the national example as a supporting illustration of a broader principle about individual salvation; other traditions (broadly, Arminian and others) read Paul's argument as remaining fundamentally about corporate, national election and God's sovereign right to structure the historical outworking of his covenant purposes through Israel rather than a direct claim about specific individuals' eternal destinies. Both readings are defended at length by serious, careful theologians, and this project does not take a position on which is correct.

### 2. 'I the LORD do not change' -- the Old Testament's clearest statement of divine immutability

**References:** Malachi 3:6; Genesis 6:6; Exodus 32:14; 1 Samuel 15:29; Jonah 3:10

**The claim:** Malachi 3:6 states plainly, 'For I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed' -- one of the most direct Old Testament affirmations of divine immutability, connecting to the same general tension addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (regarding Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29's similar affirmations, set against Genesis 6:6, Exodus 32:14, and Jonah 3:10's descriptions of God 'relenting' or changing his stated intention).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Malachi 3:6 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same distinction between unchanging character and responsive, conditional intention addressed at greater length in the companion Jeremiah and 1 Samuel documents, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in this project's companion documents addressing the same recurring pattern (most explicitly in the Jeremiah document's discussion of Jeremiah 18:7-10 as the Bible's clearest statement of the underlying conditional principle, and in the 1 Samuel document's treatment of 1 Samuel 15's own internal tension). As with those cases, Malachi 3:6's affirmation of God's unchanging character and covenant faithfulness (the specific point the verse itself makes: Israel's continued existence despite its own unfaithfulness rests on God's own unchanging commitment to his covenant promises, not on Israel's merit) is generally read as compatible with, rather than contradicting, the pattern of God's responsive change of specific, stated intentions in response to changed human circumstances (repentance, intercession) addressed throughout the Old Testament -- a distinction between unchanging character, purpose, and covenant faithfulness on the one hand, and specific, conditionally-stated intentions that appropriately and consistently respond to changing human conduct on the other, which this project has addressed at several points as the standard resolution to this recurring pattern.

### 3. 'Bring the full tithe... and see if I will not open the windows of heaven' -- a specific prosperity promise

**References:** Malachi 3:8-10; Proverbs 3:9-10; Job 1:1-3, 13-19

**The claim:** Malachi 3:8-10 issues a direct challenge: 'Bring the full tithe into the storehouse... and thereby put me to the test, says the LORD of hosts, if I will not open the windows of heaven for you and pour down for you a blessing until there is no more need' -- a specific, testable promise of material blessing tied to faithful tithing, connecting to the same general question addressed at greater length elsewhere in this project (regarding Proverbs 3:9-10's similar promise and Job's own experience of severe loss despite exemplary piety).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Malachi 3:8-10 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing this as a specific, corporate covenantal challenge addressed to Malachi's own immediate historical community, addressed at greater length regarding the same underlying question in the companion Proverbs, Job, and Haggai documents, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Proverbs and Job documents, both addressing the same underlying genre distinction between a general, reliable covenantal principle and a strict, universal, individually-guaranteed mechanical outcome. Malachi's own specific challenge is best read within its immediate historical context -- addressed to a post-exilic community whose collective, corporate failure to support the Temple and its priesthood through tithing (the immediate target of Malachi's rebuke throughout this section, 3:8, 'you are robbing me') had produced real agricultural hardship (3:11, locusts devouring crops), inviting the community as a whole to test, through renewed corporate covenant faithfulness, whether God's own covenant blessing would be restored -- a pattern of national, corporate blessing and curse tied to collective covenant faithfulness (addressed at greater length regarding Deuteronomy 28 and its outworking in the companion Haggai document, which addresses a closely parallel case) rather than a strict, universal promise guaranteeing every individual tither's material prosperity regardless of all other circumstances.

### 4. Can God be tempted, and do those who tempt him actually get away with it?

**References:** Malachi 3:13-15; Exodus 17:2; James 1:13

**The claim:** Malachi 3:15 states, 'we call the proud happy; yea, they that work wickedness are set up; yea, they that tempt God are even delivered' -- appearing to claim that people who test or tempt God nevertheless escape consequence, and raising the same general question addressed elsewhere in this project's Exodus companion document (regarding Exodus 17:2 and James 1:13) about the different senses in which God can or cannot be 'tempted.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This verse is best read as the text's own explicit report of a cynical, faithless complaint circulating among Malachi's discouraged post-exilic audience -- 3:13-14 frames the whole statement as what 'ye have said' against the LORD ('your words have been stout against me'), a report of the people's own skeptical grumbling rather than the book's own theological affirmation, and the immediately following verses directly answer and refute it: those who feared the LORD are specifically remembered and recorded (3:16), and a coming day is promised that will decisively distinguish 'between the righteous and the wicked, between him that serveth God and him that serveth him not' (3:18), directly overturning the cynics' claim that evildoers and those who test God simply get away with it. On the underlying 'tempt' terminology itself, this passage uses the same range of meaning addressed at greater length in the Exodus document's entry on Exodus 17:2 -- 'tempting' God in the sense of testing his patience and providence through faithless demand (which the skeptics here are shown, by the chapter's own continuing argument, to be mistaken about escaping consequence for) rather than the sense James 1:13 denies, in which God cannot be enticed toward moral evil.

## Numbers and Text

### 5. 'I hate divorce' -- one of the most genuinely difficult and consequential translation cruxes in the Old Testament

**References:** Malachi 2:16

**The claim:** Malachi 2:16 is popularly known through the phrase 'I hate divorce, says the LORD' -- treated in much contemporary teaching as one of Scripture's clearest, most direct statements on the subject. The underlying Hebrew, however, is widely acknowledged by scholars across the theological spectrum to be among the most difficult and genuinely ambiguous constructions in the entire Old Testament, and English translations diverge into three substantially different renderings with three different grammatical subjects: some have God himself declaring 'I hate divorce' (first person); others render the Hebrew's actual third-person verb form more literally as 'he [God] hates divorce' (KJV's 'the LORD... saith that he hateth putting away'); and still others read the verse as describing not God's own attitude at all, but a human husband's sin -- 'the man who hates and divorces his wife... covers his garment with violence' -- removing any direct statement of God's own view on divorce from this verse altogether.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'For I hate divorce,' says the LORD, the God of Israel, 'and him who covers his garment with wrong and violence,' says the LORD of hosts. 'Therefore keep watch on your spirit, so that you do not deal treacherously [with your wife],'' the first-person reading, matching NASB and LSB (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB also follows the 'man who hates and divorces' reading, consistent with ESV and the current NIV (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'For the man who does not love his wife but divorces her... covers his garment with violence,' the substantially different reading in which a human husband, not God's own attitude, is the subject -- with a footnote acknowledging 'Hebrew who hates and divorces' as the more literal underlying text (verified against the published text; this represents a documented change from the earlier RSV, upon which the ESV is based, which read 'I hate divorce').
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'the LORD, the God of Israel, saith that he hateth putting away,' the third-person 'he hates' reading with God as the implied subject.
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'the LORD God of Israel says that He hates divorce,' also the third-person 'He hates' reading with God as the subject (capitalized in NKJV's convention for divine pronouns).
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'For I hate divorce,' the first-person reading with God as speaker (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'For I hate divorce,' says Yahweh, the God of Israel, and him who covers his garment with wrong,' says Yahweh of hosts,' matching NASB's first-person reading exactly (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** The current (2011) NIV follows the same reading as ESV, rendering 'the man who hates and divorces his wife... does violence to the one he should protect' -- a documented change from the earlier 1984 NIV edition, which read 'I hate divorce' (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'For I hate divorce,' also the first-person reading.
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'For I hate divorce, says the LORD the God of Israel, and covering one's garment with violence, says the LORD of hosts,' the first-person reading (verified against the published RSV text) -- confirming that the ESV's later departure to the 'man who hates and divorces' reading was a deliberate change from its own RSV base, not a continuation of it; RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves the original RSV's first-person wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and openly acknowledged textual and grammatical difficulty, not a matter with a single, confidently correct answer, and this entry presents it as such. The Hebrew text as it stands, ki-sane shallach, roughly 'for he-hates sending-away,' uses a third-person singular verb without an explicit stated subject -- a grammatical gap Hebrew sometimes fills contextually but which is genuinely ambiguous here. Some scholars, and a number of translations, supply an implied first-person subject, 'I' meaning God, matching the surrounding first-person divine speech throughout the passage, on the assumption that a pronoun may have dropped out during the text's transmission. Other scholars, including a growing number of recent translators, read the third-person form as intentional and original, referring instead to an unfaithful husband: 'he who hates [his wife] and divorces her.' This is consistent with the immediately preceding verses' sustained focus on husbands treacherously divorcing 'the wife of your youth' (2:14-15) for other reasons. On this reading, the verse condemns a husband's own hate-driven divorce as an act of 'covering one's garment with violence,' a specific image of injustice or assault, rather than making a general, standalone statement of God's own attitude toward divorce as an institution in every possible circumstance. Both readings are defended by serious, careful Hebrew scholars, and the difference has real practical consequences for how the verse has been popularly used in pastoral and ethical teaching on marriage and divorce.

## Theological Interpretation

### 6. 'Behold, I send my messenger' -- one figure, or two, preparing the way before the Lord's own coming

**References:** Malachi 3:1; Matthew 11:10; Mark 1:2; Luke 7:27

**The claim:** Malachi 3:1 states, 'Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me. And the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple; and the messenger of the covenant in whom you delight, behold, he is coming' -- a verse that mentions what could be read as either two distinct figures (a preparatory 'messenger' distinct from 'the Lord' who then comes) or, given the further mention of 'the messenger of the covenant,' potentially three. All three Synoptic Gospels quote the verse's opening clause and apply it to John the Baptist as the forerunner who prepares the way specifically for Jesus (Matthew 11:10, Mark 1:2, Luke 7:27).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Malachi 3:1 plainly and consistently as given, without resolving the number of figures described through the text's own wording; the harmonization rests on recognizing 'the Lord' and 'the messenger of the covenant' as two titles for the single figure whose coming the initial messenger prepares for, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read the verse as describing two figures, not three -- 'my messenger' who prepares the way, and 'the Lord' who then comes to the temple, with 'the messenger of the covenant' in the verse's second half generally understood as a further, parallel description of this same 'Lord' who comes (not a third, separate figure), since the verse's own structure presents the coming of 'the Lord' and the coming of 'the messenger of the covenant' as the same anticipated arrival, described twice using different, complementary titles. The New Testament's application of the verse's opening clause to John the Baptist as 'my messenger' preparing the way, with Jesus himself identified as 'the Lord' who then comes to his temple, is treated by most commentators as a direct, intentional messianic identification -- Malachi's own text, written centuries before Jesus' birth, is understood to anticipate precisely this specific relationship (a forerunner preparing the way for the LORD's own personal coming), which the Gospels then present as literally fulfilled in the specific, sequential relationship between John's ministry and Jesus' own arrival.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. 'I will send you Elijah the prophet' -- Jesus identifies John the Baptist as this Elijah, yet John himself denies it

**References:** Malachi 4:5-6; Matthew 11:13-14; Matthew 17:10-13; John 1:19-21; Luke 1:15-17

**The claim:** Malachi's closing prophecy promises, 'Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes' (4:5). Jesus directly and explicitly identifies John the Baptist as the fulfillment of this prophecy on two separate occasions: 'if you are willing to accept it, he [John] is Elijah who is to come' (Matthew 11:14), and again, 'Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him' -- with Matthew's narrative explicitly clarifying 'the disciples understood that he was speaking to them of John the Baptist' (17:12-13). Yet when religious leaders directly ask John the Baptist himself, 'Are you Elijah?' -- his own recorded answer is a flat, unqualified denial: 'I am not' (John 1:21).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Malachi 4:5-6, Jesus' identification of John with Elijah, Luke 1:17's 'spirit and power of Elijah' language, and John's own denial plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing literal personal identity from typological, functional fulfillment -- a distinction the New Testament itself states explicitly at Luke 1:17, not one introduced only to resolve the apparent tension -- not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuine and frequently noted apparent tension, and most commentators resolve it by distinguishing a literal, personal identity claim (which John himself explicitly and correctly denies -- he was not the ninth-century-BC prophet Elijah returned to life or reincarnated) from a typological, functional fulfillment of Elijah's anticipated prophetic role and ministry (which Jesus affirms John did accomplish). This distinction is not merely inferred to resolve the tension but is directly stated in the New Testament's own account of John's birth: the angel Gabriel, announcing John's coming birth to his father Zechariah, explicitly describes John's future ministry not as Elijah's literal return but as coming 'in the spirit and power of Elijah' (Luke 1:17) -- language establishing, well before either John's own denial or Jesus' later affirmation, that John's relationship to Elijah was always understood as one of ministry and spirit, not literal personal identity. On this reading, John's denial in John 1:21 truthfully answers the specific, literal question being asked of him (by questioners likely influenced by a popular expectation that Elijah himself would return, in person, from heaven), while Jesus' later statements affirm the deeper, typological sense in which Malachi's prophecy was genuinely fulfilled through John's own ministry -- both statements are true, addressing two different senses of the same question, rather than one speaker contradicting the other.
