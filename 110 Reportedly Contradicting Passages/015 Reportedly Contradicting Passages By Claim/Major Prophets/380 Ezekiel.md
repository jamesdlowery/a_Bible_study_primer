# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Ezekiel

This document catalogs 12 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable theological
and interpretive questions involving the book of Ezekiel, either entirely internal to the book
or in comparison with Exodus, Deuteronomy, Psalms, Isaiah, 1 Kings, Numbers, Daniel, 2 Kings,
and Revelation. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes
how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's
own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and
gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entry 2 ("I gave them statutes that were not good") is one of the most
difficult individual verses addressed anywhere in this project, and is presented with more
than one seriously defended reading rather than a single confident resolution. Entries 4 and
12 (the king of Tyre's lament, and the identity of "Daniel" alongside Noah and Job) are
likewise presented with genuine scholarly diversity of opinion. Entry 10 (Gog and Magog)
touches on actively debated eschatological schools of interpretation this project does not
adjudicate.

**Verification status:** Entry 7 (the 390-day figure and its Septuagint variant) and entry 8
(Zedekiah's blinding as the resolution to Ezekiel 12:13) were both checked against multiple
sources for this document. The remaining entries are theological, historical, and literary
questions where all ten translations render the same underlying text and the harmonization is
exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'The soul who sins shall die' -- the Bible's most extensive statement of individual responsibility

**References:** Ezekiel 18:1-32; Exodus 20:5; Deuteronomy 24:16

**The claim:** Ezekiel 18 opens by explicitly quoting and directly rejecting a popular proverb of its day -- 'The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge' -- stating flatly, 'the soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son' (18:20), and developing this principle at great length across the entire chapter with detailed case studies of a righteous father, a wicked son, and a righteous grandson, each judged strictly on their own conduct. This raises, in its most extensive canonical form, the tension addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding Exodus 20:5's language of God 'visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 18 plainly and consistently as given, in its full extended argument; this chapter is treated throughout this project as the key text resolving several related tensions elsewhere, not one requiring separate harmonization of its own.

**Reconciliation:** This chapter functions as the fullest, most systematic biblical statement of the individual-responsibility principle addressed piecemeal elsewhere in this project, regarding Exodus 20:5, Deuteronomy 24:16, Jeremiah 31:29-30, and Lamentations 5:7's tension with 5:16. Rather than requiring a separate harmonization, Ezekiel 18 itself supplies the interpretive key. God's 'visiting' language in Exodus 20:5 describes the natural, relational consequences of sin working themselves out across generations within families and communities that continue in the same patterns of sin -- a reading Ezekiel 18 itself does not deny occurs as an observable social reality -- while denying that this constitutes legal guilt transferred from parent to child in God's own individual moral accounting. Each person, Ezekiel insists at length, stands before God on the basis of their own conduct alone: a wicked son of a righteous father, or a righteous son of a wicked father, will each be judged strictly according to their own actual choices, not their parent's. This chapter is widely regarded by scholars as either directly informing or closely paralleling Jeremiah's own similar, briefer statement of the same principle (31:29-30), likely reflecting a broader theological clarification current among the prophets addressing the exile generation's own sense of unjustly inherited guilt.

### 2. 'I gave them statutes that were not good' -- one of the most startling verses in the Old Testament

**References:** Ezekiel 20:25-26; Psalm 19:7; Deuteronomy 30:15-16

**The claim:** Ezekiel 20:25-26 has God declare, in the midst of a lengthy historical review of Israel's wilderness rebellion, 'Moreover, I gave them statutes that were not good, and rules by which they could not have life, and I defiled them through their very gifts in their offering up all their firstborn, that I might devastate them' -- language that appears to have God directly attributing to himself the giving of bad, life-denying laws, in stark tension with Psalm 19:7's confident affirmation that 'the law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul,' and Deuteronomy 30:15-16's presentation of the Law as offering life and good to those who obey it.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 20:25-26 plainly and consistently as given, without softening its difficult wording; this remains one of the most actively debated individual verses in Old Testament scholarship, and the harmonization offered here is not universally agreed upon, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most difficult and actively debated individual verses addressed in this whole project, and serious, careful commentators genuinely differ on its meaning, so this entry presents the leading readings rather than forcing one. Many commentators read this as continuing Ezekiel's broader theme, already stated at 20:23-24, of God's judicial 'giving over' of a persistently rebellious people to consequences of their own choosing. On this reading, 'statutes that were not good' does not refer to the Mosaic Law given at Sinai, which the same chapter, only verses earlier, describes approvingly as containing rules 'by which if a person does them, he shall live' (20:11, 13, 21), directly echoing Leviticus 18:5. It refers instead to a further, punitive 'law' or decree God subsequently imposed specifically in response to Israel's persistent idolatry -- most commonly identified with the specific, horrifying practice of child sacrifice to Molech, referenced immediately afterward (20:26, 20:31). Some read this as God judicially 'giving Israel over' to the very practice of their chosen pagan neighbors, as a self-inflicted judgment for having already rejected his own good statutes, comparable to Romans 1:24-28's pattern of God 'giving people over' to the consequences of their own persistent idolatry. A minority of scholars read the verse more starkly, as God directly, if difficultly, claiming responsibility for imposing certain harsh ritual demands -- potentially including, on this reading, an actual command regarding the firstborn that Israel misunderstood or that functioned punitively -- specifically as judgment for the wilderness generation's persistent rebellion. Some hold this reading is compatible with God's broader sovereignty over even judicial hardening, compare the discussion of Pharaoh's heart addressed in this project's companion Exodus document, without this constituting an endorsement of child sacrifice as good in itself -- which the same chapter elsewhere explicitly and repeatedly condemns as detestable (20:30-31).

### 3. Four-faced living creatures, six-winged seraphim, and Revelation's differently-described four living creatures

**References:** Ezekiel 1:5-14; Ezekiel 10:1-22; Isaiah 6:1-3; Revelation 4:6-8

**The claim:** Ezekiel's inaugural vision (chapter 1, revisited in chapter 10) describes four living creatures (later identified as cherubim, 10:20), each possessing four faces (of a man, a lion, an ox, and an eagle) and four wings. Isaiah's throne vision (6:1-3) instead describes seraphim, each with six wings, calling out 'holy, holy, holy.' Revelation 4:6-8 describes four living creatures around God's throne, but assigns each creature only one of the four faces individually (one like a lion, one like an ox, one with a human face, one like an eagle in flight) rather than each creature having all four faces as in Ezekiel, and gives them six wings each, matching Isaiah's seraphim rather than Ezekiel's four-winged cherubim.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each vision's specific details plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the descriptions across the three passages in the text; the harmonization rests on recognizing symbolic visionary literature's flexibility across different prophets and different visions, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these three visions not as competing, contradictory claims about a single, fixed angelic species with an exact, unchanging physical description, but as differently stylized symbolic representations -- appropriate to each prophet's own visionary experience and literary purpose -- of the same broader category of exalted heavenly beings who continually worship God and serve his throne. Ancient Near Eastern visionary and apocalyptic literature regularly uses composite, symbolic imagery (combining animal and human features to represent qualities like strength, wisdom, swiftness, and service) without requiring strict consistency of exact anatomical detail across different visions or different visionaries, any more than modern symbolic or heraldic imagery is expected to be anatomically uniform across different artistic representations of the same general concept. Revelation's own vision, explicitly written centuries later and drawing consciously on both Ezekiel's and Isaiah's earlier throne-room visions (a recognized and common practice in apocalyptic literature, which frequently echoes and recombines imagery from earlier prophetic visions rather than aiming for a photographically precise, single 'correct' description), is generally read as intentionally blending features from both earlier visions (Isaiah's six wings, Ezekiel's four distinct faces, now distributed one per creature rather than four per creature) as its own distinct, later theological synthesis, not as correcting or contradicting either earlier prophet's report of what they themselves specifically saw.

### 4. The lament over the king of Tyre -- a human ruler, or Satan's own primordial fall?

**References:** Ezekiel 28:11-19; Isaiah 14:12-15; Genesis 3:1-15

**The claim:** Ezekiel 28:11-19, explicitly framed as 'a lamentation for the king of Tyre' (28:12), describes its subject in language that goes well beyond typical royal hyperbole: 'You were in Eden, the garden of God... you were an anointed guard cherub... blameless in your ways from the day you were created, till unrighteousness was found in you.' This raises the same general question addressed in this project's companion Isaiah document regarding Isaiah 14's 'Lucifer' oracle against the king of Babylon -- is this describing a human king using cosmic imagery, or Satan's own primordial fall, and does the Eden/cherub language here make the case for a supernatural referent stronger than in the Isaiah passage?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 28:11-19 plainly and consistently as given; the interpretive question addressed here parallels, and is treated alongside, the companion Isaiah document's discussion of Isaiah 14, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the parallel Isaiah 14 case, most commentators, including many conservative ones, read this primarily and directly as an extended taunt against a specific, historical human king of Tyre (as the passage's own explicit framing states), using deliberately exaggerated, mythologically resonant language to mock Tyre's own extraordinary wealth, pride, and self-deification (the immediately preceding oracle in chapter 28:1-10 explicitly addresses 'the prince of Tyre' who says 'I am a god,' setting up this second oracle's continuation of the same theme with even more elevated imagery) -- ancient Tyrian royal ideology and iconography are independently known to have made extensive use of Edenic and paradise imagery for its own kings, which this oracle may be deliberately co-opting and subverting. As with Isaiah 14, a long tradition of Christian interpretation has read this passage as also, or primarily, describing Satan's own fall from an originally exalted, blameless status through pride (a reading with some support from the passage's explicit reference to a being 'created' blameless who fell through iniquity found within him, language some read as more naturally fitting a supernatural being present at creation's outset than any merely human king), a connection reinforced for many readers by the passage's explicit setting in Eden itself, directly evoking Genesis 3's account of the serpent's role in humanity's own fall. This entry, like its Isaiah 14 counterpart, does not resolve fully between these readings, since serious, Scripture-affirming scholars hold both positions and various mediating views (a human king's fall dramatizing and echoing, without being identical to, Satan's own prior and paradigmatic fall).

### 5. 'I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked' -- amid a book largely devoted to extensive judgment oracles

**References:** Ezekiel 33:11; Ezekiel 18:23, 32; Ezekiel 6:1-14; Ezekiel 25:1-32:32

**The claim:** Ezekiel 33:11 has God declare, 'As I live, declares the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live' -- a statement repeated in similar terms at 18:23 and 18:32 -- emphasizing God's preference for repentance over judgment. Yet the majority of the book of Ezekiel consists of extensive, often graphically detailed oracles of severe judgment against Israel, Judah, and numerous surrounding nations (chapters 6-7 and 25-32 alone comprise an extended sequence of judgment prophecies), raising a question about whether God's stated preference for the wicked's repentance is compatible with the book's own extensive record of actual, executed judgment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 33:11, 18:23, 18:32, and the book's extensive judgment oracles plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing God's stated preference from his willingness to execute judgment when that preference is persistently refused, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as fully compatible rather than competing claims: God's stated lack of 'pleasure' in judgment describes his own underlying disposition and preference (that repentance, not destruction, would be the outcome), not a claim that judgment will therefore never actually occur when that preferred alternative is persistently and finally refused -- the same chapters that state God's preference for repentance (18, 33) are explicitly framed as calls to repentance precisely because judgment remains the real, executed consequence for those who do not turn (33:12-20 immediately continues by detailing exactly how a person's own choices, whether toward righteousness or persistent wickedness, determine their actual fate). Ezekiel's extensive judgment oracles are consistently framed throughout the book not as arbitrary or gratuitous divine cruelty, but as the necessary, reluctantly executed consequence of sustained, unrepented national and individual unfaithfulness across many generations, exactly the pattern 18:30-32 itself calls Israel to break through repentance ('turn, and live') before judgment's consequences become unavoidable -- both are simultaneously true within the same coherent theological framework: God genuinely prefers and calls for repentance, and God genuinely executes real judgment when that call is persistently refused.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 6. The prophecy against Tyre -- and its own later, honest acknowledgment of partial non-fulfillment

**References:** Ezekiel 26:7-14; Ezekiel 29:17-20

**The claim:** Ezekiel 26:7-14 pronounces an extensive, detailed prophecy that Nebuchadnezzar would utterly destroy Tyre, break down its walls, plunder its wealth, and scrape it 'like the top of a rock' so that it would 'never be rebuilt.' Historically, Nebuchadnezzar's actual 13-year siege of Tyre (approximately 585-572 BC) appears to have ended in a negotiated settlement rather than the city's total destruction and permanent desolation the oracle describes -- mainland Tyre continued to exist, and the more famous, harder-to-conquer island portion of the city was not taken by Nebuchadnezzar at all (it would only fall centuries later, to Alexander the Great, using very different methods).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the original Tyre oracle and its later, corrective follow-up in chapter 29 plainly and consistently as given; this is one of the more remarkable and instructive cases in this project of Scripture itself openly acknowledging a prophecy's partial, rather than total, historical fulfillment, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Remarkably, and unlike most alleged cases of unfulfilled biblical prophecy, this is a case the biblical text itself directly and candidly addresses rather than leaving unexplained. Ezekiel 29:17-20, dated some sixteen years after the original Tyre oracle, has God state plainly: 'Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon made his army labor hard against Tyre... yet neither he nor his army got anything from Tyre for the labor that he had performed against it.' This is an explicit, unusual acknowledgment that the specific plunder and reward originally associated with the Tyre campaign did not materialize as expected, followed immediately by God granting Nebuchadnezzar the land of Egypt instead, 'as his payment for which he labored, because they worked for me.' Most commentators read this remarkable follow-up passage as the clearest possible internal evidence that biblical prophecy could describe a general, historically accurate outcome using the same kind of confident, totalizing prophetic language addressed elsewhere in this project regarding Babylon's own prophesied desolation in the companion Isaiah document. Nebuchadnezzar's real, extended, costly siege genuinely devastated and subdued mainland Tyre, ending its independent regional power in a way from which it never fully recovered as a major force, even though the specific island stronghold and its full plunder were not achieved by Nebuchadnezzar specifically. The text's own subsequent, corrective acknowledgment in chapter 29 shows the prophetic tradition itself was capable of honestly revisiting and adjusting the specific terms of an earlier oracle's application in light of how history actually unfolded, rather than requiring every specific detail to be defended as literally, exhaustively fulfilled regardless of the facts.

## Numbers and Text

### 7. 390 days representing years of iniquity -- a number that puzzled ancient interpreters as much as modern ones

**References:** Ezekiel 4:4-6

**The claim:** Ezekiel is commanded to lie on his left side for 390 days, each day representing one year of 'the iniquity of the house of Israel' (4:4-5), followed by 40 days on his right side representing Judah's iniquity (4:6). The 390-year figure for Israel (the northern kingdom) does not correspond neatly to any commonly reckoned historical span for that kingdom's existence or apostasy (which most chronologies place at under 210 years from its founding to its fall to Assyria, and at roughly 130-150 years of actual exile by Ezekiel's own time) -- a difficulty ancient interpreters were aware of as well: the Greek Septuagint reads different, smaller figures (most commonly cited as 190 days, closer to the exile's actual length by Ezekiel's day) at this verse, though this reading is not attested in any surviving Hebrew manuscript.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '390 days' at Ezekiel 4:5 consistently, following the Masoretic Hebrew text; none of the ten adopts the Septuagint's alternate, smaller figure, which lacks Hebrew manuscript support.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely difficult numerical question, and commentators offer several different approaches without full consensus: some read the 390 years as referring not to the length of the exile itself but to the cumulative span of Israel's persistent idolatry and covenant unfaithfulness counted from an earlier point (proposals include counting from the kingdom's division under Rehoboam and Jeroboam, or from an even earlier point in Israel's history), which would allow the figure to represent an extended period of accumulated sin rather than a literal duration of exile; others read the number symbolically rather than as a precise historical measurement, consistent with a broader pattern of round or stylized numbers used elsewhere in prophetic literature for total, comprehensive periods of judgment. The Septuagint's alternate, smaller figure is judged by most textual scholars to be a later, interpretive adjustment attempting to reconcile the number more closely with the exile's known actual length, rather than an independently attested original reading, since no Hebrew manuscript evidence supports it -- consistent with this, no standard English translation adopts the Septuagint's alternate figure in its main text.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 8. Zedekiah 'shall not see' Babylon -- yet he 'shall die there'

**References:** Ezekiel 12:13; 2 Kings 25:6-7

**The claim:** Ezekiel 12:13 prophesies concerning King Zedekiah, 'I will bring him to Babylon, the land of the Chaldeans, yet he shall not see it, though he shall die there' -- a statement that reads, on its face, as internally puzzling: how can someone be brought to a specific place, and die there, without ever 'seeing' it?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 12:13 and 2 Kings 25:6-7 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a fulfillment relationship rather than a contradiction, and there is no translation variance bearing on it.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction but a remarkably precise prophecy whose exact fulfillment is separately recorded in the historical narrative: 2 Kings 25:6-7 (paralleled in Jeremiah 39:6-7 and 52:10-11) reports that after Zedekiah's capture, Nebuchadnezzar's forces killed his sons before his own eyes and then 'put out the eyes of Zedekiah,' blinding him, before binding him in chains and taking him to Babylon -- meaning Zedekiah was blinded before arriving in Babylon, and therefore genuinely never 'saw' the land he was brought to and in which he would eventually die, exactly as Ezekiel's earlier oracle had specified. This is treated as a striking case of precise, verifiable prophetic detail rather than an internal inconsistency, and is often cited by commentators specifically because Ezekiel's oracle (delivered years before the actual siege and capture) and the later historical narrative's fulfillment detail were composed independently enough that the correspondence is not obviously the product of one account being conformed after the fact to match the other's specific wording.

## Theological Interpretation

### 9. The valley of dry bones: individual bodily resurrection, or corporate national restoration?

**References:** Ezekiel 37:1-14

**The claim:** Ezekiel's vision of a valley full of dry bones being reassembled, covered with flesh, and brought to life by God's breath (37:1-10) is one of the most vivid resurrection images in the Old Testament, and has been read by later Jewish and Christian tradition as bearing on the doctrine of individual bodily resurrection at the end of the age -- raising a question about whether this is the vision's own intended meaning, since the passage itself supplies an explicit interpretation identifying the bones with the corporate nation: 'these bones are the whole house of Israel... I will open your graves and raise you from your graves, O my people, and bring you into the land of Israel' (37:11-12).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 37:1-14, including its own explicit interpretive statement in 37:11-12, plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here concerns the passage's primary versus secondary significance, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, since the text itself directly and unambiguously supplies its own primary interpretation, which nearly all commentators, across the theological spectrum, accept as the vision's immediate and intended meaning: a symbolic representation of the exiled nation of Israel's own national restoration and return to its land, described in the vivid imagery of resurrection from death (exile experienced as a kind of national death) to life (national restoration). Most commentators additionally note that this does not necessarily exhaust the passage's full significance or later canonical resonance -- the vision's own choice of resurrection imagery to describe national restoration presupposes that its original audience already found bodily resurrection a meaningful, comprehensible category (a doctrine more explicitly developed elsewhere in the Old Testament, e.g., Daniel 12:2, and much more fully in the New Testament), and many later interpreters, without denying the passage's own primary, explicitly stated corporate meaning, have found in its imagery a fitting and theologically resonant anticipation of the fuller individual resurrection hope later revealed, without this constituting two competing or contradictory meanings for the same text.

### 10. Gog and Magog: a specific historical invasion of Israel, or the final rebellion at history's end?

**References:** Ezekiel 38:1-39:29; Revelation 20:7-10

**The claim:** Ezekiel 38-39 describes an extended, detailed prophecy of an invasion of a restored Israel by a coalition led by 'Gog, of the land of Magog,' which God dramatically and decisively defeats. Revelation 20:7-10, describing events at the very end of the millennium, states that Satan will be released and will 'deceive the nations... Gog and Magog, to gather them for battle,' surrounding 'the camp of the saints' before being decisively defeated by fire from heaven -- using the identical names in a context that appears to be a different, later, and more explicitly cosmic-eschatological conflict than the one Ezekiel describes.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 38-39 and Revelation 20:7-10 plainly and consistently as given, using the same names in both passages without harmonizing or distinguishing them in the text; the question addressed here concerns eschatological interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Revelation's use of 'Gog and Magog' as a deliberate, symbolic literary allusion to Ezekiel's earlier prophecy rather than a claim that the exact same historical invasion Ezekiel described will occur a second time at the end of the millennium -- by John's time, 'Gog and Magog' had evidently become a widely recognized, almost proverbial byword for a great, climactic, God-opposing military coalition assembled against God's people, regardless of the coalition's specific historical or geographic identity in any given generation, similar to how this project has addressed 'Babylon' functioning as a recognizable symbol for corrupt worldly power beyond the literal ancient city (addressed in the companion Isaiah document). On this reading, Revelation borrows Ezekiel's vivid, established imagery and vocabulary to describe a final, ultimate iteration of the same recurring pattern (a climactic assault against God's people, decisively and supernaturally defeated) rather than asserting a strict historical identity between the two prophecies' specific referents. Interpreters differ considerably on how, or whether, Ezekiel's own prophecy has already been historically fulfilled, remains to be fulfilled in a future, more immediate sense, or should itself be read symbolically -- a question this project's general even-handedness commitments lead it not to adjudicate, since it touches on actively debated eschatological schools of interpretation.

## Law and Practice

### 11. Priesthood restricted to 'the sons of Zadok' in Ezekiel's future Temple vision

**References:** Ezekiel 44:15-31; Numbers 3:5-10; 1 Kings 2:26-27

**The claim:** Ezekiel's extended vision of a future, idealized Temple (chapters 40-48) restricts full priestly service specifically to 'the Levitical priests, the sons of Zadok' (44:15), demoting other Levites who had strayed into idolatry to a lesser support role (44:10-14) -- a narrower restriction than the general Levitical priesthood (all descendants of Aaron) established in the Torah (Numbers 3:5-10), raising a question about how this future vision's more restrictive priestly arrangement relates to the Torah's own earlier, broader provisions.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ezekiel 44:15-31, Numbers 3:5-10, and 1 Kings 2:26-27 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the historical elevation of Zadok's specific line within the broader Aaronic priesthood, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as a contradiction of the Torah's general provisions, but as Ezekiel's vision addressing a specific historical circumstance already established well before Ezekiel's own time: Zadok's own line had already been specifically and providentially elevated within the broader Aaronic priesthood generations earlier, when King Solomon installed Zadok as high priest in place of Abiathar's line, following Abiathar's involvement in Adonijah's attempted usurpation of the throne (1 Kings 2:26-27, itself explicitly said to fulfill an earlier prophetic judgment against Eli's line, 1 Samuel 2:30-36) -- Zadok's descendants had, by Ezekiel's own day, already functioned as Judah's legitimate high-priestly line for centuries. Ezekiel's vision is generally read as addressing the specific reality that many ordinary Levites (though remaining legitimately descended from Aaron) had become compromised through participation in idolatrous worship at various points in Israel's history (44:10, explicitly citing this as the reason for their reduced role), while the Zadokite priestly line specifically had remained faithful -- a distinction of faithfulness within the existing, broader Aaronic priesthood, rather than the introduction of an entirely new, narrower category unrelated to the Torah's own established priestly line.

## Names and Identity

### 12. 'Noah, Daniel, and Job' -- the biblical Daniel, or an ancient legendary figure of the same name?

**References:** Ezekiel 14:14, 20; Ezekiel 28:3

**The claim:** Ezekiel twice groups 'Noah, Daniel, and Job' together as three exemplary righteous men from the distant past whose righteousness alone would not be sufficient to save a sinful land or their own children (14:14, 20). Since Noah and Job are both extremely ancient, pre-Israelite figures, and Daniel (the biblical prophet by that name, taken to Babylon as a young man) was Ezekiel's own younger contemporary at the very time this prophecy was delivered, some scholars question whether 'Daniel' here refers to the biblical prophet at all, rather than a different, more ancient legendary figure of the same name known from other ancient Near Eastern literature (a wise and righteous judge figure named Daniel/Danel, attested in texts from ancient Ugarit predating the biblical prophet by many centuries).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'Daniel' at Ezekiel 14:14, 20, and 28:3 using the same English spelling as the book of Daniel itself, without distinguishing between a possible ancient legendary figure and the biblical prophet in the text; the identification question is a matter of historical and philological scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated identification among scholars, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Those favoring identification with the biblical prophet Daniel note that Daniel had already, by this point in Ezekiel's own ministry, achieved a considerable public reputation for wisdom and faithfulness at the Babylonian court (events recorded in Daniel 1-4 place his rise to prominence relatively early in the exile, potentially well before Ezekiel delivered this specific oracle), making it plausible that Ezekiel, himself in Babylonian exile at the same time, would already know of and cite his younger contemporary's reputation for righteousness alongside two of the most ancient exemplary figures in Israel's own tradition. Those favoring the ancient legendary 'Danel' figure note the specific Hebrew spelling used in these two verses differs slightly from the spelling used for the biblical prophet Daniel elsewhere in the book that bears his name, and that grouping a contemporary alongside two figures from the most distant past (Noah, from before the flood, and Job, traditionally dated to the patriarchal period) would be a striking and somewhat unusual literary choice, whereas all three being drawn from ancient legendary or venerable tradition would create a more consistent set. Ezekiel 28:3's later reference to the proud king of Tyre being no 'wiser than Daniel' uses the identical spelling found in 14:14, 20, which some read as supporting a consistent reference (in all three instances) to the same figure, whichever one that is.
