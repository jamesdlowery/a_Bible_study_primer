# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Jeremiah

This document catalogs 14 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual and
compositional questions involving the book of Jeremiah, either entirely internal to the book
or in comparison with 2 Kings, 1 Chronicles, Matthew, Numbers, Titus, 2 Chronicles, Daniel,
and Hebrews. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes
how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's
own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and
gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's textual history:** Entry 1 addresses one of the most substantial
textual-critical facts covered anywhere in this project -- Jeremiah exists in two genuinely
different ancient forms (a longer, differently-arranged Masoretic Hebrew text, which every
standard English translation follows, and a shorter Greek Septuagint text-form independently
confirmed by Dead Sea Scroll fragments). This is a feature of the book's transmission history
invisible to readers of any single English translation.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Textual History

### 1. Two significantly different textual forms of Jeremiah -- a longer Hebrew, and a notably shorter Greek

**References:** Jeremiah (entire book)

**The claim:** The book of Jeremiah exists in two substantially different ancient textual forms: the Masoretic Hebrew text (which every standard English translation, including all ten surveyed in this project, follows as its base text) is roughly one-eighth longer than the ancient Greek Septuagint translation of Jeremiah, and the material is arranged in a notably different order -- most strikingly, the extended oracles against foreign nations (chapters 46-51 in the Masoretic Hebrew) appear in a completely different location, inserted in the middle of chapter 25, in the Septuagint's arrangement.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations follow the longer, Masoretic Hebrew arrangement and content of Jeremiah as their base text, without incorporating or footnoting the shorter Septuagint/Dead Sea Scrolls text-form's substantial differences in length and chapter order; this is a genuine feature of the book's transmission history, not a matter of translation choice among the standard English Bibles surveyed here.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most substantial and well-documented textual-critical facts addressed anywhere in this project, and unlike many isolated numeral disputes, it is not merely a matter of a Greek translator's loose paraphrase: fragments of Jeremiah found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran (most significantly 4QJerb and 4QJerd) preserve a Hebrew text-form that agrees closely with the shorter, differently-arranged Septuagint version rather than with the longer Masoretic tradition -- direct manuscript evidence that two distinct Hebrew editions of Jeremiah circulated in ancient Israel before either the Masoretic or Septuagint traditions were fixed, rather than the difference arising solely from a Greek translator's editorial choices. Most textual scholars conclude the book underwent a documented process of expansion and rearrangement at the hands of ancient Hebrew scribes and editors (a process some connect to Jeremiah 36's own account of the prophet's scroll being burned and then rewritten 'with many similar words added,' 36:32, suggesting the book's own text describes something like the kind of expansion textual criticism has independently detected), with the longer, rearranged Masoretic edition becoming the standard form Judaism and later Christianity received, while the shorter, differently-ordered edition (attested at Qumran and in the Septuagint) represents an earlier or alternate stage of the book's development. Every standard English translation follows the longer Masoretic arrangement as its base text, meaning this substantial textual history is generally invisible to readers of any single modern English Bible without consulting specialized scholarship.

## Theological Tensions

### 2. 'I did not speak to them... concerning burnt offerings' -- denying the Sinai sacrificial legislation?

**References:** Jeremiah 7:22-23; Exodus 20-Leviticus 27

**The claim:** Jeremiah 7:22-23 has God declare, 'For in the day that I brought your fathers out of the land of Egypt, I did not speak to them or command them concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices. But this command I gave them: Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people' -- language that, read at face value, flatly denies that God gave any sacrificial commands at the time of the Exodus, apparently contradicting the Torah's own extensive, detailed sacrificial legislation given at Sinai (Exodus 20 onward, and throughout Leviticus).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jeremiah 7:22-23 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing a recognized pattern of emphatic Hebrew relative-priority language, well attested elsewhere in the prophetic literature, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this passage as a case of emphatic Hebrew idiom (a rhetorical device using an absolute negative to express a strong relative priority, well attested elsewhere in Scripture -- compare Hosea 6:6's similarly absolute-sounding 'I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice,' addressed at greater length in this project's companion documents on the Minor Prophets, and 1 Samuel 15:22's 'to obey is better than sacrifice') rather than a literal, historically precise denial that any sacrificial commands were given. On this reading, Jeremiah's point is a matter of relative priority and sequence, not an absolute historical denial: the fundamental covenant relationship and moral obedience ('obey my voice, and I will be your God') was the primary and logically prior demand at Sinai, of which the sacrificial system was always meant to be a subordinate, supporting expression -- not an end in itself, and never intended to function as a substitute for genuine covenant obedience, which is exactly the corrupted use of sacrifice Jeremiah's contemporaries were making it (the wider context of chapter 7 condemns exactly this kind of hollow ritualism divorced from ethical faithfulness, echoing Isaiah 1:11-17's similar critique addressed in the companion Isaiah document). Some scholars additionally note the sacrificial legislation given at Sinai itself came somewhat later in the wilderness narrative sequence than the initial Exodus and the giving of the Ten Commandments and core covenant obligations, offering a further, more narrowly chronological sense in which sacrificial commands were not what God 'spoke to them' on the specific day he 'brought your fathers out of Egypt.'

### 3. 'I will make a new covenant' -- renewal of the Mosaic covenant, or its supersession?

**References:** Jeremiah 31:31-34; Exodus 24:3-8; Hebrews 8:6-13

**The claim:** Jeremiah 31:31-34 announces a striking future promise: 'Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, not like the covenant that I made with their fathers... which they broke' -- language explicitly distinguishing this coming covenant from the Sinai covenant, raising a question about how this 'new' covenant relates to, and whether it replaces, the covenant given through Moses, which itself contained language suggesting permanence and perpetual obligation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jeremiah 31:31-34 and Hebrews 8:6-13 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the new covenant as addressing a disclosed defect in Israel's own capacity to keep the first covenant, not a claim that God's original covenant terms were themselves flawed or dishonestly offered, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in this project's companion documents addressing similar covenant-permanence language (particularly regarding the Levitical priesthood and sacrificial system in the Leviticus document, and the Davidic covenant's conditional and unconditional dimensions in the 1 Kings and Psalms documents). Most commentators read Jeremiah's own explicit statement of the reason for a new covenant (31:32, 'because they broke my covenant') as identifying the problem not with the Sinai covenant's own terms or God's own faithfulness to it, but with Israel's persistent inability to keep it -- the 'newness' Jeremiah announces is explicitly located in an internal transformation absent from the first covenant's arrangement: 'I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts' (31:33), addressing the root problem (human hearts inclined toward disobedience) rather than merely repeating the same external covenant terms Israel had already shown itself unable to keep. Hebrews 8:6-13, quoting this passage at length, explicitly identifies this new covenant with the arrangement inaugurated through Christ, describing the first covenant as having become 'obsolete' specifically in light of this promised replacement -- language this project's Leviticus document addresses at greater length regarding the semantic range of covenant-permanence terminology, concluding that the Sinai covenant's own provisions were always understood, even within the Old Testament's own developing theology, as pointing toward and anticipating a fuller, final arrangement rather than constituting an unconditional, unchangeable final word in themselves.

### 4. David and the Levites will 'never lack a man' -- an unconditional promise, given as the monarchy and Temple were ending

**References:** Jeremiah 33:17-18; Jeremiah 33:19-26

**The claim:** Jeremiah 33:17-18 promises, 'David shall never lack a man to sit on the throne of the house of Israel, and the Levitical priests shall never lack a man in my presence to offer burnt offerings' -- an unconditional-sounding promise of perpetual dynastic and priestly continuity, delivered, notably, during the very period (Jeremiah's own ministry, during the Babylonian siege and destruction of Jerusalem) when both the Davidic monarchy and the Levitical sacrificial system were about to be, and shortly were, interrupted by the exile.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jeremiah 33:17-26 plainly and consistently as given, including the cosmic day/night analogy clarifying the promise's scope; the harmonization rests on reading the promise's own immediate qualifying context, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jeremiah 33:19-26 itself immediately continues and clarifies this promise using an explicit analogy: just as God's covenant with day and night (the fixed, unbreakable order of the created cosmos) cannot be broken, so too his covenant with David and the Levitical priests cannot be broken (33:20-21) -- language most commentators read as affirming the ultimate, cosmic certainty and eventual fulfillment of the promise across the full span of redemptive history, not a claim that the Davidic throne and Levitical priesthood would experience no interruption whatsoever at any point, including the specific historical exile the immediate context assumes is imminent or already underway. This is treated as an example of the same conditional/unconditional distinction addressed at length in this project's companion 1 Kings and Psalms documents regarding the Davidic covenant -- the ultimate, final fulfillment of David never lacking an heir on his throne is understood by most Christian interpreters as fulfilled definitively and permanently in Christ's own eternal kingship (compare Luke 1:32-33), while the Levitical priesthood's own perpetual continuation is understood in light of Hebrews' argument (addressed in entry 3 above and the companion Leviticus document) that Christ's own priesthood fulfills and supersedes, without contradicting, the pattern the Levitical priesthood typologically anticipated.

### 5. The clearest statement in Scripture of God's conditional dealing with nations

**References:** Jeremiah 18:7-10

**The claim:** Jeremiah 18:7-10 states explicitly, in the clearest and most systematic terms found anywhere in the Bible, a principle of conditional divine action: 'If at any time I declare concerning a nation... that I will pluck up... but if that nation turns from its evil... I will relent... And if at any time I declare concerning a nation... that I will build and plant it, but if it does evil in my sight... then I will relent of the good that I had intended.' This raises the same general question addressed elsewhere in this project regarding whether God's stated intentions and pronouncements can change in response to human response and repentance.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jeremiah 18:7-10 plainly and consistently as given; this passage functions as an explanatory key elsewhere in this project rather than itself requiring harmonization, and there is no translation variance affecting this reading.

**Reconciliation:** Rather than presenting a difficulty, this passage is generally read as the key text supplying the Bible's own explicit, systematic statement of the principle this project has applied in several other entries to resolve apparent tensions regarding whether God 'changes his mind' (addressed at length regarding Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 versus Genesis 6:6, Exodus 32:14, and Jonah 3:10, and again regarding 1 Samuel 15's own internal tension within a single chapter) -- Jeremiah 18:7-10 states directly and without ambiguity that prophetic pronouncements of judgment or blessing regarding a nation's corporate fate are consistently understood, within Israel's own prophetic tradition, as conditioned on that nation's ongoing moral response, not as fixed, unconditional decrees immune to change regardless of subsequent repentance or renewed wrongdoing. This passage is widely treated by commentators as the closest thing Scripture offers to an explicit statement of its own hermeneutical principle for reading conditional prophecy throughout the historical and prophetic books, directly illuminating cases such as Jonah's prophecy against Nineveh (which was not fulfilled, given Nineveh's repentance) without requiring the prophecy itself to have been false or the prophet mistaken.

### 6. Does God refuse to hear prayer and accept worship from a specific generation, given Scripture's promise that all who call on him will be delivered?

**References:** Jeremiah 7:16; Jeremiah 14:12; Joel 2:32; Romans 10:13

**The claim:** God twice tells Jeremiah not to intercede for the people, and declares he will not hear them: 'pray not thou for this people... for I will not hear thee' (7:16), and 'when they fast, I will not hear their cry; and when they offer burnt offering and an oblation, I will not accept them' (14:12). Elsewhere, Scripture promises without qualification that 'whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered' (Joel 2:32, quoted again in Romans 10:13).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both Jeremiah passages address a specific, time-bound historical judgment already determined against a particular generation whose persistent, unrepentant covenant-breaking (detailed at length in the surrounding chapters, including continued idolatry and injustice alongside hollow ritual observance) had reached a point where God had settled on judgment as certain and imminent -- not a categorical statement that God never hears anyone's prayer or worship, nor a denial of the general promise that genuine calling on the LORD brings deliverance. The fasting and offerings in 14:12 are explicitly tied to a request that God avert the coming judgment, which God declares he will not do at this point in the nation's history, given as compensation for the covenant already so thoroughly and repeatedly broken -- not a refusal to hear repentant, faithful prayer as such, which this same book elsewhere assumes continues (Jeremiah's own extensive personal prayers and laments throughout the book are never described as unheard by God). This is the same kind of specific, judicial withholding addressed elsewhere in this project regarding 1 Samuel 8:18's warning that God would not hear Israel's complaint about the king they insisted on having -- a targeted consequence tied to one particular situation, not a general denial of Joel 2:32's and Romans 10:13's unconditional promise to all who call on the LORD.

### 7. Should believers look for and heed signs in the heavens?

**References:** Jeremiah 10:2; Matthew 2:1-2; Luke 21:25

**The claim:** Jeremiah 10:2 warns, 'Learn not the way of the heathen, and be not dismayed at the signs of heaven; for the heathen are dismayed at them.' Yet Scripture elsewhere describes God himself using unusual heavenly phenomena as genuine signs -- a star leading the magi to the infant Jesus (Matthew 2:1-2), and Jesus' own description of future 'signs in the sun, and in the moon, and in the stars' preceding his return (Luke 21:25).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jeremiah 10:2 is addressed specifically against pagan astrology -- the practice, widespread among Israel's neighbors, of reading ordinary celestial phenomena (the routine positions and movements of stars and planets) as omens governing fate, to be anxiously divined and feared as controlling powers in their own right, a practice the surrounding chapter explicitly connects to idolatry (10:1-16 as a whole mocks the folly of idol-worship generally). This is a categorically different matter from Scripture's own occasional accounts of God sovereignly using an extraordinary, particular heavenly phenomenon as a specific, revealed sign of his own action at a specific moment (the star of Bethlehem, or the end-times signs Jesus describes) -- in these cases, God himself is the source and the sign points to his own specific redemptive activity, rather than the observer anxiously divining an impersonal, deterministic fate from the ordinary heavens the way pagan astrology claimed to do. Condemning superstitious astrological divination and affirming that God can sovereignly use an extraordinary heavenly event as his own sign are not competing claims.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 8. Jehoiakim's prophesied burial 'like a donkey' -- vs. 2 Kings' more neutral report

**References:** Jeremiah 22:18-19; Jeremiah 36:30; 2 Kings 24:6

**The claim:** Jeremiah twice pronounces a harsh judgment on King Jehoiakim's death and burial: 'They shall not lament for him... with the burial of a donkey he shall be buried, dragged and dumped beyond the gates of Jerusalem' (22:18-19), and later, 'he shall have none to sit on the throne of David, and his dead body shall be cast out to the heat by day and the frost by night' (36:30) -- language describing a shameful, undignified death without proper burial. 2 Kings 24:6, by contrast, reports simply and neutrally that 'Jehoiakim slept with his fathers,' a standard formula elsewhere in Kings typically implying an ordinary, honorable royal burial and succession.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both Jeremiah's oracles and 2 Kings 24:6's formulaic notice plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the limited, formulaic nature of the Kings notice rather than reading it as a specific claim about burial honor, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read 2 Kings 24:6's formula ('slept with his fathers') as this book's standard, formulaic transition marking the end of a king's reign and the succession of his son, rather than as a specific claim about the manner, location, or dignity of his burial. The same formula is used of kings elsewhere whose deaths are described very differently or in more violent terms in fuller detail -- a pattern of formulaic summary language this project has addressed regarding other regnal-succession notices. 2 Kings itself provides no further burial detail for Jehoiakim specifically, unlike its notices for several other kings, which do specify burial location. Several commentators read this silence as consistent with, rather than contradicting, Jeremiah's account of an unusual, dishonorable death without the normal royal burial rites. The brevity and formulaic nature of the Kings notice does not itself assert the kind of honorable burial the same phrase might imply elsewhere, and the historical circumstances of Jehoiakim's final days -- during the early stages of Nebuchadnezzar's siege and eventual capture of Jerusalem (2 Kings 24:1-2) -- are consistent with the kind of chaotic, dishonorable death and disposal Jeremiah's oracles describe.

### 9. 'Write this man down as childless' (Jehoiachin) -- yet he had sons, and appears in Jesus' own genealogy

**References:** Jeremiah 22:24-30; 1 Chronicles 3:17-18; Matthew 1:11-12

**The claim:** Jeremiah pronounces a severe judgment on King Jehoiachin (also called Coniah): 'Write this man down as childless, a man who shall not succeed in his days, for none of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David and ruling again in Judah' (22:30). Yet 1 Chronicles 3:17-18 explicitly lists several named sons of Jehoiachin (Shealtiel, Malchiram, Pedaiah, and others), directly contradicting a literal reading of 'childless.' Compounding this, Matthew 1:11-12 places Jehoiachin directly in Jesus' own legal genealogy through Joseph, a descendant of exactly the Davidic line Jeremiah's curse seems to permanently disqualify from the throne.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jeremiah 22:30, 1 Chronicles 3:17-18, and Matthew 1:11-12 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing dynastic/throne-succession language from a literal biological claim, and on recognizing the two distinct Davidic lines traced by Matthew and Luke, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read 'childless' in 22:30 not as a literal biological claim (which 1 Chronicles 3:17-18 already shows to be false, and which the verse's own second half immediately clarifies is not the primary point: 'for none of his offspring shall succeed...') but as a statement about dynastic and political outcome -- Jehoiachin would be treated, for the purposes of the Davidic throne's succession, as though he had no viable heir, since none of his actual descendants would ever again reign as king over Judah on David's throne, a judgment that held true for the remainder of the Old Testament's own historical record (the line continued as a prominent family but never again reigned as sovereign kings in Jerusalem). On the Christological question, most conservative theologians hold this is precisely why Jesus' throne right is most securely established not through Joseph's legal, adoptive descent from Jehoiachin's cursed line (which Matthew's genealogy traces for legal purposes, showing Joseph's rightful legal claim to David's throne, which Jesus would inherit as Joseph's legal, adopted son without being Joseph's biological son and therefore without personally bearing Jehoiachin's blood-curse) but through Mary's own separate, biological descent from David via a different son, Nathan (traced in Luke's genealogy, addressed at greater length in this project's general document set regarding the differences between Matthew's and Luke's genealogies) -- meaning Jesus inherits the legal Davidic throne-right through Joseph while avoiding Jehoiachin's specific biological curse through his actual, virgin-born descent via Mary's different Davidic line. This reading, while not universally held, is widely offered as resolving both the biological 'childless' problem and its Christological implications together.

### 10. 'You have deceived me, O LORD' -- a prophet's raw accusation against God

**References:** Jeremiah 20:7; Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2

**The claim:** In one of his most anguished personal laments (traditionally called his 'confessions'), Jeremiah cries out, 'O LORD, you have deceived me, and I was deceived; you are stronger than I, and you have prevailed' (20:7) -- language that reads as a direct, personal accusation that God deceived him, in apparent tension with Scripture's repeated affirmations that God cannot lie or deceive (Numbers 23:19, Titus 1:2).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'You have persuaded me and I was deceived,' a mixed rendering using a softer verb for God's action but retaining 'deceived' for its effect (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'You deceived me, LORD, and I was deceived,' matching the stronger wording (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'you have deceived me, and I was deceived,' matching the KJV's stronger wording (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'thou hast deceived me, and I was deceived,' the strongest and most provocative-sounding rendering (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'You induced me, and I was persuaded,' a notably softer rendering that departs significantly from the KJV's own 'deceived' at this specific verse (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'You have deceived me and I was deceived,' also matching the stronger wording (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'O Yahweh, You have enticed me, and I was enticed,' notably departing from NASB's 'deceived' at this specific verse -- a softer rendering closer to NKJV's 'induced/persuaded' than to NASB's own wording, despite LSB's usual close tracking of NASB (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'You deceived me, LORD, and I was deceived,' also using 'deceived' (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'you misled me, and I allowed myself to be misled,' a moderately softer rendering than 'deceived.'
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'O LORD, you have deceived me, and I was deceived; you are stronger than I, and you have prevailed,' matching the stronger wording (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this passage, like several of Jeremiah's other raw, personal 'confessions' scattered throughout the book, as an honest record of the prophet's own anguished, momentary perception and complaint under extreme social pressure, mockery, and persecution for faithfully delivering God's word (the immediate context, 20:7-10, describes Jeremiah's continuous public ridicule and the temptation to abandon his prophetic calling entirely) -- not as an inspired doctrinal assertion that God had, in fact, literally deceived him. The underlying Hebrew verb (pathah) has a range of meaning from 'deceive' to the milder senses of 'persuade,' 'entice,' or 'induce,' which several translations reflect in their own specific word choices at this verse; on either rendering, the passage is generally read as expressing Jeremiah's felt sense that God had drawn him into a prophetic calling far more costly, painful, and socially humiliating than he had anticipated when first called (compare his call narrative's own emphasis on youth and reluctance, 1:6-8) -- an honest, unfiltered expression of a faithful person's momentary struggle and sense of having been overpowered by God's call, in the same category as several other biblical laments this project has addressed (Job's cursing of his own birth, Ecclesiastes' darkest statements) that record genuine anguish without functioning as settled theological doctrine.

### 11. Called and set apart before birth -- yet cursing the very day of his own birth

**References:** Jeremiah 1:5; Jeremiah 20:14-18

**The claim:** Jeremiah's call narrative opens with God's declaration, 'Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, and before you were born I consecrated you; I appointed you a prophet to the nations' (1:5) -- a striking statement of purposeful, pre-natal calling and destiny. Yet later in the book, in the depths of persecution and despair, Jeremiah curses the very day of his own birth in some of the darkest language in the prophetic literature: 'Cursed be the day on which I was born... why did I come out from the womb to see toil and sorrow, and spend my days in shame?' (20:14-18).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing an honest record of situational despair from a retraction of an earlier stated calling, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This tension parallels similar cases already addressed at greater length in this project's companion Job and Ecclesiastes documents (regarding Job's own curse of his birth day in Job 3, and Ecclesiastes' 'better unborn than alive' statements) -- most commentators read Jeremiah's lament here in the same way: as an honest, unfiltered record of a faithful person's genuine anguish and momentary despair at a specific low point, rather than a retraction of, or genuine theological contradiction with, God's own earlier stated purpose for his life. The book itself does not present Jeremiah's despair in chapter 20 as God's own assessment or as canceling the calling declared in chapter 1 -- rather, Jeremiah continues faithfully prophesying for many more years after this lament, consistent with a reading in which Scripture records without either censoring or theologically endorsing as final doctrine even its most faithful figures' most extreme, anguished statements, made under conditions of genuine and severe suffering.

## Chronology

### 12. Seventy years of Babylonian captivity

**References:** Jeremiah 25:11-12; Jeremiah 29:10; 2 Chronicles 36:20-21; Daniel 9:2

**The claim:** Jeremiah twice specifies a seventy-year period for Babylon's dominance and Judah's exile (25:11-12, 29:10) -- a figure that does not correspond exactly to any single, easily identified seventy-year span depending on which start and end dates are used (the first deportation in 605 BC, the Temple's destruction in 586 BC, and the actual return under Cyrus's decree in 538 BC yield different spans when measured against each other, none of them precisely seventy years by every possible reckoning).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'seventy years' at Jeremiah 25:11-12 and 29:10 identically; no translation differences are involved in this chronological question.

**Reconciliation:** This figure is treated at greater length in this project's companion 2 Chronicles document, which addresses 2 Chronicles 36:20-21's own explicit theological explanation of the seventy years as compensation for the land's accumulated, unobserved Sabbath years. On the specific chronological question, most scholars hold that seventy years is best measured from the first major Babylonian deportation and the beginning of Babylonian political dominance over Judah (605 BC, the year Daniel and other nobles were first taken to Babylon, per Daniel 1:1-6) to the decree of Cyrus permitting return (538 BC) or the actual rebuilding of the Temple's foundations shortly afterward -- a span that, on this reckoning, comes very close to seventy years, and which Daniel himself, reading Jeremiah's prophecy near the end of the exile, explicitly understood as nearing its stated fulfillment (Daniel 9:2). Some scholars alternatively measure from the Temple's actual destruction (586 BC) to its rebuilding's completion (516 BC under Darius), which also yields approximately seventy years by a different pairing of start and end points -- both reckonings are defended, and the general seventy-year figure is treated by most scholars as substantially, though perhaps not to the exact year by every possible calculation, historically accurate regardless of which specific pair of dates is used.

## Literary Composition

### 13. Two versions of Jeremiah's Temple sermon

**References:** Jeremiah 7:1-15; Jeremiah 26:1-24

**The claim:** Jeremiah 7 and Jeremiah 26 both record Jeremiah delivering a sermon at the Temple gate condemning false confidence in the Temple's presence as a guarantee of safety despite the nation's moral and covenant unfaithfulness -- the same core message, in the same setting, but presented with different emphases: chapter 7 gives an extended, detailed account of the sermon's actual content, while chapter 26 gives a much briefer summary of the message but a considerably fuller narrative account of its aftermath (the priests' and prophets' demand for Jeremiah's death, his trial, and his defense and acquittal).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both chapters plainly and consistently as given, without cross-referencing or merging them in the text; the harmonization is a matter of recognizing complementary selective emphasis within the book's own structure, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as two complementary accounts of a single historical event, presented for different literary purposes at different points in the book's overall structure, rather than as two separate sermons or as conflicting records of the same one -- chapter 7 is positioned within a larger collection of Jeremiah's oracles and sermons, appropriately emphasizing the sermon's actual theological content and message, while chapter 26 is positioned within a narrative section of the book more focused on Jeremiah's own personal experience of persecution and vindication as a true prophet, appropriately emphasizing the sermon's dramatic aftermath and Jeremiah's own legal defense rather than repeating the sermon's full content already given in chapter 7. This pattern -- the same underlying event narrated more than once with different selective emphasis depending on a book's larger structural purpose at that point -- has been addressed elsewhere in this project regarding similar cases (including Manasseh's reign as recorded differently in Kings and Chronicles), and is a recognized and unremarkable feature of ancient historical narrative composition.

### 14. Chapter 52: a later appendix, closely copied from 2 Kings and omitting Jeremiah's own name entirely

**References:** Jeremiah 52:1-34; 2 Kings 24:18-25:30; Jeremiah 39:1-14; Jeremiah 40-44

**The claim:** Jeremiah's final chapter (52) closely parallels 2 Kings 24:18-25:30 in extensive, often nearly word-for-word detail, recounting Jerusalem's fall, Zedekiah's capture and fate, the exile, and Jehoiachin's eventual release from Babylonian prison -- yet this closing chapter of 'Jeremiah's' own book never once mentions Jeremiah by name, and conspicuously omits material the book itself has already narrated at length in earlier chapters concerning events after Jerusalem's fall (Gedaliah's brief governorship and assassination, and the flight to Egypt that Jeremiah himself was forced to join, recounted in chapters 40-44) -- raising a question about whether this closing chapter was composed by Jeremiah himself or added later by a different hand.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Jeremiah 52 and its own internal marker at 51:64 exactly as given, without commenting on or resolving the compositional question through translation choice; this is a matter of literary and historical scholarship, not translation.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, including many with a high view of the book's inspiration, read chapter 52 as a later editorial appendix -- closely related to, and probably substantially drawn from, the same source material underlying 2 Kings 24-25 (or directly copied from an early form of that text) -- added to the end of Jeremiah's own book, likely by an editor compiling and completing the book after Jeremiah's own lifetime, to supply a fuller historical conclusion and context for the oracles the book itself has already recorded (this reading finds some support in the book's own internal notice at 51:64, 'Thus far are the words of Jeremiah,' immediately preceding chapter 52 -- a statement several commentators read as the compiler's own explicit marker that what follows is an appended historical supplement rather than part of Jeremiah's own recorded words). This pattern of a later editorial appendix supplying additional historical context at a book's conclusion has been addressed elsewhere in this project regarding comparable cases (the account of Moses' own death at the end of Deuteronomy, and Nehemiah 12's high-priestly genealogy extending beyond Nehemiah's own lifetime), and is generally treated as compatible with, rather than undermining, the substantial books' overall attribution to their named prophetic authors.
