# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Exodus

This document catalogs 35 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Exodus, either entirely internal to Exodus or between Exodus and a directly related passage
elsewhere (Genesis, Deuteronomy, Leviticus, Galatians, Acts). For each item, this document
states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim (for example, Deuteronomy 16:8's "six days" against Exodus 12:15's "seven days," and
1 Kings 8:9's description of the ark's contents). Where no such check turned up a meaningful
difference, the entry states that all ten translations render the passage similarly --
reflecting the fact that most of these claims turn on a disagreement between two biblical
passages rather than on how any single verse happens to be translated, not an unchecked
assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Names and Identity

### 1. Moses' father-in-law: Reuel, Jethro, or Hobab?

**References:** Exodus 2:18; Exodus 3:1; Exodus 18:1, 5; Numbers 10:29; Judges 4:11

**The claim:** Exodus 2:18 names the Midianite priest whose daughters Moses helps as 'Reuel.' Exodus 3:1 and 18:1 call Moses' father-in-law 'Jethro.' Numbers 10:29 and Judges 4:11 refer to 'Hobab,' described there as either Moses' father-in-law or (in some readings) his brother-in-law -- raising the question of whether these are one man given three names, or the text has confused generations.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the three names (Reuel, Jethro, Hobab) exactly as given in their respective passages, without harmonizing them into one name in the text; the resolution is exegetical, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization treats 'Reuel' and 'Jethro' as the same individual under two names, a documented and unremarkable pattern for the period (compare Gideon/Jerubbaal, Solomon/Jedidiah) -- 'Jethro' (meaning roughly 'his excellency') plausibly functioning as an honorific or priestly title for a man whose personal name was Reuel. 'Hobab' is more debated: many harmonists take Numbers 10:29's Hebrew (which can be read either as 'Hobab, son of Reuel the Midianite, Moses' father-in-law' or 'Hobab, son of Reuel the Midianite, father-in-law of Moses') to mean Hobab was Reuel/Jethro's son and therefore Moses' brother-in-law, not his father-in-law, with the Hebrew term for '-in-law' (choten) being broad enough in Hebrew usage to cover the relationship loosely.

### 2. Was the divine name YHWH known before Moses?

**References:** Exodus 6:2-3; Genesis 4:26; Genesis 12:8; Genesis 22:14; Genesis 15:2; Genesis 28:13

**The claim:** Exodus 6:2-3 has God tell Moses, 'I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob as God Almighty, but by my name the LORD [YHWH] I did not make myself known to them.' Yet Genesis repeatedly has the patriarchs -- and even earlier figures -- using the name YHWH: Genesis 4:26 says people 'began to call upon the name of the LORD' in the days of Seth's son Enosh, and Abraham himself addresses God as YHWH by name (Genesis 15:2) and builds an altar 'calling on the name of the LORD' (Genesis 12:8).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'YHWH' as 'LORD' (in small capitals) consistently throughout both Genesis and Exodus, preserving the same underlying tension; the resolution is exegetical, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Several harmonizations are widely offered. First, Genesis -- composed and/or compiled after the events of Exodus -- may consistently use the name YHWH retrospectively and editorially throughout the patriarchal narratives, even though the patriarchs themselves, in the flow of the actual history, did not yet use or fully understand this name; an author writing later routinely uses names anachronistically for a reader's clarity, without claiming the characters used them at the time. Second, Exodus 6:3 may mean not that the bare name was previously unspoken, but that its full character and significance -- God's covenant-keeping power to deliver, which the name YHWH is about to be dramatically demonstrated to mean -- had not yet been experientially 'known' by the patriarchs. Hebrew 'to know' (yada) often implies experiential knowledge rather than mere awareness of a label, so the patriarchs could have known the word YHWH while not having 'known' God in the fuller covenantal sense the Exodus generation was about to.

### 3. Did Moses fear Pharaoh, or did he leave Egypt without fear?

**References:** Exodus 2:14-15; Hebrews 11:27

**The claim:** Exodus 2:14-15 has Moses afraid after his killing of an Egyptian becomes known, fleeing Pharaoh's reach to Midian. Hebrews 11:27 states that Moses 'forsook Egypt, not fearing the wrath of the king.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two texts are standardly read as describing two different departures from Egypt rather than the same moment: Exodus 2's flight follows Moses' panicked realization that his killing of the Egyptian was known, an act of immediate self-preservation, while Hebrews 11:27 is widely understood by commentators to refer instead to the later, final Exodus departure under Moses' leadership decades afterward, undertaken in open defiance of Pharaoh and by deliberate faith rather than panic, consistent with Hebrews 11's overall focus on the Exodus generation's faith (the surrounding verses 11:28-29 describe the Passover and the Red Sea crossing, both from that later departure). On this reading, Hebrews is not describing the flight of Exodus 2 at all, so there is no single moment the two texts describe inconsistently.

### 4. Who appeared to Moses at the burning bush -- the angel of the LORD, or God himself?

**References:** Exodus 3:2, 4, 6

**The claim:** Exodus 3:2 states that 'the angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire,' but the very next verses have 'God' and 'the LORD' calling to Moses and identifying himself as 'the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob' (3:4, 6), without the text distinguishing the angel from God as two separate speakers.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of several Old Testament passages (compare Genesis 16:7-13; Genesis 22:11-18; Judges 6:11-24) in which 'the angel of the LORD' is presented as speaking and acting with a directness and divine authority indistinguishable from God speaking in his own person -- a recurring figure many conservative theologians identify as a visible, pre-incarnate manifestation of God (some specifically identifying this 'Angel' with the Son, given the New Testament's teaching that no one has seen the Father directly, John 1:18) rather than a created, subordinate messenger of the kind 'angel' usually designates elsewhere. Read this way, Exodus 3:2's 'angel of the LORD' and 3:4's 'God'/'the LORD' are the same speaker described from two angles, not two different beings in dialogue.

### 5. What is God's name -- 'I AM THAT I AM,' or the LORD (YHWH)?

**References:** Exodus 3:13-15

**The claim:** When Moses asks what he should say if the Israelites ask God's name, God first answers 'I AM THAT I AM' (3:14) and then, in the very next verse, gives a different-sounding answer: 'the LORD God of your fathers... this is my name for ever' (3:15) -- using the personal name YHWH instead.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two verses are read by the great majority of commentators as directly connected rather than competing: 'I AM THAT I AM' (Hebrew ehyeh asher ehyeh, from the verb 'to be') is widely taken as an explanation of the meaning behind the name YHWH given immediately afterward, since YHWH itself is built from the same Hebrew root for 'to be/exist.' On this reading, 3:14 supplies the meaning ('I am the self-existent, ever-present one') and 3:15 supplies the actual name (YHWH) that encodes that meaning -- a definition followed by the term it defines, not two rival answers to the same question.

### 6. Was Moses a poor public speaker, or was he 'mighty in words and deeds'?

**References:** Exodus 4:10; Acts 7:22

**The claim:** Exodus 4:10 has Moses protesting to God that he is 'not eloquent... I am slow of speech, and of a slow tongue.' Acts 7:22, recounting the same man's life, describes Moses as having been 'mighty in words and in deeds.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization notes that the two statements describe different periods and different kinds of speaking ability: Acts 7:22 describes Moses' formal education and eloquence as a young man raised 'in all the wisdom of the Egyptians,' a description of his rhetorical training and general capability, while Exodus 4:10 is Moses' own self-assessment decades later, as an aged shepherd being called out of forty years of exile to confront Pharaoh -- most commentators read this as Moses expressing reluctance, self-doubt, or a specific speech impediment (some suggest a stutter, based on the Hebrew phrase's repetition) rather than a claim to have never possessed rhetorical skill at all. A gifted speaker can still doubt his own present fitness for an unprecedented and intimidating commission; the two statements are not describing the same moment or the same capacity.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. If all Egyptian livestock died in the plague of pestilence, what livestock died in the later plagues?

**References:** Exodus 9:1-7; Exodus 9:9-11; Exodus 9:19-25; Exodus 12:29

**The claim:** Exodus 9:6 states that in the fifth plague (pestilence on livestock), 'all the livestock of the Egyptians died,' and verse 7 confirms 'not one of the livestock of the Israelites was dead' by contrast, implying total loss of Egyptian animals. Yet the seventh plague (hail, 9:19-25) explicitly addresses livestock left in the field as still-vulnerable, and the tenth plague (12:29) kills the firstborn of Egyptian livestock -- animals that, on a strict reading of 9:6, should no longer have existed.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'all the livestock' at Exodus 9:6 and the later plague narratives exactly as given, without softening or qualifying the term in the text; the harmonization is exegetical, resting on reading 9:6 in light of 9:19-21's own explicit assumption of surviving animals.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization notes two things the text itself supplies: first, Exodus 9:6 most likely refers only to livestock left out in the open fields at the time (a natural implication given that livestock kept sheltered would not be exposed to a pestilence spread among grazing/exposed animals), consistent with 9:19-21's explicit instruction to bring servants and livestock in from the fields to escape the coming hail, which presupposes surviving animals to shelter; second, 'all' in Hebrew narrative (kol) is frequently used as an emphatic, rhetorical totalizing term for a very severe or comprehensive event rather than a strict mathematical universal (a usage pattern well attested elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible), and would be naturally understood by an ancient audience as 'a devastating, comprehensive loss' rather than 'the literal, complete, permanent extinction of every single animal.'

### 8. Can God be seen face to face, or not -- even within Exodus itself?

**References:** Exodus 33:11; Exodus 33:20; Exodus 24:9-11; Exodus 33:23

**The claim:** Exodus 33:11 states plainly, 'the LORD used to speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend.' Only nine verses later, Exodus 33:20 has God tell Moses, 'you cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live.' Exodus 24:9-11, earlier in the same book, has Moses, Aaron, and seventy elders go up the mountain and 'see the God of Israel,' eating and drinking in his presence without apparent harm.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render all three passages (33:11, 33:18-23, 24:9-11) plainly as given, preserving the same vocabulary distinctions in each case; the harmonization rests on reading Exodus 33 as a single, self-explaining unit, not on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the clearest cases where the same book supplies its own resolving vocabulary: the surrounding verses of Exodus 33 itself distinguish an intimate, direct mode of communication ('face to face,' 33:11, describing the unusual closeness and directness of God's speech to Moses compared to other prophets, as Numbers 12:6-8 explicitly reaffirms) from a full, unmediated visual apprehension of God's essential glory, which 33:18-23 immediately goes on to describe as fatal for any mortal -- God's response to Moses' request to see his glory is to let 'my goodness pass before you' while placing Moses in the cleft of the rock and covering him with his hand, allowing Moses to see God's 'back' but not his 'face' (33:20-23), drawing the very distinction the apparent contradiction requires. Exodus 24:9-11's vision is likewise generally read as a partial, mediated theophany (a vision of God's feet and what was under them, per 24:10, not an unveiled encounter with the divine essence) rather than the kind of exposure 33:20 rules out.

### 9. Was there already a functioning 'Tent of Meeting' before the Tabernacle was built?

**References:** Exodus 33:7-11; Exodus 35:1-40:38

**The claim:** Exodus 33:7-11 describes Moses regularly pitching 'the tent of meeting... outside the camp,' where 'the LORD used to speak with Moses' and where 'the pillar of cloud' would descend -- described in ordinary, already-functioning terms. Yet the elaborate Tabernacle, also called 'the tent of meeting' throughout Exodus 35-40, is not constructed and consecrated until several chapters later, at the very end of the book.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly as given, using 'tent of meeting' for both the provisional tent of Exodus 33 and the later Tabernacle of Exodus 35-40 without distinguishing them terminologically in the text; the harmonization is exegetical, resting on context and narrative placement rather than translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, including many who hold to Mosaic authorship of the whole book, read Exodus 33:7-11 as describing a simple, provisional tent Moses used for the purpose of meeting with God during the interim period after the golden-calf crisis (immediately preceding this passage) and before the elaborate, purpose-built Tabernacle described later was constructed -- the passage itself marks this as an unusual, temporary arrangement ('outside the camp, far off from the camp'), distinct in scale and function from the later, permanently-installed sanctuary Tabernacle at the center of the camp. On this reading, the same descriptive phrase ('tent of meeting') is applied to two different structures serving a similar purpose at two different points in the narrative, a pattern of reused terminology that does not by itself require identity of structure.

### 10. Did God harden Pharaoh's heart, or did Pharaoh harden his own heart?

**References:** Exodus 4:21; Exodus 7:3; Exodus 9:12; Exodus 8:15; Exodus 8:32; Exodus 9:34

**The claim:** Several verses have God stating in advance, or narrating, that he hardened Pharaoh's heart. Other verses, especially earlier in the plague sequence, state that Pharaoh hardened his own heart.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each verse's stated agent (God or Pharaoh) plainly and consistently as given in the Hebrew; the harmonization is exegetical, tracking the sequence across the narrative, not a matter of translation.

**Reconciliation:** Reading the plague narrative sequentially, the standard observation is that the earlier plagues consistently attribute the hardening to Pharaoh's own choice (Exodus 7:13-14, 7:22, 8:15, 8:19, 8:32, 9:7), while only in the later plagues does the text shift to describing God's hardening (9:12 onward) -- widely read as God confirming and giving Pharaoh over to a direction Pharaoh had already repeatedly chosen for himself, rather than God unilaterally overriding Pharaoh's will from the outset. The initial predictive statements (4:21, 7:3) are understood as God's foreknowledge of the whole sequence stated in summary at the outset, encompassing both Pharaoh's own repeated choices and God's later confirming hardening.

### 11. Miriam's dance before the LORD, and the dancing around the golden calf -- is dancing approved, or condemned?

**References:** Exodus 15:20; Exodus 32:19

**The claim:** Exodus 15:20 has Miriam leading the women in celebratory dance after the Red Sea deliverance, presented entirely approvingly. Exodus 32:19, later in the very same book, has Moses discover the Israelites dancing around the golden calf, a scene of condemned idolatry that provokes his anger and God's judgment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The text itself supplies the distinguishing factor: the object and occasion of the dancing, not the act of dancing as such. Miriam's dance in Exodus 15 is explicitly directed toward the LORD, in celebration of his real deliverance at the Red Sea narrated in the preceding verses; the dancing in Exodus 32 is explicitly directed toward the golden calf idol Aaron had just fashioned, a direct violation of the second commandment given only weeks earlier at the very same mountain. Dancing itself is treated elsewhere in Scripture as a legitimate expression of worship and joy (Psalm 149:3; Psalm 150:4; 2 Samuel 6:14), so the two passages are not in tension over whether dancing is acceptable -- both agree the question turns entirely on whom or what the dancing honors.

### 12. What was inside the Ark of the Covenant?

**References:** Exodus 16:32-34; Numbers 17:10; Deuteronomy 10:5; 1 Kings 8:9; Hebrews 9:4

**The claim:** Exodus 16:32-34 has Aaron directed to place a pot of manna 'before the LORD, to be kept'; Numbers 17:10 similarly has Aaron's budded rod kept 'before the testimony.' Deuteronomy 10:5 states Moses placed only the two tablets of the law in the ark. Centuries later, 1 Kings 8:9, describing the ark's contents at the dedication of Solomon's temple, states plainly that 'there was nothing in the ark save the two tables of stone.' Hebrews 9:4, by contrast, describes the ark as containing 'the golden pot that had manna, and Aaron's rod that budded, and the tables of the covenant' -- all three items together.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization notes that Exodus and Numbers describe the manna and the rod as placed 'before the LORD' or 'before the testimony/ark' (a phrase most naturally read as meaning near or in front of the ark, within the Most Holy Place, rather than necessarily inside the ark's own chest), consistent with 1 Kings 8:9's later, explicit statement that only the two tablets remained inside the ark by Solomon's time -- the manna and rod having been lost, removed, or destroyed at some point across the centuries between Moses and Solomon (a span that included the ark's capture by the Philistines, 1 Samuel 4-6, during which such items could easily have gone missing). Hebrews 9:4 is widely read either as describing the ark's furnishings in the broader sense (items kept alongside or in the same sacred enclosure as the ark, a use of 'in' well attested in ancient descriptions of temple furniture generally) or as reflecting a later Jewish tradition about the tabernacle's original, ideal arrangement rather than the ark's specific contents at any one point in its centuries-long history; either reading is consistent with 1 Kings 8:9's specific, dated report of the ark's contents when it was inspected in Solomon's day.

### 13. Was the water-from-the-rock incident one event, or two -- and was Moses told to strike the rock, or speak to it?

**References:** Exodus 17:1-7; Numbers 20:1-13

**The claim:** Exodus 17:1-7, early in the wilderness wandering, has God instructing Moses to strike a rock at Horeb with his rod to bring forth water, which Moses does. Numbers 20:1-13, decades later near the end of the wilderness period, records a strikingly similar episode -- a thirsty people, a rock, water miraculously provided -- but this time God instructs Moses only to 'speak' to the rock (20:8), and Moses instead strikes it (twice, in apparent anger, 20:11), an act of disobedience for which Moses is barred from entering the promised land (20:12).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two accounts are almost universally read by conservative scholars as two distinct historical incidents, occurring roughly forty years apart at two different locations (Rephidim/Horeb in Exodus 17, near the start of the wilderness period; Kadesh/Meribah in Numbers 20, near its end) rather than a single event told twice -- the specific divine instruction differs precisely because the circumstances differ: God explicitly permits striking the rock in Exodus 17 but explicitly requires only speaking in Numbers 20, and Moses' culpability in Numbers 20 rests specifically on departing from the instruction actually given to him on that second occasion. Some interpreters additionally see typological significance in 1 Corinthians 10:4's description of a spiritual rock that 'followed' Israel, but this is a theological reflection on the pattern of provision across both incidents, not a claim that Exodus 17 and Numbers 20 describe the identical historical event.

### 14. Did God himself destroy and drive out the Canaanite nations, an angel, or Israel's own army?

**References:** Exodus 33:2-3; Exodus 23:27-30; Joshua 24:8, 11-12

**The claim:** Exodus 33:2 has God saying, 'I will send an angel before thee; and I will drive out the Canaanite,' crediting an angelic intermediary. Exodus 33:3, in the same speech, has God saying he himself will not go up 'in the midst of thee' because of Israel's stubbornness. Exodus 23:27-30 has God speaking of sending 'hornets' and gradually driving out the nations himself. Joshua 24:8, 11-12, reviewing the conquest afterward, credits God directly ('I delivered them into your hand... I sent the hornet before you'), while the historical narrative of Joshua's own military campaigns describes Israel's army doing the actual fighting and driving out.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These descriptions are read as complementary layers of a single, multi-agent process rather than competing claims about who alone acted: Scripture routinely credits God as the ultimate cause of a victory accomplished through ordinary or angelic secondary means (compare the frequent pattern of God 'giving' a battle into Israel's hand while Israel's army does the actual fighting, e.g. throughout Joshua and Judges), without treating the human or angelic agency as excluding or contradicting God's own decisive causation. In Exodus 33 specifically, the distinction between an accompanying angel (33:2) and God's own more direct, dangerous presence 'in the midst' (33:3, withheld here specifically because of the golden-calf crisis just narrated) reflects two different modes of divine involvement available to God, not a claim about who ultimately directs and empowers either one -- the chapter itself resolves this tension by verse 34:9-10 with God renewing his commitment to go with the people directly after Moses' intercession, showing the withdrawal in 33:3 was provisional, not final.

## Nature of God

### 15. Is God a warlike God, or a God of peace?

**References:** Exodus 15:3; Romans 15:33; 1 Corinthians 14:33

**The claim:** Exodus 15:3, in the Song of Moses after the Red Sea crossing, declares 'the LORD is a man of war: the LORD is his name.' Paul, by contrast, repeatedly calls God 'the God of peace' (Romans 15:33; 1 Corinthians 14:33), and Isaiah 9:6 anticipates a coming ruler titled 'the Prince of Peace.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Man of war' in Exodus 15:3 is a specific, celebratory description of God's decisive victory over Pharaoh's army moments earlier in the narrative -- a description of God's action in a particular historical deliverance, not a claim about his settled character or disposition in general, any more than calling a national leader 'a wartime commander' during an active conflict claims that war, rather than the nation's overall wellbeing, is his defining and permanent orientation. 'The God of peace,' by contrast, describes God's ultimate purposes and character toward those in covenant relationship with him. Scripture consistently holds both descriptions together without treating them as competing: the same God who acts decisively and forcefully against oppression and evil (as at the Red Sea) is also, and more fundamentally, oriented toward peace, reconciliation, and the wellbeing of his people.

### 16. Can God be tempted?

**References:** Exodus 17:2, 7; James 1:13

**The claim:** Exodus 17:2 and 17:7 have Moses rebuking the Israelites for 'tempting' the LORD by demanding water and doubting his presence among them. James 1:13 states plainly that 'God cannot be tempted with evil.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew and Greek terms translated 'tempt' in these two contexts carry different senses that most English translations preserve: at Exodus 17, the Israelites are testing or trying God's patience and provision through faithless demand -- putting God's willingness and ability to provide to an unwarranted test, a wrong done by the people toward God, not an attempt to entice God toward moral evil. James 1:13 is addressing a different question entirely: whether God can be enticed or solicited toward sin, which James denies of God's own moral nature. Being 'tested' or 'tried' by unbelieving demands (a testing God does not invite but which people can wrongly impose, as at Massah/Meribah) is a different category from being 'tempted' toward evil (an enticement God's nature makes impossible), and both English words can translate either sense depending on context -- the two passages are not describing the same kind of 'temptation.'

### 17. Is only God holy, or are ordinary people called holy too?

**References:** Exodus 22:31; 1 Samuel 2:2; Revelation 15:4

**The claim:** Exodus 22:31 has God telling Israel, 'ye shall be holy men unto me,' applying the term 'holy' directly to the people. 1 Samuel 2:2 and Revelation 15:4 both declare that 'there is none holy as the LORD' and that God 'only' is holy, in language some read as reserving the term exclusively for God.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Samuel 2:2's 'none holy as the LORD' and Revelation 15:4's language are best read as claims about the unique, underived, and supreme quality of God's holiness -- a holiness that is his own essential nature, the source and standard against which any other holiness is measured -- not a claim that the word 'holy' can never be applied, in a derivative or dependent sense, to people or objects God sets apart for himself. Scripture consistently uses 'holy' in this second, derivative sense throughout both testaments (holy priests, a holy nation, holy garments, a holy Sabbath, holy ground), always understood as holiness received or conferred by association with and consecration to God, not a competing, independent holiness rivaling God's own. Exodus 22:31's command that Israel 'be holy' is this same derivative, consecrated sense -- entirely compatible with 1 Samuel 2:2's claim that God alone possesses holiness in the primary, underived sense.

### 18. Does God ever grow weary, or tire?

**References:** Exodus 31:17; Isaiah 40:28

**The claim:** Exodus 31:17 states that God rested on the seventh day of creation 'and was refreshed' (Hebrew vayinnafash, related to the word for 'breath' or 'soul,' suggesting a renewal after exertion). Isaiah 40:28 states plainly that God 'fainteth not, neither is weary.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Exodus 31:17's language is widely recognized by commentators as anthropomorphic -- describing God's rest in terms drawn from ordinary human experience so that the Sabbath commandment's rationale (God's own rest as the pattern for Israel's, Exodus 20:11) would be vivid and relatable to its human audience, not a literal claim that the infinite, uncreated God experiences physical fatigue the way a human laborer does. The same descriptive strategy appears throughout the Old Testament, where God is spoken of as having hands, eyes, and emotions in ways theologians have long understood as accommodated language for creatures who have no other vocabulary for describing God's actions and relations to the world. Isaiah 40:28 makes the literal theological point directly and explicitly (God, as the everlasting Creator, does not actually tire), which the anthropomorphic language of Exodus 31:17 was never intended to contradict.

## Chronology and Population

### 19. Only a few generations from Jacob's family entering Egypt to an exodus of 600,000 men -- does the math work?

**References:** Genesis 46:8-27; Exodus 1:5; Exodus 6:16-20; Exodus 12:37; Exodus 12:40-41

**The claim:** The genealogy in Exodus 6:16-20 lists only four generations from Levi (who entered Egypt as an adult, per Genesis 46) to Moses: Levi, Kohath, Amram, Moses. Exodus 12:40-41 places the sojourn at 430 years, and Exodus 12:37 states about 600,000 men (plus women and children) left Egypt -- a population explosion some readers find hard to reconcile with so few generations and the 70 persons who originally went down (Genesis 46:27).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the genealogy, the 430-year figure, and the population figures exactly as given, without adjusting any of them in the text; the harmonization is a matter of demographic and genealogical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The most common harmonizations offered are: (1) genealogies of this kind, as elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible (compare the compressed genealogies of Ezra and the Gospels' own genealogical lists), frequently skip generations, listing only prominent ancestors rather than every biological link, so 'Amram' in Exodus 6 may represent a clan/family name spanning several actual generations rather than one man directly fathering Moses; (2) a documented, unusually rapid natural population growth over roughly four centuries (from 70 to several hundred thousand) is mathematically achievable given a large average family size across many centuries, a pattern demographers note is not implausible over 400+ years even without generational skipping, especially given the text's own emphasis that the Israelites 'multiplied greatly' as a central plot point (Exodus 1:7, 1:12, 1:20) under divine blessing; most conservative harmonists hold both factors likely combined.

### 20. Is taking a census sinful, given the atonement-money requirement here and David's later, judged census?

**References:** Exodus 30:11-16; 2 Samuel 24:1-15; 1 Chronicles 21:1-14

**The claim:** Exodus 30:11-16 has God directly commanding a census of Israel, with a required 'ransom' payment from each man counted specifically so that 'there be no plague among them' -- implying a census, properly conducted, carries a real risk requiring atonement, but is not itself forbidden. Centuries later, David's census in 2 Samuel 24 and 1 Chronicles 21 is explicitly described as sinful, provoking God's anger and a devastating plague on Israel as judgment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two texts are read together as consistent rather than contradictory: Exodus 30:11-16 itself already establishes that a census carries spiritual danger requiring atonement, which is precisely what David's census in 2 Samuel 24 lacked -- nothing in the text of 2 Samuel 24 indicates David collected the atonement money Exodus 30 required, and the plague that follows is widely read by commentators as the very consequence Exodus 30:12 warns a census could bring if conducted without the prescribed atonement. Beyond the missing atonement payment, most conservative commentators additionally identify David's underlying motive (1 Chronicles 21:1's account of Satan inciting David, and the broader narrative context suggesting pride in Israel's military strength as a substitute for trust in God) as the deeper sin being judged, rather than census-taking as such -- Exodus 30 and Numbers 1 and 26 both record God-commanded, non-judged censuses elsewhere, confirming that the practice itself was not inherently forbidden.

## Law and Ethics

### 21. Amram's marriage to his aunt Jochebed, given the incest laws later in the Torah

**References:** Exodus 6:20; Leviticus 18:12

**The claim:** Exodus 6:20 states that Amram, Moses' father, married Jochebed, described there as 'his father's sister' (his own paternal aunt). Leviticus 18:12, part of the Law given later at Sinai, explicitly prohibits exactly this relationship: 'You shall not uncover the nakedness of your father's sister; she is your father's relative.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Exodus 6:20's description of Jochebed as Amram's paternal aunt consistently, without softening the relationship; the harmonization concerns the timing of the Law relative to the patriarchal narratives, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization notes simply that the Levitical prohibitions were given after Amram and Jochebed's marriage, not before it -- the Law's incest provisions, like its provisions on multiple other matters (compare the patriarchs' polygamy and marriages to close relatives before the Law, such as Abraham's marriage to his half-sister Sarah in Genesis 20:12, a relationship Leviticus 18:9 also later prohibits), applied prospectively from Sinai forward and did not retroactively criminalize marriages already contracted under earlier, less restrictive customs. This is treated as a straightforward matter of legal timing rather than an internal conflict, since Exodus 6:20 nowhere claims the marriage was lawful under a legal code that, narratively, does not yet exist at that point in the story.

### 22. 'You shall not kill/murder' (Exodus 20:13) vs. the Levites killing 3,000 at Moses' command

**References:** Exodus 20:13; Exodus 32:25-28

**The claim:** The sixth commandment, given in Exodus 20, prohibits killing (in the KJV's wording) or murder (in most modern translations). Later in the very same book, after the golden calf incident, Moses commands the Levites to go through the camp killing those who had participated in the idolatry, resulting in about 3,000 deaths (Exodus 32:27-28) -- an action carried out at Moses' direct instruction, attributed to 'the word of Moses' and, in context, understood as divinely sanctioned covenant enforcement.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'You shall not commit murder.'
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'Do not murder.'
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'You shall not murder.'
- **KJV:** KJV renders Exodus 20:13 as 'Thou shalt not kill,' the broader English word most often cited in this alleged contradiction.
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'You shall not murder,' departing from the KJV's 'kill' at this verse.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'You shall not murder.'
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'You shall not murder,' tracking NASB.
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'You shall not murder.'
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'You must not murder.'
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders 'You shall not kill,' following the RSV tradition's more literal wording at this verse.

**Reconciliation:** As with the broader 'kill vs. murder' question addressed for Exodus 20:13 elsewhere in this project, the Hebrew verb in the commandment (ratsach) denotes unlawful killing specifically -- murder, unauthorized homicide -- and is a different word from the more general terms used for killing in judicial execution, warfare, or covenant-enforcement contexts, several of which the Torah elsewhere explicitly commands or permits under specified circumstances. The Levites' action at Sinai is presented in the text as a form of judicial execution for a capital covenant violation (idolatry, explicitly prohibited immediately prior in the same Ten Commandments, Exodus 20:3-4), carried out under the authority of Moses as covenant mediator -- a category the commandment's narrower Hebrew term was never understood by the text's own framework to prohibit.

### 23. How should 'strangers' living among Israel be treated, compared to the nations Israel was commanded to dispossess?

**References:** Exodus 22:21; Exodus 23:9; Exodus 23:23, 28; Deuteronomy 7:1-2

**The claim:** Exodus 22:21 and 23:9 command, 'thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him... for ye know the heart of a stranger,' extending clear protection and compassion to foreigners living among Israel. Exodus 23:23, 28 and the parallel conquest commands elsewhere (Deuteronomy 7:1-2) instead direct Israel to drive out and, in some cases, destroy the Canaanite nations occupying the promised land -- a sharply different posture toward a different set of non-Israelites.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew text itself distinguishes two different categories of non-Israelite, and the commands are addressed to each consistently: the 'stranger' or 'sojourner' (Hebrew ger) in the protective commands is a resident alien living peaceably within Israelite society, integrated into its economic and social life without the idolatrous, land-owning claims of the Canaanite nations; the Canaanite nations targeted in the conquest commands, by contrast, are addressed as an occupying, hostile political and religious system practicing what the text repeatedly characterizes as entrenched, severe moral and religious corruption (Leviticus 18:24-25; Deuteronomy 9:4-5; 20:18) which God is removing from a specific, promised territory at a specific moment in redemptive history -- a wartime and land-inheritance context nowhere applied to the ger living peaceably among Israel elsewhere. The two sets of commands govern two different relationships and contexts (peaceful cohabitation with individuals versus a specific, bounded military campaign against an occupying national and religious system) rather than offering two rules for treating the same category of person.

### 24. Did God command burnt offerings and sacrifices, or did he never command them?

**References:** Exodus 29:16, 18; Jeremiah 7:22-23

**The claim:** Exodus 29 gives detailed instructions for burnt offerings as part of the priestly ordination ceremony, explicitly commanded by God. Jeremiah 7:22-23, by contrast, has God saying through the prophet, 'I spake not unto your fathers, nor commanded them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices... but this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard conservative reading holds that Jeremiah 7:22-23 is a specific, rhetorically emphatic form of Hebrew comparison (a 'not-but' construction used elsewhere in the prophets, e.g. Hosea 6:6's 'I desired mercy, and not sacrifice,' likewise not a literal denial that God ever wanted sacrifice) that emphasizes the priority and primacy of covenant obedience over ritual performance, rather than a literal, historical denial that the sacrificial system was ever given -- Jeremiah's larger point, addressed to a generation offering hollow sacrifices while disregarding the moral core of the covenant, is that obedience from the heart was always the substance the sacrificial system was meant to express and was commanded prior to, and as more fundamental than, the sacrificial regulations themselves (which, in the actual Exodus narrative, follow rather than precede the more foundational call to covenant obedience given at Sinai, e.g. Exodus 19:5). Read this way, Jeremiah is not disputing that Exodus 29 and the sacrificial law were given, but insisting that they were never meant to function as a substitute for genuine obedience -- the same point Israel's own prophets and psalms make repeatedly (1 Samuel 15:22; Psalm 51:16-17).

### 25. Do the instructions for the tabernacle's daily offering match the later description of the same offering in Ezekiel?

**References:** Exodus 29:38-42; Numbers 28:1-8; Ezekiel 46:13-15

**The claim:** Exodus 29:38-42 and Numbers 28:1-8 both specify a daily burnt offering of two lambs (one morning, one evening), each with a specified measure of flour and oil. Ezekiel's temple vision, centuries later, describes a similar daily offering but specifies it as a single lamb each morning only (46:13), with somewhat different accompanying measures of flour and oil (46:14-15) than either Exodus or Numbers gives.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Ezekiel 40-48's temple vision is widely read by conservative interpreters as an idealized, symbolic, and in some respects future or eschatological picture rather than a literal blueprint intended to replace or correct the Mosaic tabernacle/temple regulations already given through Moses -- the book itself signals its visionary character throughout (40:2's 'in the visions of God'), and many of its architectural and ritual details differ in scale and specificity from any temple Israel ever actually built, consistent with a prophetic, symbolic genre rather than a literal replacement code. On this reading, differences between Ezekiel's daily-offering description and the Exodus/Numbers regulations reflect the distinct purpose and literary character of Ezekiel's vision, not a claimed correction or contradiction of the Mosaic law, which Ezekiel elsewhere consistently treats as authoritative (e.g., 43:18's offering regulations explicitly echo Mosaic sacrificial categories).

### 26. Is it acceptable to drink alcohol, given that wine was part of the tabernacle's prescribed drink offerings?

**References:** Exodus 29:40; Leviticus 10:9; Proverbs 20:1; Proverbs 23:31

**The claim:** Exodus 29:40 prescribes a 'drink offering' of a quarter hin of wine as a regular part of the tabernacle's daily sacrificial service, given by direct divine command. Elsewhere, Scripture repeatedly warns against alcohol's dangers (Proverbs 20:1; 23:31) and specifically forbids priests from drinking wine or strong drink before entering the tabernacle (Leviticus 10:9).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These texts are consistently read together as establishing a distinction between wine's liturgical, ceremonial use (poured out as an offering, not consumed as a beverage in that context, and used celebratively elsewhere in Israel's worship, e.g. Deuteronomy 14:26) and the separate question of personal alcohol consumption, on which Scripture consistently counsels moderation and warns against drunkenness and its dangers, rather than commanding total abstinence in every context. Leviticus 10:9's specific prohibition on priests drinking before entering the tabernacle is itself further evidence the texts agree: wine's presence as a poured-out offering is not treated anywhere in the text as license for those on active priestly duty to drink freely, since the concern in both texts is impaired judgment and irreverence in the sanctuary, not the mere existence of wine as such.

### 27. Is it acceptable to make and use perfume, given the restrictions on the tabernacle's sacred anointing oil and incense?

**References:** Exodus 30:22-38; Song of Solomon 1:3, 13; Esther 2:12

**The claim:** Exodus 30:22-38 gives a specific formula for the tabernacle's holy anointing oil and incense, explicitly forbidding anyone from replicating the exact formula for ordinary or personal use ('whosoever compoundeth any like it... shall even be cut off from his people,' 30:33, 38). Elsewhere, fragrant oils and perfumes are used freely and appreciatively in ordinary life (Song of Solomon 1:3, 13; Esther 2:12's months-long beauty treatments with 'oil of myrrh' and 'sweet odours').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Exodus 30's prohibition is narrow and specific -- it forbids reproducing the particular, holy formula reserved exclusively for tabernacle worship for common or personal use, treating that specific recipe as set apart the same way the tabernacle's furniture and garments were set apart -- not a general prohibition on fragrant oils, perfumes, or cosmetic use as such, which the text nowhere addresses and which other biblical texts (Song of Solomon, Esther, and the everyday anointing of guests with ordinary oil, e.g. Psalm 23:5; Luke 7:46) treat as an unremarkable, pleasant part of ordinary life. The concern in Exodus 30 is exclusively about protecting the unique sanctity of one specific, named formula reserved for sacred use, paralleling the similarly narrow restriction on the sacred incense in the same passage (30:37-38).

## Law and Text

### 28. Two 'sets' of the Ten Commandments?

**References:** Exodus 20:1-17; Exodus 34:11-26

**The claim:** Exodus 20 gives the commandments most familiar today; Exodus 34, following the golden-calf incident and the breaking of the first tablets, records a different list of stipulations (concerning festivals and ritual practice) that the text also associates with tablets God commands Moses to cut.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Exodus 34:28's reference to 'the ten commandments' as tied to the renewed tablets, consistent with the harmonization above.

**Reconciliation:** The standard scholarly reading holds that Exodus 34 records the renewal of the covenant tablets (replacing the broken ones from Exodus 32), inscribed with the same Exodus 20 commandments -- the text of Exodus 34:28 states 'the words of the covenant, the ten commandments' were written on these renewed tablets -- while the surrounding verses (34:11-26) are additional covenant stipulations restated or supplemented in this renewal context, not a claim that these particular verses themselves constitute a second, different 'ten commandments.'

### 29. Was the Passover lamb to be roasted, or boiled?

**References:** Exodus 12:9; Deuteronomy 16:7

**The claim:** Exodus 12:9 explicitly instructs that the Passover lamb must not be eaten raw or boiled in water, but roasted over fire. Deuteronomy 16:7 instructs, in some translations, that the offering should be 'boiled' (or 'cooked') and eaten.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'you shall cook and eat it,' the general term (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'you are to cook and eat it,' the general term (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'you shall cook it,' using the general term (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders Deuteronomy 16:7 as 'thou shalt roast and eat it,' actually agreeing with Exodus 12:9 rather than conflicting (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'you shall roast and eat it,' matching the KJV (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB (1977, 1995, and 2020 editions) renders 'you shall cook and eat it,' the general term (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'And you shall cook and eat it in the place which Yahweh your God chooses,' tracking NASB's wording (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'roast it,' matching the specific reading, and does not create a tension with Exodus 12:9 (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'roast the lamb,' matching the specific reading (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders this verse 'And you shall boil it and eat it at the place which the LORD your God will choose' (verified against the published RSV text); since the RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves the RSV text with minimal alteration, RSV2CE almost certainly retains 'boil' here as well, making it the one translation among the ten surveyed whose specific wording most directly raises the appearance of tension with Exodus 12:9's explicit prohibition on boiling the Passover lamb.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew verb in Deuteronomy 16:7 (bashal) is a general term for cooking that can include roasting, not exclusively boiling in water -- most modern translations render it with a general term like 'cook' precisely to avoid the false impression of a conflict with Exodus 12:9's specific prohibition on boiling. Additionally, Deuteronomy 16 addresses the broader festival offerings across the week (which included other sacrifices that could be boiled), not only the Passover lamb itself specifically.

### 30. Seven days of unleavened bread, or six?

**References:** Exodus 12:15, 19; Exodus 13:6-7; Deuteronomy 16:8

**The claim:** Exodus 12:15, 12:19, and 13:6-7 all specify seven days of eating unleavened bread during the Passover/Feast of Unleavened Bread. Deuteronomy 16:8, recounting the same festival, instead specifies 'six days thou shalt eat unleavened bread,' with the seventh day set aside as 'a solemn assembly' rather than counted among the unleavened-bread days.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** All ten translations render Deuteronomy 16:8 as 'six days' and the Exodus passages as 'seven days,' so this is a real internal Torah numbering question rather than a translation artifact. The standard harmonization holds that Deuteronomy 16:8 is not silently reducing the festival's total length but distinguishing the first six ordinary days of eating unleavened bread from the seventh day specifically, which -- per Leviticus 23:8 and Numbers 28:25, both of which agree with the seven-day total -- is marked out for a 'holy convocation' rather than ordinary activity; unleavened bread continued to be eaten on that seventh day as well; Deuteronomy 16:8 simply emphasizes the six working days against the one day of assembly, rather than asserting a different overall festival length than Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers all consistently give.

### 31. Who gave the law to Moses -- God directly, or angels acting as intermediaries?

**References:** Exodus 20:22; Galatians 3:19; Acts 7:53; Hebrews 2:2

**The claim:** Exodus 20:22 has God telling Moses, 'Ye have seen that I have talked with you from heaven,' describing a direct communication. Galatians 3:19 states the law 'was ordained by angels in the hand of a mediator,' Acts 7:53 has Stephen telling the Sanhedrin they 'received the law by the disposition of angels,' and Hebrews 2:2 refers to 'the word spoken by angels' -- all three New Testament texts crediting angels with a role in the law's giving that Exodus 20:22 does not mention.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These are widely read as complementary rather than competing descriptions of a single event involving multiple levels of agency: Exodus emphasizes that the law's ultimate source and authority was God himself, speaking audibly 'from heaven' at Sinai, while the three New Testament texts add a detail Exodus does not explicitly rule out or contradict -- that angels served as attending agents or heralds in the event's grand, theophanic administration (a pattern of angelic attendance at major divine appearances found elsewhere, e.g. Deuteronomy 33:2's 'he came with ten thousands of saints,' and Psalm 68:17's imagery of angelic chariots at Sinai). The New Testament writers' point in each case is rhetorical and theological -- contrasting the law's more indirect, mediated giving (through angels and Moses) with the gospel's more direct revelation through the Son (Hebrews 1:1-2; Galatians 3:19-20) -- not a claim that God was factually absent or uninvolved at Sinai, which Exodus 20:22 itself, alongside these same New Testament writers elsewhere, continues to affirm.

### 32. Who wrote the words on the second set of stone tablets -- God, or Moses?

**References:** Exodus 34:1, 27-28; Deuteronomy 10:1-4

**The claim:** Exodus 34:1 has God telling Moses, 'I will write upon these tables the words that were in the first tables,' seeming to specify God as the writer of the second set (matching the first set, which Exodus 32:16 explicitly says was 'the writing of God'). Exodus 34:27-28, a few verses later, has God telling Moses to 'write thou these words,' and then states 'he wrote upon the tables the words of the covenant' -- with the pronoun 'he' most naturally, in context, referring back to Moses, the subject of the preceding command. Deuteronomy 10:1-4, recalling the same event, states plainly, 'the LORD wrote on the tables... according to the first writing.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The passage is widely read, consistent with Deuteronomy 10:1-4's later, unambiguous statement that 'the LORD wrote,' as describing God as the actual writer of the Ten Commandments text on both sets of tablets (Exodus 34:1's explicit promise, fulfilled per Deuteronomy 10:4), while Exodus 34:27's separate command to Moses to 'write thou these words' is understood by most commentators to refer to a distinct act -- Moses' own writing of the surrounding covenant stipulations recounted in 34:11-26 (the 'words of the covenant' in a broader sense, recorded by Moses in what became part of the book of Exodus itself), not the ten commandments proper inscribed on the tablets. On this reading, 34:27-28's 'he wrote' need not refer to the tablets at all, and the tablets themselves -- per both Exodus 34:1 and Deuteronomy 10:4 -- were written by God on both occasions, consistent with 32:16's description of the first set.

### 33. 400 years, or 430 years, of sojourning?

**References:** Genesis 15:13; Exodus 12:40-41; Galatians 3:17; Acts 7:6

**The claim:** Genesis 15:13 and Acts 7:6 give 400 years; Exodus 12:40 and Galatians 3:17 give 430 years for the time in Egypt (or from the promise to the Law, in Galatians).

**How the ten translations render it:** Reproduces the Masoretic 430-year figure at Exodus 12:40 without altering the text; the Galatians 430-year count is unaffected by translation choice, since it reflects Paul's own historical framing.

**Reconciliation:** 400 is most naturally read as a round figure for the more precise 430; both are attested consistently across the relevant texts. Separately, the Samaritan Pentateuch and Septuagint text of Exodus 12:40 read the 430 years as spanning the whole patriarchal sojourn from Abraham's arrival in Canaan through the Egyptian period combined, rather than Egypt alone -- which is the reading Paul appears to follow in Galatians 3:17, since he counts the 430 years from the Abrahamic promise, not from Jacob's descent into Egypt.

### 34. Can God be seen by human beings, or not?

**References:** Exodus 33:11, 20; Exodus 24:9-11; Genesis 32:30; John 1:18; 1 Timothy 6:16

**The claim:** Exodus 33:20 states plainly, 'you cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live' -- yet only a few verses earlier, in the same chapter, 'the LORD used to speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend' (33:11), and shortly afterward, Exodus 24:9-11 narrates that Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders of Israel 'saw the God of Israel' and even 'ate and drank' in his presence without dying. Genesis 32:30 has Jacob declare, after wrestling with a divine figure, 'I have seen God face to face, and yet my life is preserved.' John 1:18 and 1 Timothy 6:16 add a New Testament layer to the same question, stating flatly that 'no one has ever seen God' and that God 'dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see.'

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders Exodus 33:20, 'there shall no man see me, and live,' against 33:11's 'the LORD spake unto Moses face to face, as a man speaketh unto his friend,' Exodus 24:10's 'they saw the God of Israel,' Genesis 32:30's 'I have seen God face to face,' and John 1:18's 'No man hath seen God at any time' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization distinguishes between seeing God's unmediated essence or full glory (what Exodus 33:20 denies is survivable) and various partial, mediated, or veiled manifestations of God's presence -- theophanies -- that Scripture consistently allows without contradiction. Exodus 33:18-23 itself supplies this distinction within the same conversation: God tells Moses he may see God's 'back' (a trailing, partial glimpse after God has passed by) but not his 'face' (his full glory, presented directly). The elders' vision in Exodus 24:9-11 is likewise typically read as a bounded, God-permitted glimpse of his feet and the pavement beneath them, not an unmediated encounter with the divine essence -- the text notably says 'he did not lay his hand on' them, framing their survival as a deliberate divine accommodation rather than evidence against 33:20's general rule. Jacob's 'face to face' language in Genesis 32:30, and similar idioms elsewhere (Exodus 33:11 itself; Deuteronomy 34:10; Judges 6:22), are widely read as describing a especially direct or intimate form of communication -- being addressed as an equal, without the mediation of dreams, visions, or third parties -- rather than a literal, unmediated sight of God's full being. John 1:18 and 1 Timothy 6:16 then generalize the same underlying principle for a New Testament audience, with John immediately supplying the resolution his own Gospel offers: the unseeable God 'has been made known' through the Son, who alone has seen the Father directly and reveals him to others without the seer himself viewing God's unmediated essence.

### 35. Sinai, or Horeb -- one mountain, or two?

**References:** Exodus 3:1; Exodus 19:1-2; Exodus 31:18; Deuteronomy 4:10; Deuteronomy 5:2; 1 Kings 19:8

**The claim:** Exodus 3:1 identifies the site of Moses' burning-bush encounter as 'Horeb, the mountain of God,' and this name recurs throughout Exodus, Deuteronomy, and 1 Kings (used of Elijah's later visit, 1 Kings 19:8). Yet the mountain where the Ten Commandments were given, described at length beginning in Exodus 19:1-2, is called 'Sinai' -- the name used far more frequently across Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers -- and Deuteronomy itself, in recounting the same law-giving event, consistently uses 'Horeb' instead (Deuteronomy 4:10, 5:2, 18:16). This raises the question of whether Sinai and Horeb are simply two names for a single location, or whether the text preserves a memory of two originally distinct places.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV uses 'Horeb' at Exodus 3:1 ('the mountain of God, even to Horeb') and 'Sinai' beginning at Exodus 19:1-2 ('the wilderness of Sinai... before the mount'), with Deuteronomy consistently using 'Horeb' for the same law-giving event (e.g., Deuteronomy 5:2, 'The LORD our God made a covenant with us in Horeb') (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** The overwhelming majority of interpreters, ancient and modern, treat Sinai and Horeb as two names for the same location rather than evidence of two different mountains or a genuine factual error -- a reading directly supported by 1 Kings 19:8, where Elijah's destination is called 'Horeb the mount of God' in an account that everywhere else assumes it to be the same site where the Law was originally given at Sinai. Among the proposals for why two names exist side by side: some scholars (following the documentary hypothesis) attribute 'Sinai' to one set of underlying source traditions and 'Horeb' to another, with both eventually preserved in the final combined text; others propose 'Horeb' named the wider mountainous region or mountain range, with 'Sinai' designating a specific peak within it (paralleling how a single range can contain multiple named summits); the sixteenth-century reformer John Calvin held that Sinai and Horeb named the eastern and western faces of the same mountain, respectively; and some note a possible wordplay in Exodus 33:6, where 'Horeb' may be chosen for its resemblance to the Hebrew word for 'sword' (cherev), following a bloody episode in that chapter. This project does not adjudicate among these specific proposals, noting only that none of them requires treating the two names as a factual contradiction, since the biblical text itself never distinguishes Sinai and Horeb as separate places one travels between, and 1 Kings 19:8 explicitly treats them as continuous with the same tradition centuries later.
