# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 Peter

This document catalogs 4 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 1 Peter, either
entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Ephesians, 1 Timothy,
Romans, and Titus documents, and 2 Peter and Jude. For each item, this document states the
claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages
involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable
the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entry:** Entry 1 addresses "the spirits in prison"
(3:18-20) and its closely related statement at 4:6 -- widely regarded as among the single most
difficult passages in the entire New Testament, touching on the intermediate state, the
possibility of post-mortem evangelization, and the identity of a mysterious imprisoned group.
This entry presents three major interpretive traditions in full detail, including specific
linguistic and contextual arguments on each side, without resolving the question.

**A note on candor:** Entry 1 is presented with an unusually extensive range of genuine
scholarly diversity of opinion, reflecting this passage's status as one of the most
substantively unresolved in the New Testament.

**Verification status:** Entry 1 was checked in detail against multiple sources representing
each of the three major interpretive traditions, including specific named scholars and
technical linguistic arguments on each side. The remaining entries are addressed by full
cross-reference to companion documents already established elsewhere in this series.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Interpretation

### 1. 'The spirits in prison' -- widely regarded as one of the single most difficult passages in the entire New Testament

**References:** 1 Peter 3:18-20; 1 Peter 4:6

**The claim:** 1 Peter 3:18-20 states that Christ, 'being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit... went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison, because they formerly did not obey, when God's patience waited in the days of Noah.' A related, similarly difficult statement follows at 4:6: 'the gospel was preached even to those who are dead, that though judged in the flesh the way people are, they might live in the spirit the way God does.' Together, these two passages have generated one of the most extensive and genuinely unresolved interpretive debates in the entire New Testament, touching on the nature of the intermediate state, the possibility of post-mortem evangelization, and the identity of a mysterious group of imprisoned 'spirits.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Peter 3:18-20 and 4:6 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most substantively and technically debated passages in the entire New Testament, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is among the most genuinely difficult passages addressed anywhere in this project, and this entry presents the three major interpretive traditions in detail rather than a single confident answer, since serious, technically competent scholars remain divided even after extensive study. The first major view holds that Christ, through the Holy Spirit, preached through Noah to Noah's own human contemporaries while they were still alive on earth before the flood. The 'spirits... in prison,' on this reading, are human beings who, though then still living and free, are now, from the later vantage point of Peter's own writing, confined in the 'prison' of divine judgment for having rejected that preaching. 4:6's 'those who are dead' refers to these same people, now deceased, for whom the gospel had already been proclaimed, through Noah, while they lived. The second major view, increasingly favored by contemporary scholars including Wayne Grudem and Thomas Schreiner, holds that the 'spirits' are not deceased human beings at all, but fallen angels. This is supported by several specific linguistic and contextual observations: the plural term 'spirits' is used elsewhere in the New Testament predominantly, and perhaps exclusively, for supernatural beings rather than deceased humans; the term 'prison' is elsewhere in Scripture (2 Peter 2:4, Jude 6, both addressed in their own companion documents) specifically associated with imprisoned fallen angels rather than deceased people; and the specific timeframe named, 'in the days of Noah,' connects naturally to Genesis 6:1-4's obscure account of 'the sons of God,' widely read elsewhere in this project's discussion of Genesis as a possible reference to disobedient angelic beings. On this reading, Christ's 'proclamation' after his resurrection was not an offer of salvation to these spirits, a possibility the broader New Testament, including Hebrews 2:16, is generally read as excluding for angelic beings, but a declaration of his own decisive, triumphant victory over them, sealing their judgment rather than offering them a second opportunity for repentance. Some scholars support this by noting Peter's own deliberate word choice at 3:19, a general term for 'proclaiming' or 'heralding,' distinct from the more specific term for 'preaching good news' he uses just one chapter later at 4:6 -- suggesting the two passages may address two related but distinct events or audiences rather than being strictly identical in scope. A third, minority view, associated at points with Martin Luther and, more explicitly, with some patristic sources such as Clement of Alexandria, holds Christ descended to the realm of the dead between his death and resurrection to offer the gospel, and a genuine opportunity for salvation, to those who had already died, including the disobedient of Noah's generation specifically. Most conservative commentators today reject this reading as difficult to reconcile with the New Testament's consistent emphasis elsewhere on faith exercised in this present life as the means of salvation, and with the passage's own specific focus on judgment rather than offered salvation. It nonetheless has genuine historical standing within the Christian interpretive tradition and, prior to the Reformation, connected to broader questions this project has addressed regarding purgatory and prayer for the dead, in the companion 2 Maccabees document. This project does not adjudicate between these three positions, noting that this remains one of the most substantively unresolved passages in the entire New Testament.

### 2. 'Baptism... now saves you' -- addressed at length in the companion Titus document, with a notable qualifying clause

**References:** 1 Peter 3:21; Titus 3:5

**The claim:** 1 Peter 3:21 states directly, 'baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you' -- connecting to the baptismal regeneration debate addressed at length in the companion Titus document regarding Titus 3:5's 'washing of regeneration.' This verse adds a notable qualifying clause not found in the Titus passage: baptism saves 'not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Peter 3:21 plainly and consistently as given, including its qualifying clause; see the companion Titus document for the fuller discussion of baptismal regeneration.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Titus document, which addresses the genuine, sincere denominational division between traditions holding baptism itself accomplishes regeneration and traditions holding baptism symbolizes or testifies to a regeneration accomplished through faith. This specific verse's own qualifying clause is noted by commentators on multiple sides of that debate as significant: those favoring a sacramental reading note that Peter's own explicit statement ('baptism... saves you') is more direct and less ambiguous than Titus 3:5's own more general 'washing' language, strengthening the case for baptism's own genuine instrumental role; those favoring a non-sacramental reading emphasize the qualifying clause itself, arguing Peter deliberately guards against a merely external, ritual understanding of baptism's saving significance (explicitly denying it is about 'removal of dirt from the body') in favor of an internal, conscience-directed appeal to God that the external ritual of baptism accompanies and expresses, locating the saving significance in the internal appeal to God rather than the external washing as such. This project does not adjudicate between these readings, consistent with its treatment of the same underlying question in the companion Titus document.

## Theological Tensions

### 3. Wives' submission and Sarah's example -- addressed at length in the companion Ephesians and 1 Timothy documents

**References:** 1 Peter 3:1-6; Ephesians 5:21-33; 1 Timothy 2:11-15

**The claim:** 1 Peter 3:1-6 instructs wives to be subject to their own husbands, citing Sarah's example of 'obeying Abraham, calling him lord' -- connecting to the broader egalitarian/complementarian debate regarding household relationships addressed at length in the companion Ephesians and 1 Timothy documents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Peter 3:1-6 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Ephesians and 1 Timothy documents for the fuller discussion of household relationships and gender roles.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Ephesians and 1 Timothy documents, which address the genuine, sincere theological division between egalitarian and complementarian readings of comparable New Testament household instructions, without adjudicating between them. This project takes the same approach to 1 Peter 3:1-6, noting its own distinctive contribution to the broader discussion: the passage's immediate context specifically addresses wives married to unbelieving husbands (3:1, 'even if some do not obey the word'), framing the wife's respectful conduct as a potential means of winning her husband to faith 'without a word' through her own observed manner of life -- a specific missional and relational purpose some commentators read as relevant context for understanding the passage's overall aim, alongside the broader questions addressed in the companion documents.

## Ethical Tensions

### 4. Submission to human authorities -- addressed at length in the companion Romans document

**References:** 1 Peter 2:13-17; Romans 13:1-7

**The claim:** 1 Peter 2:13-17 instructs believers to 'be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution... honor the emperor' -- closely paralleling Romans 13:1-7's teaching on submission to governing authorities, addressed at length in the companion Romans document.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Peter 2:13-17 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Romans document for the fuller discussion of submission to governing authorities.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Romans document, which addresses the standard resolution: such instructions address the general, ordinary case of civil submission to a legitimately functioning government, not an absolute, unqualified command overriding the specific instances of righteous civil disobedience found elsewhere in Scripture when a government's command directly conflicts with a clear, higher divine command. 1 Peter's own specific historical context -- written to believers facing real persecution under Roman authority, likely under or approaching Nero's reign -- is generally read as making this instruction's call to respectful, honorable conduct even toward a hostile government especially notable, modeling the same principle addressed at greater length in the companion Romans document.
