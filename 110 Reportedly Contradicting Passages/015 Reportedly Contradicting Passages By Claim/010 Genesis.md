# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Genesis

This document catalogs 57 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Genesis, either entirely internal to Genesis or between Genesis and a directly related
passage elsewhere (Luke, Acts). For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim (for example, Genesis 25:1's "wife," Genesis 6:2's "sons of God," and the two distinct
Hebrew terms behind Genesis 8:13-14's "dried up" and "dry"). Where no such check turned up a
meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten translations render the passage similarly
-- reflecting the fact that most of these claims turn on a disagreement between two biblical
books or passages rather than on how any single verse happens to be translated, not an
unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Creation

### 1. Order of creation: plants before or after humans?

**References:** Genesis 1:11-12, 26-27; Genesis 2:4-7

**The claim:** Genesis 1 has vegetation created on day three, before humans on day six. Genesis 2:5-7 reads, in a woodenly literal sequence, as though no plant of the field had yet sprung up when man was formed.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV's 'every plant of the field' and 'every herb of the field' uses the same field-specific phrasing.

**Reconciliation:** Most scholars read Genesis 1 and 2 as two complementary accounts with different purposes -- Genesis 1 a cosmic panorama, Genesis 2 a focused, non-chronological close-up on humanity's creation. Genesis 2:5 specifically mentions 'no shrub of the field' and 'no plant of the field,' terms many commentators tie to cultivated agricultural growth requiring both rain and a man to till the ground -- not the wild vegetation of Genesis 1:11-12. Read this way, the two texts describe different categories of plant life rather than contradicting each other.

### 2. Order of creation: animals before or after man?

**References:** Genesis 1:24-27; Genesis 2:7, 18-19

**The claim:** Genesis 1 places land animals on day six, before humans (also day six, but after). Genesis 2:18-19 seems to say God formed the animals *after* Adam, in response to Adam's need for a companion.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, NKJV, NLT, RSV2CE:** Uses simple past tense similar to the KJV/NASB pattern; the pluperfect reading remains grammatically available in Hebrew regardless of the English tense chosen.
- **CSB:** CSB similarly uses 'the LORD God formed out of the ground every wild animal,' with a footnote in many editions noting the pluperfect option.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'Now out of the ground the LORD God had formed every beast of the field and every bird of the heavens,' explicitly using the pluperfect, consistent with NIV's rendering and removing the sequence problem in translation.
- **KJV:** KJV's 'And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field' uses simple past, leaving the sequence more ambiguous in English than in some modern renderings.
- **NASB:** NASB reads 'out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field,' also simple past.
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'And out of the ground Yahweh God had formed every beast of the field and every bird of the sky,' explicitly using the pluperfect, consistent with NIV's and ESV's rendering and removing the sequence problem in translation.
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'Now the LORD God had formed out of the ground all the wild animals,' explicitly using the pluperfect, which removes the sequence problem in translation.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew verb form in Genesis 2:19 (wayyitser) is a simple past/converted-imperfect that can be rendered as pluperfect ('had formed') without doing violence to the grammar, which is exactly how several translations render it. Read this way, Genesis 2 is not asserting a different creation order but recounting that God brought previously-created animals to Adam to see what he would name them.

### 3. How long did creation take, given that Genesis 2:4 speaks of 'the day' the LORD God made the earth and the heavens?

**References:** Genesis 1:1-2:3; Genesis 2:4

**The claim:** Genesis 1 describes creation unfolding across six distinct days, each closed with the formula 'the evening and the morning were the [nth] day.' Genesis 2:4 then refers to 'the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens' -- using the singular 'day' (Hebrew yom) for the entire creative work just described across six days, which some read as evidence the author did not intend yom to mean a strict 24-hour period, or alternatively as an inconsistency in the number of days involved.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Hebrew yom, like its English counterpart 'day,' is regularly used both for a specific 24-hour period and, idiomatically, for a longer era or the whole span 'at the time when' something occurred -- the same flexibility appears in ordinary English ('in Lincoln's day') without implying the writer thinks a historical era is literally one day long. Genesis 2:4's use of yom to summarize 'the day' (i.e., the era, the time) when God made heaven and earth is standardly read as this common idiomatic usage, functioning as a heading or transitional summary phrase introducing the more detailed, focused narrative that follows in Genesis 2:5 onward, rather than as a claim about the literal duration of the creation week already narrated in Genesis 1 -- the two chapters are not offering competing day-counts for the same event.

### 4. When did God divide light from darkness -- on day one, before the sun, moon, and stars existed on day four?

**References:** Genesis 1:3-5; Genesis 1:14-19

**The claim:** Genesis 1:3-5 has God creating light and separating it from darkness on the first day. Genesis 1:14-19 places the creation of the sun, moon, and stars -- the created order's actual light sources -- on the fourth day, three days later, raising the question of what exactly was providing and separating the light on day one.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most interpreters read day one as establishing a light source distinct from the sun (compatible with an ancient Near Eastern text not concerned with modern astrophysics, and consistent with Revelation 21:23's picture of light without a sun), with day four assigning the sun, moon, and stars their specific function of marking 'seasons, days, and years' (1:14) for humanity's benefit rather than being the first introduction of light itself. Others read the text as a literary/topical structure (days 1-3 forming three realms -- light/dark, sky/sea, land -- and days 4-6 filling each with corresponding inhabitants -- sun/moon/stars, birds/fish, land creatures/humans) in which strict chronological sequencing was never the point. Either reading treats the two days as complementary parts of a single ordered account rather than as competing claims.

### 5. From what material were birds and land animals created -- water and ground in Genesis 1, or the ground alone in Genesis 2?

**References:** Genesis 1:20, 24; Genesis 2:19

**The claim:** Genesis 1:20 has birds and sea creatures brought forth from the waters, and 1:24 has land animals brought forth from the earth. Genesis 2:19 states that the LORD God formed 'every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air' out of the ground -- seemingly including birds among the ground-formed creatures, rather than water-formed.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 1:20's Hebrew is more naturally read as describing where the birds and sea creatures live and swarm rather than strictly their material origin (the same verse has the waters 'swarm' with sea creatures while birds fly 'above the earth,' suggesting habitat rather than material source is the point); 1:24's land animals from earth already agrees with 2:19. Read this way, Genesis 2:19 is not asserting a different origin for birds specifically but summarizing land and air creatures together as products of God's formative work 'from the ground,' consistent with 1:24, while 1:20's language about the waters describes where sea life and (by association) flying creatures were set loose to fill the sky and sea, not a rival account of birds' raw material.

### 6. Does Genesis 2:19-20's naming-of-the-animals scene place Eve's creation at a different point than Genesis 1's sequence implies?

**References:** Genesis 1:26-27; Genesis 2:18-23

**The claim:** Genesis 1:26-27 has God creating humankind, male and female, together in a single verse. Genesis 2:18-23 narrates Adam alone, naming the animals, finding no suitable companion among them, and only then having Eve formed from his side -- a sequence some read as placing Eve's creation well after Adam's, in tension with 1:27's simultaneous-sounding account.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the broader creation-order question addressed elsewhere in this document, Genesis 1 and 2 are read by most scholars as complementary rather than competing: Genesis 1:27 is a summary statement asserting that both sexes were part of God's created humanity, without specifying the internal sequence of their formation, while Genesis 2 supplies that missing sequence as a detailed, non-chronological close-up on day six specifically. The naming of the animals is best read as establishing Adam's unique standing and unmet need for a companion (2:20's explicit statement that 'there was not found an help meet for him'), not as asserting that an extended, undefined period of time separated Adam's and Eve's creation.

### 7. Does 'every tree' in Genesis 1:29 conflict with the single forbidden tree of Genesis 2:16-17?

**References:** Genesis 1:29; Genesis 2:16-17

**The claim:** Genesis 1:29 has God giving Adam and Eve 'every plant' and 'every tree' that bears fruit for food, with no exception stated. Genesis 2:16-17 then explicitly forbids eating from one specific tree, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 1 is a summary, panoramic account of the whole six-day creation addressed to humanity in general terms, while Genesis 2 supplies the additional, specific detail of the one exception given directly to Adam in Eden. A general grant ('every tree') followed by a specific, later-stated exception is an entirely ordinary literary pattern -- comparable to a parent telling a child 'you may eat anything in the kitchen' and later adding 'except the chocolate on the top shelf' -- rather than two competing rules for the same scope.

### 8. Were humans originally created to eat only plants, with meat permitted only after the Flood?

**References:** Genesis 1:29-30; Genesis 9:3

**The claim:** Genesis 1:29-30 grants humans and animals every green plant for food, with no mention of eating meat. Genesis 9:3, after the Flood, has God explicitly extending permission to eat 'every moving thing that liveth' in addition to plants, described as new ("even as the green herb have I given you all things").

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two texts are widely read as sequential grants rather than contradictory ones: Genesis 1 describes the original creation order, in which humanity's diet was plant-based, and Genesis 9:3 records a subsequent, explicit expansion of that diet after the Flood -- the text itself frames 9:3 as an addition ('even as I gave you the green plants, I now give you everything') rather than a claim that meat-eating had always been permitted. Whether the pre-Flood narrative's silence about meat-eating in practice (e.g., animal sacrifice, Abel's flocks) implies some early meat consumption occurred despite the stated dietary ideal is a secondary question on which interpreters differ, but it does not create a conflict between what Genesis 1:29-30 and Genesis 9:3 each explicitly state.

## Early Narrative

### 9. Where did Cain get his wife?

**References:** Genesis 4:16-17

**The claim:** Cain is exiled after killing Abel, and the text immediately mentions his wife, with no account of her origin, raising the question of who she was.

**How the ten translations render it:** All translations leave the same narrative gap, filled by Genesis 5:4's mention of additional unnamed offspring.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 5:4 states Adam and Eve had 'other sons and daughters' beyond the three named individually; the standard reading is that Cain married a sister or niece, a practice not yet prohibited (Levitical incest laws come many centuries later, at Sinai). This is a gap in narrative detail rather than an internal conflict.

### 10. If everyone alive descends from Adam and Eve, who was Cain afraid would kill him, and whose city did he build?

**References:** Genesis 3:20; Genesis 4:14-17

**The claim:** Genesis 3:20 identifies Eve as 'the mother of all living,' implying the entire human race descends from the first couple. Yet Cain, after killing Abel, fears that 'every one that findeth me shall slay me' (4:14) and later marries a wife and builds a city (4:17) -- both of which seem to presuppose a substantial population already existing beyond Adam, Eve, and their immediate children.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 5:4 states that Adam and Eve 'begat sons and daughters' beyond the three individually named (Cain, Abel, Seth), and Genesis records long patriarchal lifespans; by the time of the events in Genesis 4, a considerable population descended from Adam and Eve's other children (including intermarriage among siblings and cousins, not yet prohibited) could readily account for the people Cain feared and the wife he took, without requiring any humans outside the Adam-and-Eve lineage. Cain's city, on this reading, need not have been large -- the Hebrew term covers anything from a small fortified settlement to a city proper, consistent with a still-small but growing population descended entirely from the first couple.

### 11. Did God respect Abel's offering because it was an animal sacrifice, and does this mean God favors some worshippers over others?

**References:** Genesis 4:3-5

**The claim:** Genesis 4:4-5 has the LORD having 'respect' for Abel's offering of the firstlings of his flock, but not for Cain's offering of the fruit of the ground -- raising the question, for some readers, of favoritism given no stated reason for the difference in the text of Genesis itself.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** While Genesis 4 itself does not spell out the reason for God's differing response, Hebrews 11:4 explicitly supplies it: Abel offered 'by faith,' and 1 John 3:12 adds that Cain's works were evil while Abel's were righteous -- the distinction was a matter of the worshippers' inward disposition and faith, not an arbitrary preference for animal over plant offerings (grain and produce offerings were themselves a fully legitimate part of the later Mosaic sacrificial system, Leviticus 2). Genesis 4:6-7 also has God directly telling Cain that acceptance was available to him if he 'did well,' indicating the door was open and the differing outcome was not a matter of favoritism toward Abel as a person.

### 12. Does God desire animal sacrifice, given his acceptance of Abel's offering here alongside later texts that seem to downplay sacrifice's value?

**References:** Genesis 4:4; Psalms 51:16-17; Hosea 6:6

**The claim:** God's favorable regard for Abel's animal sacrifice (Genesis 4:4) is set by some readers against passages such as Psalm 51:16-17 ('thou desirest not sacrifice... the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit') and Hosea 6:6 ('I desired mercy, and not sacrifice'), which appear to state that God does not want sacrifices at all.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Psalm 51:16 and Hosea 6:6 with substantially the same sense ('you do not delight in sacrifice' / 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice'); the reconciliation below turns on context and canon-wide theology, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Psalms and prophetic texts are addressing sacrifice offered without genuine repentance or covenant faithfulness -- a formal ritual substituting for, rather than expressing, a transformed heart -- not rejecting the institution of sacrifice as such (the same Psalm 51 goes on in verse 19 to anticipate future 'sacrifices of righteousness' being pleasing to God once Jerusalem is restored). Abel's offering, by contrast, is commended precisely because it proceeded from faith (Hebrews 11:4). Read together, the consistent biblical principle is that the sacrifice's value was always understood to depend on the worshipper's heart, not that sacrifice itself was ever intrinsically unwanted apart from that condition.

### 13. What became of Cain -- did he die by violence as he feared, or as the mark implies he would not?

**References:** Genesis 4:11-16

**The claim:** Cain fears being killed by anyone who finds him (4:14), and God places a mark on him and declares sevenfold vengeance on anyone who kills him (4:15) -- yet Genesis never records how or when Cain himself eventually died, leaving some readers to ask whether the text's own internal logic (a protective mark, paired with an unresolved fear of violent death) is ever actually resolved.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 4:16-24 in fact answers the question the text itself raises: Cain settles in the land of Nod, marries, and builds a city and a lineage (Enoch, Irad, Mehujael, Methushael, Lamech), living out a full life under divine protection rather than suffering the violent death he initially feared. The passage does not record the specific circumstances of Cain's eventual natural death only because Genesis, like the rest of the Old Testament's genealogical material, generally does not narrate the deaths of secondary figures in detail; this is an argument from narrative silence, not an internal inconsistency, since nothing in the text describes Cain being killed in violation of God's protective decree.

### 14. Is polygamy morally acceptable, given that Genesis records Lamech taking two wives without explicit comment?

**References:** Genesis 4:19-24

**The claim:** Genesis 4:19 records Lamech, a descendant of Cain, taking two wives, Adah and Zillah, with no explicit condemnation in the surrounding text -- raising the question of whether the Bible approves of polygamy.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Lamech is introduced in a passage otherwise notable for his boastful, violent song (4:23-24, escalating far beyond God's own restrained sevenfold vengeance on Cain's behalf to seventy-sevenfold vengeance for a mere wound) -- the immediate context reads as an example of the moral deterioration of Cain's line, not an endorsement. More broadly, the standard position among conservative interpreters is that Genesis frequently narrates polygamous marriages (Abraham, Jacob, and others) descriptively, as historical fact, without thereby prescribing or approving the practice; the creation pattern in Genesis 2:24 ('the two shall become one flesh') is treated throughout Scripture, including by Jesus in Matthew 19:4-6, as the actual creation ideal against which these later departures are implicitly measured.

## The Flood

### 15. How many of each animal did Noah take onto the ark?

**References:** Genesis 6:19-20; Genesis 7:2-3, 15-16

**The claim:** Genesis 6:19-20 instructs Noah to bring two of every kind of animal. Genesis 7:2-3 then instructs seven pairs (or seven individuals, depending on translation) of every clean animal and bird, but only two of the unclean.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, NKJV, LSB, NIV, RSV2CE:** Follows the majority modern reading of 'seven pairs' (or is ambiguous like the KJV); either way the two-vs-seven language addresses different animal categories, not a contradiction.
- **ESV:** ESV reads 'seven pairs,' matching NASB's explicit reading.
- **KJV:** KJV's 'by sevens' (7:2) has sometimes been read as 7 pairs (14 individuals) versus other translations' 'seven each' (7 individuals); this is a translation nuance rather than a contradiction between chapters.
- **NASB:** NASB reads 'seven pairs' explicitly, resolving the ambiguity toward 14 individuals of each clean kind.
- **NLT:** NLT reads 'seven pairs,' consistent with the majority modern rendering.

**Reconciliation:** These are not competing accounts but sequential, more specific instructions: 6:19-20 states the general baseline (at least two of every kind, for survival of the species), and 7:2-3 adds a further requirement for clean animals and birds specifically -- extra numbers needed for sacrifice after the flood (Genesis 8:20) and for food. The two texts nest rather than conflict.

### 16. How long did the flood waters prevail?

**References:** Genesis 7:17; Genesis 7:24; Genesis 8:3-4

**The claim:** Genesis 7:17 says the flood was on the earth 40 days; 7:24 and 8:3 say the waters prevailed 150 days before beginning to recede, and the ark rested on Ararat in the seventh month.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this as a sequence of distinct time markers (40 days of rain, then 150 days total before recession begins), consistent with the phased reconciliation above.

**Reconciliation:** These describe different phases of a single continuous event: 40 days of rain and rising water (7:17), followed by a longer period (150 days total, inclusive of the 40) during which the waters remained at their high level before receding. The numbers describe successive stages, not competing totals for the same span.

### 17. Will God never curse the earth again, given his statement in Genesis 8:21 alongside the earlier curse of Genesis 3:17 and later judgments?

**References:** Genesis 3:17; Genesis 8:21

**The claim:** Genesis 8:21 has God saying, after the Flood, 'I will not again curse the ground any more for man's sake,' which some read against the earlier curse of the ground in Genesis 3:17 (following Adam's sin) and later biblical judgments involving the earth, as an apparent inconsistency.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 8:21's promise is specifically not to send another flood of that particular kind and scope 'for man's sake' as a response to human wickedness in general (elaborated in 8:22's promise of the regular cycle of seasons continuing), a promise later formalized as the Noahic covenant in 9:11-15 and marked by the rainbow. It is not framed as a claim that the ground-curse of Genesis 3:17 -- describing the toil and difficulty of agricultural labor as an ongoing condition -- would be lifted, nor does it preclude God's other, differently-framed acts of judgment recorded later in Scripture (such as against specific nations or cities). The 8:21 promise is narrow and specific (no repeat of a universal flood-judgment against humanity as such), not a blanket statement that the earth would never again experience any divine judgment of any kind.

### 18. Does 'sons of God' in Genesis 6:2 mean angels can have sex with humans, or is a supernatural reading of the phrase mistaken?

**References:** Genesis 6:1-4; Job 1:6; Job 38:7

**The claim:** Genesis 6:2 has 'the sons of God' seeing that the 'daughters of men' were fair and taking wives from among them, producing the Nephilim. Elsewhere, 'sons of God' (Job 1:6, 38:7) refers to angelic beings, raising the question of whether this passage depicts angels having sexual relations with human women, and if angels are understood as non-corporeal spirits (Matthew 22:30; Luke 20:35-36 -- 'neither marry, nor are given in marriage'), whether that is even coherent.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the phrase as 'sons of God' (a few modern study editions footnote alternative renderings, but the base text across all ten agrees); no translation choice among these ten resolves the underlying interpretive question.

**Reconciliation:** Three main interpretations exist among conservative scholars, and this is treated as a genuine, long-standing interpretive question rather than a resolved contradiction: (1) the Sethite view, that 'sons of God' refers to the godly line of Seth intermarrying with the ungodly line of Cain ('daughters of men'), avoiding any angel/human union; (2) the fallen-angel view, following the Septuagint, Second Temple Jewish texts (1 Enoch), and 2 Peter 2:4/Jude 6-7's apparent allusions to angels who 'kept not their first estate,' understanding these as angelic beings who took on some capacity for physical union, a unique and non-repeated act of rebellion rather than a claim that angels ordinarily marry; and (3) the divine-king view, that 'sons of God' designates powerful human rulers claiming divine status who took wives by force or coercion. Matthew 22:30's statement about angels 'in heaven' not marrying is understood by proponents of view (2) as describing angels' normal created state and heavenly existence, not necessarily foreclosing a unique act of rebellion described nowhere else as recurring. The passage is widely regarded as one of the most genuinely difficult and debated texts in Genesis, and this document does not adjudicate between the three views, but none of the three requires treating Genesis 6:2 as self-contradictory.

### 19. Does the commandment against killing conflict with God's own destruction of nearly all life in the Flood?

**References:** Genesis 6:7, 13, 17; Genesis 9:5-6; Exodus 20:13

**The claim:** Genesis records God resolving to destroy all flesh in the Flood (6:7, 13, 17) and, immediately afterward, establishing capital punishment for murder ('whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed,' 9:5-6) -- language some set against the later commandment 'Thou shalt not kill' (Exodus 20:13), asking whether the Bible is consistent on the taking of life.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard conservative distinction, addressed at greater length in this document's Exodus entry on the sixth commandment, is between unauthorized, personal killing (murder, the specific act the commandments and 9:5-6 both address and prohibit as between one human and another) and God's own prerogative, as the giver of life and righteous judge of the whole earth, to bring judgment on creation -- a category the text never treats as bound by the same command it gives to humans regarding each other. Genesis 9:5-6 in fact grounds the prohibition against murder specifically in humanity being made 'in the image of God' (9:6), the very same standing that here underwrites God's own authority to judge; the Flood and the sixth commandment are addressing different actors and different kinds of authority, not offering two rules for the same case.

### 20. Was there ever a truly just or righteous person before Noah, and is Noah's own righteousness absolute or relative?

**References:** Genesis 6:9; Genesis 7:1

**The claim:** Genesis 6:9 calls Noah 'a just man and perfect in his generations,' and 7:1 has God telling Noah 'thee have I seen righteous before me in this generation' -- language some read as claiming Noah was without sin, in tension with passages elsewhere stating no one is righteous (e.g., Ecclesiastes 7:20; Romans 3:10).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Perfect in his generations' (Hebrew tamim) is standardly understood as 'blameless' or 'having integrity' relative to the corrupt generation surrounding him, not as sinless perfection in an absolute sense -- the same word describes sacrificial animals as 'without blemish' and describes Job (Job 1:1) and Abraham (Genesis 17:1, where God commands him to 'be perfect') in the same relative sense, without any biblical writer treating those men as literally sinless. Genesis itself records Noah's later drunkenness and its consequences (9:20-21), confirming that 'righteous' and 'perfect' here describe faithful, relative uprightness amid widespread corruption, not the sinless perfection reserved in Scripture for Christ alone.

### 21. Is it accurate to call Noah a 'preacher of righteousness,' when Genesis never records him preaching?

**References:** Genesis 6:13-22; 2 Peter 2:5

**The claim:** 2 Peter 2:5 describes Noah as 'a preacher of righteousness,' but the Genesis account of Noah's 120 years of ark-building (implied by Genesis 6:3) contains no record of any sermon, message, or call to repentance that Noah delivered to his contemporaries.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is an argument from narrative silence rather than a contradiction: Genesis's flood narrative is compressed and selective, focused on the instructions God gave and the ark's construction, and does not claim to record every word Noah spoke to his generation over more than a century. 2 Peter, writing with the benefit of extra-biblical Jewish tradition and inspired apostolic insight, supplies information Genesis does not narrate in detail; the two texts are complementary rather than competing, comparable to how the Gospels routinely record actions and speeches of Jesus not mentioned in every parallel account.

### 22. Does the seven-day warning before the Flood (Genesis 7:4) fit with the flood beginning on the very day Noah entered the ark (Genesis 7:11-13)?

**References:** Genesis 7:1-4; Genesis 7:10-13

**The claim:** Genesis 7:4 has God telling Noah seven days in advance that the flood would come. Genesis 7:10-13 then describes Noah, his family, and the animals entering the ark on 'the selfsame day' the flood began, which some read as an inconsistency between an advance warning and same-day entry.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two texts are sequential rather than conflicting: God gives the seven-day advance notice in 7:4, Noah spends that week loading the ark with his family and the animals as instructed, and 7:10-13 describes the culmination of that process -- the loading being completed and the rain beginning -- on the seventh day. 'The selfsame day' in 7:13 marks the day the flood itself began, at the end of the seven-day preparation period the text already told the reader about, not a claim that Noah received no warning before that day.

### 23. Did every land creature outside the ark die in the Flood, and how does this square with post-Flood accounts of giants and large populations?

**References:** Genesis 7:21-23; Numbers 13:33

**The claim:** Genesis 7:21-23 states that 'all flesh died that moved upon the earth,' with only Noah and those in the ark remaining alive. Numbers 13:33, centuries later, describes the Israelite spies encountering the Nephilim ('giants') in Canaan -- the same term used for the pre-Flood beings of Genesis 6:4 -- raising a question about how such beings, or their name, reappear after a flood said to have destroyed 'all flesh.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard resolution is that 'Nephilim' in Numbers 13:33 is most likely descriptive language (comparing the unusually large stature of certain Canaanite clans, such as the sons of Anak, to the legendary pre-Flood Nephilim) rather than a claim of unbroken biological descent from the exact pre-Flood beings -- the spies' report is explicitly flagged in the text itself as an exaggerated, fear-driven account ('we were in our own sight as grasshoppers,' 13:33) rather than a sober ethnographic claim. This entry, together with the broader question of the Flood's geographic extent (global versus regional), is one on which conservative scholars hold differing views; either position is compatible with Genesis 7:21-23's claim that the judgment recorded there was total within its stated scope.

### 24. Had the earth fully dried after the Flood by the date given in Genesis 8:13, or only later, per 8:14?

**References:** Genesis 8:13-14

**The claim:** Genesis 8:13 states that by the first day of the first month of Noah's 601st year, 'the waters were dried up from off the earth,' and Noah saw that 'the face of the ground was dry.' Genesis 8:14 then states that the earth was not fully dry until the twenty-seventh day of the second month, nearly two months later -- seemingly two different dates for the same event.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations preserve two distinct English expressions across 8:13 and 8:14 (typically 'the surface of the ground was dry' at 8:13 versus 'the earth was dry' or 'completely dry' at 8:14), mirroring the two distinct Hebrew terms and supporting the two-stage reading given below.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew text itself uses two different words at these two points, generally rendered 'dried up' (8:13, Hebrew charebu, describing the surface no longer being covered by standing water) and 'dry' (8:14, Hebrew yabeshah, describing the ground as fully dried out and firm underfoot) -- a distinction most translations preserve in English. Read this way, 8:13 describes the water's visible recession while 8:14 describes the ground's return to a fully dry, solid condition suitable for the animals and people to disembark onto safely -- two stages of the same drying process rather than two conflicting dates for full dryness.

### 25. Is it acceptable to eat blood, given the explicit prohibition in Genesis 9:4?

**References:** Genesis 9:3-4; Leviticus 17:10-14; Acts 15:20, 29

**The claim:** Genesis 9:4 prohibits eating meat 'with the life thereof, which is the blood thereof,' establishing what appears to be a permanent dietary law given to all of Noah's descendants (i.e., all humanity). The prohibition is repeated in the Mosaic law (Leviticus 17:10-14) and again in the Jerusalem Council's instructions to Gentile believers (Acts 15:20, 29), yet many Christians today do not observe a blood-avoidance dietary practice, raising a question of consistency.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** There is no contradiction in the text of these three passages themselves -- all three consistently prohibit consuming blood, for closely related theological reasons (blood represents life, and in the Mosaic law specifically its use is reserved for atonement, Leviticus 17:11). The practical question of whether this prohibition remains binding on Christians today is a matter of ongoing theological and denominational discussion (some hold the Acts 15 instruction was a temporary accommodation for Jewish-Gentile fellowship in the first-century church, others that it remains a standing requirement), but that discussion concerns the prohibition's contemporary application, not any disagreement between what Genesis, Leviticus, and Acts each explicitly say -- all three agree completely on the underlying rule.

### 26. Did God promise never to destroy the earth by flood again, and how does this relate to 2 Peter 3's description of a future destruction by fire?

**References:** Genesis 9:11-15; 2 Peter 3:6-7

**The claim:** Genesis 9:11 has God promising 'neither shall all flesh be cut off any more by the waters of a flood; neither shall there any more be a flood to destroy the earth.' 2 Peter 3:6-7 speaks of the pre-Flood world being 'destroyed' by water, and describes a future judgment of the heavens and earth reserved for destruction by fire -- raising a question of whether a future 'destruction' of the earth is consistent with the Genesis 9 promise.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 9:11's promise is specifically and explicitly about a flood -- 'the waters of a flood' -- and is never claimed anywhere in Scripture to rule out judgment by an entirely different means. 2 Peter 3 explicitly draws this same distinction, contrasting the 'world that then was' destroyed by water with 'the heavens and the earth, which are now' being 'reserved unto fire' (3:6-7) -- the passage itself treats these as two different, non-competing modes of judgment rather than treating the coming judgment as a violation of the earlier flood-specific promise.

### 27. Does God's curse of Canaan for Ham's sin (and its application to Ham's descendants) mean people are punished for others' sins?

**References:** Genesis 9:20-27

**The claim:** After Ham 'saw the nakedness of his father' Noah, Noah curses not Ham directly but Ham's son Canaan, condemning him to servitude (9:25) -- language later read by some as extending guilt for one person's act onto an entire line of descendants, in tension with passages stating each person bears responsibility only for their own sin (Ezekiel 18:20; Deuteronomy 24:16).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The curse recorded here is Noah's own prophetic pronouncement as a wronged patriarch, not a direct judicial decree of God stated to be inherently fair or unfair on its own terms; Genesis records what Noah said, not that God commanded or endorsed collective punishment as a governing principle. Ezekiel 18 and Deuteronomy 24:16 establish the standing principle that individuals are judged for their own sin, and Canaan's own later, independently-recorded moral corruption (reflected in the conduct of Canaanite peoples throughout the Pentateuch, e.g. Leviticus 18:24-30) is what conservative commentators typically point to as the actual basis for the nation's later subjugation, with Noah's oracle read as prophetic insight into that future reality rather than an arbitrary transfer of guilt across generations.

## Genealogies and Chronology

### 28. The 'second Cainan' in Luke's genealogy

**References:** Luke 3:36; Genesis 11:12-13; 1 Chronicles 1:24

**The claim:** Luke's genealogy includes a 'Cainan' between Arphaxad and Shelah who does not appear in the Hebrew Masoretic Text of Genesis 11 or 1 Chronicles 1, raising a question about an extra generation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 3:36 with 'Cainan' present, following the Greek text of Luke as transmitted; none of the ten alter Genesis 11:13 to match, since all ten OT translations here follow the Masoretic Text rather than the LXX.

**Reconciliation:** This Cainan is present in the Greek Septuagint text of Genesis 11:13, which Luke (writing in Greek, for a largely Greek-reading audience) appears to be following. The 'discrepancy' is a difference between the Hebrew Masoretic tradition and the Greek Septuagint tradition -- already documented and expected -- rather than an error unique to Luke.

### 29. How old was Terah when Abram left Haran?

**References:** Genesis 11:26, 32; Acts 7:4

**The claim:** Genesis 11:26 states Terah was 70 when his sons (including Abram) were born, and 11:32 says Terah died at 205 in Haran. Acts 7:4 states Abram left Haran only after his father died -- but if Abram was born when Terah was 70 and left Haran at 75 (Gen 12:4), Terah would only be 145, sixty years before his stated death.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations preserve the same numbers; the harmonization rests on recognizing non-chronological name-order in Genesis 11:26, not on any translation difference.

**Reconciliation:** The standard resolution is that Genesis 11:26 lists Terah's three sons in order of prominence, not birth order -- Abram is named first because of his narrative importance, but was likely the youngest, born when Terah was around 130. That would make Terah roughly 205 (his recorded age at death) when Abram, then 75, departed Haran -- matching Acts 7:4 exactly.

### 30. Was Abram called by God while still in Ur, or only after settling in Haran?

**References:** Genesis 11:31; Genesis 12:1-4; Acts 7:2-4

**The claim:** Genesis's narrative sequence has Terah's family leave Ur and settle in Haran (11:31), with God's call to Abram to leave his country appearing afterward, in Haran (12:1). Stephen's speech in Acts 7:2-4 states God appeared to Abraham and called him 'before he lived in Haran,' while he was still in Mesopotamia (Ur).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages as given, without harmonizing the sequence in the text; the resolution is exegetical, drawing on Genesis 15:7 and Nehemiah 9:7 alongside Acts 7, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that God's call actually came to Abram while still in Ur (as Acts 7 states, and as Genesis 15:7 and Nehemiah 9:7 also imply, both explicitly tying God's call to 'Ur of the Chaldeans'), and that Genesis 11-12's narrative order is topical/summary rather than strictly chronological -- the family's move to Haran is recorded first as part of Terah's own initiative or an initial partial response to the call, with Genesis 12:1 restating and elaborating the call's content at the point where Abram (after Terah's death) resumes and completes the journey God had already initiated back in Ur.

### 31. Do the parallel genealogical lists for the sons of Noah and the descendants of Esau (Edom) agree with themselves and with 1 Chronicles?

**References:** Genesis 10:7, 23; Genesis 36:2, 10, 14, 20, 25, 40; 1 Chronicles 1:9, 17, 35-42, 51

**The claim:** Several individually minor genealogical details differ or appear ambiguous across parallel Table-of-Nations and Edomite king-list passages: the father of Sheba and Dedan is given as Raamah (10:7) in one Hamite line while a separate Sheba and Dedan appear descended from Abraham through Keturah (25:3); the father of Uz, Hul, and Gether is Aram in 10:23 but the names Uz and Hul also appear as sons of Nahor (22:21) and Shobal (36:23); Bashemath is called both a daughter of Ishmael and (in a differently-spelled instance, Basemath/Adah) a daughter of Elon the Hittite as one of Esau's wives (26:34; 28:9; 36:2-3); Anah appears as both 'son' and, in the same chapter, effectively identified among 'daughters,' and as both Zibeon's son and Seir's son (36:2, 20, 24-25); Korah is listed as both a son of Esau by Adah and, a few verses later, a chief descended from Eliphaz (36:14, 16); and Dishon appears as both a son of Seir and (spelled Dishan) a separate descendant in the same list (36:21, 25-26, 28); the place-name Timnah similarly appears both as a concubine of Eliphaz (36:12) and, feminine and identically spelled, as a Horite/Edomite chief's name (36:40).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 10 and 36, and the parallel material in 1 Chronicles 1, are widely recognized by conservative scholars as compiling multiple genuinely overlapping ancient genealogical and clan traditions -- some organized by direct descent, others by clan/territorial affiliation, tribal alliance, or intermarriage between originally distinct peoples (particularly between the Horites native to Seir and the incoming Edomites descended from Esau, who are explicitly said to have displaced and absorbed the Horites, Deuteronomy 2:12,22) -- with the same personal names recurring across different lines both because ancient Near Eastern naming pools were limited and reused across related clans, and because intermarriage between lines (as with Esau's Hittite, Ishmaelite, and Horite wives) naturally produced overlapping family trees rather than strictly separate ones. Ancient genealogies of this kind, both biblical and extra-biblical, regularly mix lineal descent with tribal, geographic, and political affiliation in ways that appear inconsistent to a modern reader expecting a single, strict biological family tree, but that were well understood by the text's original audience as normal genealogical convention; this document treats the individual name-overlaps above as a known feature of this material's compilation from multiple authentic ancient sources, rather than as errors, without claiming certainty about every individual relationship.

### 32. Was it Abraham or Jacob who purchased the burial plot at Shechem, and where exactly was each patriarch buried?

**References:** Genesis 23:16-20; Genesis 33:18-19; Genesis 50:12-13; Joshua 24:32; Acts 7:15-16

**The claim:** Genesis is explicit that Abraham purchased the cave of Machpelah near Hebron from Ephron the Hittite as a burial site (23:16-20), where Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Rebekah, Leah, and Jacob (50:12-13) were all buried; separately, Jacob purchased a parcel of land at Shechem from the sons of Hamor (33:18-19), where Joseph was later buried (Joshua 24:32). Stephen's speech in Acts 7:15-16, however, states that Jacob 'died... and was carried over into Sychem [Shechem], and laid in the sepulchre that Abraham bought for a sum of money of the sons of Emmor' -- appearing to combine the two separate transactions and burial sites into one, and to misattribute the Shechem purchase to Abraham rather than Jacob.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis's own record is internally consistent and unambiguous on both burial sites and both transactions; the difficulty lies entirely within Acts 7:15-16's compressed summary, which most conservative commentators take as a rhetorically condensed telescoping of two closely related historical facts (Abraham's purchase of Machpelah and Jacob's later purchase at Shechem, and the burials of Jacob at Machpelah and Joseph at Shechem) into a single sentence within a long, extemporaneous speech being summarized by Luke -- a recognized feature of ancient historical speech-summary, not a claim that Luke or Stephen intended to assert a different set of underlying facts than Genesis and Joshua record. Some commentators alternatively suggest Stephen (or Luke's Greek source) may be following a variant Jewish tradition, current at the time, that combined these two accounts; either explanation locates the compression in Acts's summary of the speech rather than in any conflict within Genesis's own narrative.

## Patriarchal Narrative

### 33. The 'wife-sister' episodes: one incident retold, or three separate incidents?

**References:** Genesis 12:10-20; Genesis 20:1-18; Genesis 26:1-11

**The claim:** Genesis records three closely similar episodes in which a patriarch (Abraham twice, then Isaac) claims his wife is his sister out of fear for his life before a foreign ruler, and the ruler discovers the deception -- leading some to argue these are actually one event told three different ways by different sources, rather than three distinct historical incidents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the three episodes as given, without merging or altering them in the text; the question is one of historical/literary interpretation, not translation.

**Reconciliation:** Conservative harmonizers generally read these as three genuinely distinct incidents, noting that Abraham's own words in Genesis 20:13 indicate this was a recurring practice of his ('whenever we came to a place, I would say of him, He is my brother'), which is consistent with three separate occurrences rather than requiring three tellings of one event; the differing rulers, locations, and specific details across the three accounts are also consistent with distinct incidents. Other scholars, working from a documentary-source model of the Pentateuch's composition, view the parallels as evidence of a single underlying tradition transmitted and adapted through different source strands -- a question of literary/source-critical interpretation on which serious, faithful readers differ, rather than a claim that the text asserts mutually exclusive facts.

### 34. Was Jacob renamed Israel once, or twice?

**References:** Genesis 32:28; Genesis 35:10

**The claim:** Genesis 32:28 records God (through the wrestling figure) renaming Jacob 'Israel.' Genesis 35:10 records God renaming him Israel again, in what reads as a separate, later scene.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both scenes as given, without harmonizing them explicitly in the text; the resolution is exegetical.

**Reconciliation:** The standard reading treats Genesis 35:10 as a formal confirmation and reaffirmation of the name change first given at Peniel (32:28) -- similar to how a covenant or promise is sometimes restated and solemnly reconfirmed at a later, significant moment (compare God's covenant with Abraham being restated on more than one occasion). The text of 35:10 itself refers back to the earlier naming ('your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel shall be your name'), read by most commentators as an explicit reaffirmation rather than an independent, competing first-time naming.

### 35. What is the human life span, given God's statement that it would be limited to 120 years, set against the much longer ages recorded afterward?

**References:** Genesis 6:3; Genesis 25:7; Genesis 35:28; Genesis 47:28

**The claim:** Genesis 6:3 has God declaring, before the Flood, that human days would be limited to 120 years. Yet Genesis itself subsequently records Abraham living to 175 (25:7), Isaac to 180 (35:28), and Jacob to 147 (47:28) -- all well beyond the stated 120-year limit, and all after the pronouncement in Genesis 6:3.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Genesis 6:3's figure as 120 years and the patriarchal ages (175, 180, 147, etc.) identically; no translation choice affects this entry.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 6:3 is widely read not as an immediate, universal cap on every individual human lifespan going forward, but either as (a) a 120-year period of grace before the Flood judgment fell (consistent with the verse's context, immediately following God's declaration that he would not 'strive with man' forever, i.e., a countdown to judgment rather than a biology statement), or (b) a longer-term trend describing the gradual, generational decline in human lifespans that Genesis's own genealogies document over subsequent centuries (from pre-Flood ages in the 900s down through the patriarchal ages in the 100s-200s, eventually approaching the roughly 70-80 year span described in Psalm 90:10). Either reading is fully compatible with individual patriarchs living well past 120 in the generations immediately following the statement, since neither reading treats it as an immediate, hard individual ceiling.

### 36. Was Abraham ever seen making theophanic appearances of the LORD the same way later Israel was told no one could see God and live?

**References:** Genesis 12:7; Genesis 18:1-2; Exodus 33:20

**The claim:** Genesis records the LORD 'appearing' to Abram on more than one occasion (12:7; 17:1; 18:1-2, where three visitors, one addressed as 'the LORD,' visit Abraham), language some set against Exodus 33:20's statement that 'there shall no man see me, and live.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length, with the fuller range of relevant texts, in the Exodus document in this series (see the entries on whether God can be seen face to face). In brief, the patriarchal narratives' theophanies are widely understood as visible but veiled manifestations -- appearances of God in a form perceptible to human senses (sometimes explicitly identified as the Angel of the LORD) without the patriarch beholding God's unmediated essence or glory, the specific thing Exodus 33:20 says is fatal to view. Genesis's language of God 'appearing' to Abram is consistent with this pattern and does not describe an unmediated vision of God's essential glory.

### 37. Was the city of Dan already named 'Dan' in Abram's day, centuries before the tribe of Dan conquered and renamed it in Judges?

**References:** Genesis 14:14; Judges 18:27-29

**The claim:** Genesis 14:14 has Abram pursuing his enemies 'unto Dan,' using a place name that, according to Judges 18:27-29, was only given to that city (formerly called Laish) after the tribe of Dan conquered it -- centuries after Abram's lifetime.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is widely regarded as a clear example of a later scribal or editorial updating of a place name for the benefit of the text's readers -- a common and non-controversial ancient practice in which a copyist or later editor substitutes a place's current, familiar name for an older one no longer in common use, so that the original audience can locate the place on their contemporary map (compare similarly updated place names elsewhere in the Pentateuch, and Genesis 36:31's explicit forward-looking reference to Israel's future kings). This is treated as evidence of the text's transmission history and later editorial updating, not as an error regarding when Abram lived or where he traveled -- the geographic location itself is unaffected by which name for it the text uses.

### 38. Did Israel ever actually possess the full extent of the land God promised to Abram, 'from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates'?

**References:** Genesis 15:18; 1 Kings 4:21; 1 Kings 4:24

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Kings 4:21 and 4:24 describe Solomon's kingdom as extending its influence and receiving tribute 'over all the kingdoms from the river [Euphrates] unto the land of the Philistines, and unto the border of Egypt,' which most conservative commentators identify as at least a substantial, if not exhaustive, fulfillment of the Genesis 15 promise during Solomon's reign, understood in terms of a sphere of dominion and tribute-paying vassal states rather than uniform, permanent territorial settlement of every square mile. Beyond this historical fulfillment under Solomon, many interpreters additionally hold that the promise's fullest and final fulfillment remains future, consistent with the broader biblical pattern of covenant promises given partial initial fulfillment with a fuller consummation still to come.

### 39. Is incest forbidden, given that Sarah was Abraham's half-sister?

**References:** Genesis 17:15-16; Genesis 20:12; Leviticus 18:9, 11

**The claim:** Genesis 20:12 has Abraham explaining that Sarah is 'the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my mother' -- his half-sister -- yet the Mosaic law later explicitly forbids marriage to a sister, including a half-sister sharing one parent (Leviticus 18:9, 11).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Levitical incest laws were given centuries after Abraham, as part of the Sinai covenant specifically binding on Israel going forward; nothing in Genesis states or implies that this prohibition existed or applied at the time Abraham and Sarah married. Genesis records, without either commending or condemning, several practices later restricted under the Mosaic law (polygamy and close-kin marriage among them), consistent with the standard conservative principle that God's law was progressively given over redemptive history rather than existing in full, binding form from the beginning -- Abraham is nowhere held to a standard not yet revealed to him.

### 40. Did Sarah have faith that she would conceive in her old age, given her laughter of disbelief in Genesis, set against Hebrews 11:11's praise of her faith?

**References:** Genesis 18:10-15; Hebrews 11:11

**The claim:** Genesis 18:12-15 has Sarah laughing to herself in disbelief at the promise she would bear a son in her old age, and then denying she laughed out of fear -- seemingly the opposite of faith. Hebrews 11:11, by contrast, commends Sarah by name as one who, through faith, 'received strength to conceive seed... because she judged him faithful who had promised.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The most common conservative harmonization holds that Sarah's initial reaction of incredulous laughter (Genesis 18) was a natural, momentary human response to an extraordinary promise, which does not preclude her subsequently exercising genuine faith by the time she actually conceived -- Hebrews 11 is commending the settled faith she came to hold and act on, not claiming she never doubted at any point in the process. (A minority of manuscripts and some translations render Hebrews 11:11 as referring to Abraham's faith enabling Sarah's conception rather than Sarah's own faith, which would remove the tension entirely for that reading, though the majority reading keeps Sarah as the subject.) Either way, an initial moment of doubt followed by a settled trust that God would keep his word is consistent, ordinary human experience, not a logical contradiction.

### 41. Can God do anything, given the rhetorical question 'Is any thing too hard for the LORD?' set against passages describing things God cannot do?

**References:** Genesis 18:14; Titus 1:2; Hebrews 6:18; 2 Timothy 2:13

**The claim:** Genesis 18:14 poses the rhetorical question 'Is any thing too hard for the LORD?', implying God's power is unlimited. Elsewhere, Scripture states God 'cannot lie' (Titus 1:2; Hebrews 6:18) and cannot deny himself (2 Timothy 2:13), which some read as items God is unable to do, in seeming tension with Genesis 18:14's implication of unlimited power.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The classical theological distinction, held across the conservative tradition, is between God's omnipotence (his unlimited power to do anything that is a proper object of power) and the logical impossibility of God acting against his own perfectly consistent moral nature -- lying, for instance, is not a further exercise of power that an even-more-powerful being could perform, but a moral inconsistency, an impossibility of a fundamentally different kind than the impossibilities in view in Genesis 18:14 (there, specifically, Sarah's advanced age preventing conception). God's inability to lie is understood as a function of his unchanging, perfect character, not a limitation on his power, and Genesis 18:14 is answered, not contradicted, by that distinction: nothing is 'too hard' for God to do that is consistent with who God necessarily is.

### 42. Who destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, and what does 'the LORD rained... from the LORD' imply about the nature of God?

**References:** Genesis 19:24

**The claim:** Genesis 19:24 reads, 'Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire from the LORD out of heaven' -- a repeated reference to 'the LORD' in a single sentence that has drawn attention (including in later Jewish and Christian theological discussion) as an unusual construction, with some questioning whether it implies two distinct divine persons or is simply a stylistic redundancy.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The repetition of the divine name in a single verse (a known Hebrew literary device called an epizeuxis-style emphatic construction, appearing elsewhere in the Old Testament for emphasis) is read by most conservative commentators simply as an emphatic way of stressing that this was unmistakably an act of the LORD himself, not a natural disaster nor the work of some lesser power -- a stylistic feature of Hebrew narrative rather than an internal contradiction. Some Christian theologians have additionally seen in the verse an early hint of the plurality within the unity of God more fully revealed in the New Testament (the LORD on earth interacting with Abraham in the preceding chapter, and the LORD in heaven), a theological inference some readers draw from the text rather than a claim the verse is grammatically inconsistent or contradictory in itself.

### 43. Is it acceptable to swear oaths, given Abraham's oath in Genesis 21:23-24 set against Jesus's instruction to 'swear not at all'?

**References:** Genesis 21:22-24; Matthew 5:34-37

**The claim:** Genesis 21:23-24 records Abraham willingly swearing an oath to Abimelech, apparently without any moral objection in the text. Jesus, in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:34-37), instructs his followers to 'swear not at all,' and instead let their 'yes' be 'yes' and their 'no,' 'no.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 5:34 as some form of 'do not swear at all' or 'do not take an oath at all'; none soften this into merely discouraging casual oaths, so the harmonization below rests on interpreting the target of Jesus's teaching, not on translation differences.

**Reconciliation:** The standard conservative reading of Matthew 5:34-37 holds that Jesus is correcting a Pharisaic practice of using elaborate, hair-splitting oath formulas (invoking heaven, earth, Jerusalem, or one's own head) as a way of implying that ordinary speech not formally sworn was less binding or could be broken -- Jesus's point is that a disciple's plain word should be so reliably truthful that no oath is needed to reinforce it, not that all solemn oath-taking as such (including before civil authorities) is sinful. Jesus himself answered under oath when the high priest formally adjured him (Matthew 26:63-64), and Paul invokes God as witness in ways functioning as oaths (2 Corinthians 1:23; Galatians 1:20), which most interpreters take as confirming that formal oath-taking in appropriate civil or covenantal contexts (as in Abraham's transaction with Abimelech) is not what Jesus's teaching condemns.

### 44. Who named the well of Beersheba -- Abraham, or his son Isaac's servants, a generation later?

**References:** Genesis 21:31; Genesis 26:32-33

**The claim:** Genesis 21:31 states that Abraham named the place Beersheba after making a covenant with Abimelech there. Genesis 26:32-33, in Isaac's generation, describes Isaac's servants finding water and Isaac naming the well Shebah, from which 'the city is called Beersheba unto this day' -- seemingly a second, independent naming.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that Abraham named the site (and its original well) Beersheba in his generation (21:31), but that the specific well is later filled in by the Philistines after Abraham's death (26:15,18) and the name lost or the site abandoned; Isaac's servants dig a new well at the same general location and Isaac renames it, consciously or not restoring essentially the same name his father had already given the place, with the text noting the name's durability ('unto this day') as of the final naming rather than claiming it was the very first time the location bore that name. This is comparable to a well or town being renamed or re-founded generations after in a way that revives, deliberately or coincidentally, an earlier name for the same site.

### 45. Was Laban the son of Nahor, or of Nahor's son Bethuel?

**References:** Genesis 22:20-23; Genesis 24:15, 29; Genesis 28:5; Genesis 29:5

**The claim:** Genesis 24:15 and 24:29 identify Bethuel as the son of Nahor and Rebekah's father, and Laban as Rebekah's brother (making Laban Bethuel's son and Nahor's grandson). Genesis 29:5, however, has Jacob's response identifying Laban as 'the son of Nahor' directly -- seemingly skipping a generation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Son of' in Hebrew genealogical usage regularly denotes any male descendant, not exclusively a direct first-generation offspring (the same flexible usage appears throughout Old Testament genealogies, e.g. Matthew 1:1 calling Jesus 'the son of David, the son of Abraham' across many intervening generations). Genesis 29:5's reference to Laban as 'son of Nahor' is standardly read as identifying Laban by his more prominent, well-known ancestor (his grandfather Nahor, patriarch of that branch of the family) rather than asserting a different, conflicting genealogy than the one given explicitly in Genesis 22 and 24, which consistently place Bethuel as the intervening generation.

### 46. Was Keturah Abraham's wife, or his concubine?

**References:** Genesis 25:1, 6; 1 Chronicles 1:32

**The claim:** Genesis 25:1 states that 'Abraham took another wife, whose name was Keturah.' 1 Chronicles 1:32, listing the same woman and her sons, calls her 'Abraham's concubine.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Genesis 25:1 as 'wife' (or 'another wife'/'had taken another wife'); none render it 'concubine' there. The tension is entirely between Genesis's and 1 Chronicles' wording, not between different English versions of the same verse.

**Reconciliation:** All ten translations in this survey render Genesis 25:1 as 'wife' (or an equivalent, such as 'another wife'), so the tension here is between two biblical books rather than between translations of the same verse. The standard harmonization notes that Genesis 25:6 itself, in the very same passage, refers to 'the sons of the concubines' Abraham had (plural), a group that -- alongside Hagar -- most naturally includes Keturah, since the chapter distinguishes these sons collectively from Isaac, the sole heir through Sarah. The Hebrew word rendered 'wife' in 25:1 ('ishshah) is the ordinary word for 'woman' or 'wife' and does not by itself specify full covenantal status equal to Sarah's; 1 Chronicles' later use of the more specific term 'concubine' (pilegesh) for the same woman is read as clarifying, rather than contradicting, her actual secondary standing in Abraham's household -- consistent with Genesis's own use of that same term for her in 25:6.

### 47. Was Luz renamed Bethel by Jacob once, at his first vision, or again decades later on his return?

**References:** Genesis 28:16-19; Genesis 35:1, 6-7, 15

**The claim:** Genesis 28:19 has Jacob naming the place Bethel ('house of God') immediately after his dream of the ladder, on his way to Haran. Genesis 35:6-7 and 35:15, decades later, on Jacob's return from Haran, again describe him arriving at 'Luz, that is, Bethel' and again naming the place (or an altar there) in connection with God's appearance to him.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization reads Genesis 35 as Jacob returning to and formally reaffirming the sacred site he had already named decades earlier -- building an altar and renewing his worship there in fulfillment of the vow he had made at the original naming (28:20-22, where Jacob had vowed that if God brought him back safely, 'this stone... shall be God's house') -- rather than an independent, competing first-time naming. The text of 35:15 itself is most naturally read as Jacob reaffirming the name already given the place in chapter 28 ('and he called the name of the place where God spake with him... Bethel'), consistent with the pattern already discussed elsewhere in this document regarding Jacob's name being reaffirmed at Bethel in the same chapter (see the entry on Jacob's renaming as Israel).

### 48. Is it acceptable to marry your wife's sister while she is still alive, given Jacob's simultaneous marriage to Leah and Rachel?

**References:** Genesis 29:21-30; Leviticus 18:18

**The claim:** Genesis 29 records Jacob marrying both Leah and her sister Rachel, with both wives living simultaneously for years, without explicit narrative condemnation. Leviticus 18:18, given centuries later at Sinai, explicitly forbids marrying a woman 'to her sister, to vex her,' while the first wife is still alive.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the parallel questions concerning Abraham's marriage to his half-sister and other patriarchal practices later restricted under the Mosaic law, the standard conservative position holds that the Levitical prohibition did not yet exist in Jacob's day and was not violated by an act that predates the law that would later forbid it; Genesis's narration of Jacob's marriage (itself the result of Laban's deception, not Jacob's original intent, 29:23-25) is descriptive rather than prescriptive, and the considerable domestic strife between Leah and Rachel that Genesis goes on to narrate in detail (30:1-24) is broadly read as the text's own implicit commentary on the difficulty and pain such an arrangement produces, consistent with the concern the later Levitical law explicitly addresses ('to vex her').

### 49. Is it always wrong to steal, given that Rachel's theft of her father's household idols goes unpunished and unremarked in the narrative?

**References:** Genesis 31:19, 30-35

**The claim:** Genesis 31:19 records Rachel stealing her father Laban's household idols (teraphim) as the family flees, and the ensuing narrative (31:30-35) has her successfully concealing the theft from her father's search, with no explicit condemnation of the act stated by the narrator.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Narrative silence about condemning a character's specific action is not the same as approval; Genesis frequently records the sins and failings of its major figures (Abraham's deception of Pharaoh and Abimelech, Jacob's own deceptions of Isaac and Esau) as straightforward historical narration, trusting the reader to bring the moral standard already established elsewhere in Scripture (against theft, and here specifically against idolatry) rather than appending an explicit editorial condemnation to every recorded wrongdoing. The episode is also widely read as furthering the narrative's broader theme of Jacob's household definitively leaving pagan household-idol practices behind (compare Jacob's later command in 35:2 to 'put away the strange gods' before going to Bethel), rather than as an endorsement of Rachel's theft.

### 50. Who did Jacob wrestle with at Peniel -- 'a man,' an angel, or God himself?

**References:** Genesis 32:24-30; Hosea 12:3-4

**The claim:** Genesis 32:24 describes Jacob wrestling with 'a man' through the night. By 32:28-30, the figure renames Jacob 'Israel' and Jacob concludes, 'I have seen God face to face,' naming the place Peniel. Hosea 12:3-4, recalling the same event centuries later, says Jacob 'had power over the angel' and 'wept, and made supplication unto him.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is broadly read as a single theophany -- an appearance of God in human, physical form (widely identified by conservative commentators with 'the Angel of the LORD,' a recurring figure elsewhere in Genesis and Exodus understood as a pre-incarnate manifestation of the Son, who is elsewhere both distinguished from and identified with the LORD, e.g. Genesis 16:7-13; Exodus 3:2-6) -- described from three complementary angles: physically, as encountered, Jacob wrestled with what appeared to be 'a man'; theologically, per Jacob's own concluding confession, he recognized he had been in God's own presence; and in Hosea's later poetic retelling, the same figure is called 'the angel,' the standard biblical designation for this kind of visible divine messenger-appearance. The three descriptions are read as different facets of one event, not three competing identifications.

## Law and Covenant

### 51. Was circumcision required of Gentile believers, or not?

**References:** Genesis 17:7-14; Acts 15:1-29; Galatians 5:2-6

**The claim:** The Abrahamic covenant explicitly requires circumcision as an everlasting sign, binding on Abraham's household and descendants. The Jerusalem Council in Acts 15 explicitly rules that Gentile believers are not required to be circumcised, and Paul in Galatians states that if Gentile believers accept circumcision as necessary for standing before God, Christ will be of no advantage to them.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each set of passages plainly as given; the harmonization is a matter of covenant-theological interpretation explicitly worked out within the New Testament text itself, not a matter of translation.

**Reconciliation:** This is generally treated not as a contradiction but as a redemptive-historical development explicitly addressed within the New Testament itself: circumcision functioned as the covenant sign for ethnic Israel under the Mosaic economy, while the Jerusalem Council and Paul both explicitly reason (Acts 15:8-11; Galatians 3) that with the coming of Christ and the giving of the Spirit to Gentile believers apart from circumcision, the sign given specifically to Abraham's covenant people is not imposed as a requirement for Gentile inclusion in the new covenant community -- a change in requirement the New Testament argues for and defends at length, rather than one it treats as self-contradictory.

## Joseph Narrative

### 52. How many years did the famine in Joseph's account last, and does the timeline of Jacob's family's move to Egypt fit?

**References:** Genesis 41:47-49; Genesis 45:6; Genesis 47:9

**The claim:** Some readers raise a timeline question over the seven years of plenty, the onset of famine, when Jacob's household relocated relative to those years, and Jacob's stated age (130) at the time he met Pharaoh, asking whether the years add up consistently across the narrative.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the relevant ages and time markers identically; no translation differences are involved in this case.

**Reconciliation:** Working through the text's own explicit markers (Joseph is 30 when he enters Pharaoh's service, Genesis 41:46; seven years of plenty follow; the famine begins in its second year when Jacob's family arrives, per Genesis 45:6; Joseph is around 39 at that point) produces a self-consistent internal chronology without requiring any harmonization beyond careful reading -- this entry is included as a caution that not every apparent 'chronology problem' raised in popular lists withstands a careful check of the text's own explicitly stated figures, several of which resolve immediately once laid out in sequence.

### 53. How many of Jacob's family went down to Egypt -- 70, or 75?

**References:** Genesis 46:27; Exodus 1:5; Genesis 47:27; Acts 7:14

**The claim:** Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5 (Masoretic Hebrew) total 70 persons of Jacob's household who came to Egypt. Stephen's speech in Acts 7:14 gives 75.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 70 at Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5, following the Masoretic Text, and 75 at Acts 7:14, following the Greek NT text Luke wrote in -- the same pattern across all ten, since this reflects two authentic ancient textual traditions rather than a translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Greek Septuagint text of Genesis 46:27 (and of Exodus 1:5 in some witnesses) reads 75, arrived at by including five additional descendants of Joseph (grandsons and great-grandsons born in Egypt) not counted in the Hebrew Masoretic total of 70. Stephen, addressing a Greek-speaking, Diaspora Jewish audience, follows the Septuagint figure his listeners would have known -- both totals are internally consistent within their respective textual traditions, reflecting a difference in which generation of descendants was included in the count, not a factual error.

### 54. Who sold Joseph into Egypt, and who bought him there -- Midianites, or Ishmaelites?

**References:** Genesis 37:25-28, 36; Genesis 39:1

**The claim:** Genesis 37:25-28 has Joseph's brothers selling him to a caravan of Ishmaelites, but the same short passage also mentions Midianite merchantmen drawing Joseph up out of the pit, and 37:36 states 'the Midianites sold him into Egypt'; Genesis 39:1 then says Potiphar bought him 'of the hands of the Ishmeelites.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Ishmaelite' and 'Midianite' are widely understood as overlapping or interchangeable terms in this narrative, reflecting the historical reality that Midianite traders (descendants of Abraham through Keturah, Genesis 25:2) regularly traveled in caravans alongside or were themselves loosely grouped under the broader term 'Ishmaelite' for Arabian caravan traders generally (compare Judges 8:24, which explicitly equates Midianites with 'Ishmaelites' as a description of the same people, 'for they were Ishmeelites'). Read this way, the same group of traders is called by both names across the passage, consistent with 37:28's own compressed telling (the brothers sell Joseph to the passing caravan, described by both names, who in turn sell him in Egypt), rather than describing two separate transactions or two rival, contradictory accounts of who was involved.

### 55. Where did Joseph's brothers find the money returned in their sacks -- at the lodging place on the journey home, or later when they arrived home and opened the sacks?

**References:** Genesis 42:27-28, 35; Genesis 43:21

**The claim:** Genesis 42:27 has one brother discovering his money in his sack at the lodging place on the way home from the first trip to Egypt. Genesis 42:35, however, describes all the brothers finding their individual bundles of money only when they empty their sacks at home in front of their father Jacob. Genesis 43:21 then has the brothers telling Joseph's steward that they discovered the money 'in the mouth of our sacks' when they 'opened our sacks' at their lodging place on the return journey.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The most straightforward harmonization holds that one brother discovered his own money first, at the lodging place on the road (42:27, an initial, partial discovery affecting just one sack), causing alarm; the remaining sacks were not fully unpacked and checked until the full company arrived home and unloaded everything before Jacob (42:35), at which point the rest of the money was found. Genesis 43:21's later summary to the Egyptian steward compresses this two-stage discovery (one sack found on the road, the rest found at home) into a single retrospective account focused on the outcome rather than the precise stop-by-stop sequence, which is an ordinary feature of summarized recollection rather than an assertion that all the money was found in only one of the two locations.

### 56. How old was Benjamin when Jacob's family relocated to Egypt, given that he is called 'a lad' shortly before the move yet already has ten grown sons by the time the family arrives?

**References:** Genesis 44:20; Genesis 46:21

**The claim:** Genesis 44:20 has Judah describing Benjamin to Joseph as 'a little one' and 'a lad' shortly before the family's relocation to Egypt. Genesis 46:21, in the list of those who came to Egypt, lists ten named sons already born to Benjamin.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Judah's description of Benjamin as 'a lad' (Hebrew na'ar, a term of relative youth and endearment that can describe anyone from an infant to a young adult man, and is used elsewhere in the Old Testament of men well into adulthood) is standard rhetorical language emphasizing Benjamin's status as Jacob's youngest, most cherished son and Judah's plea for sympathy on his father's behalf, not a precise statement of chronological childhood; Benjamin, as the youngest of Jacob's twelve sons, could readily have been in his late twenties or early thirties by this point in the narrative (a span of roughly two decades having elapsed since Joseph, roughly a decade older than Benjamin, was sold into Egypt at seventeen and is now around thirty-nine when the family relocates), old enough to have fathered ten sons of his own.

### 57. Do the different tribal lists in Genesis 49, Numbers, Deuteronomy 33, and Revelation 7 give the same twelve tribes of Israel?

**References:** Genesis 49:1-28; Numbers 1:5-15; Deuteronomy 33:1-29; Revelation 7:4-8

**The claim:** Jacob's blessing in Genesis 49 addresses each of his twelve named sons by name, but the various tribal lists elsewhere in Scripture (the wilderness census lists in Numbers, Moses's blessing in Deuteronomy 33, and John's list of the twelve tribes in Revelation 7) do not always list the same twelve names: Levi is typically omitted from land-allotment and census lists (assigned priestly duties instead), Joseph is sometimes replaced by his two sons Ephraim and Manasseh as separate tribes, and Revelation 7 omits Dan altogether, replacing him with Manasseh alongside a separately listed Joseph and Levi.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Genesis 49 lists all twelve sons of Jacob by name, establishing the base list; the tribal-list variations elsewhere are consistently explained by the specific purpose each list serves rather than by any disagreement over Jacob's actual sons. Levi is regularly omitted from land-allotment and troop-census lists because the Levites received cities rather than a tribal land allotment and were set apart for priestly and tabernacle service (Numbers 1:47-49; Deuteronomy 18:1-2) -- a substitution the text itself explains rather than conceals. Joseph's tribe is often split into Ephraim and Manasseh (Jacob's own grandsons, formally adopted as full tribal heirs in Genesis 48:5) specifically for land-allotment purposes, keeping the total number of land-receiving tribes at twelve despite Levi's exclusion. Revelation 7's specific list (omitting Dan, a departure some tie to Dan's prominent role in Israelite idolatry, Judges 18, and 1 Kings 12:29-30) is widely recognized by commentators as a distinctive, symbolic arrangement suited to its apocalyptic genre and twelve-times-twelve-thousand structure, not a claim about historical genealogy competing with Genesis 49's foundational list.
