# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Philippians

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Philippians,
either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Isaiah, Joel,
Romans, Ephesians, 2 Corinthians, and Luke documents, and Isaiah 45. For each item, this
document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically
renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects
how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly
reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entry:** Entry 1 addresses the "kenosis" debate --
one of the most substantively discussed Christological questions in the history of Christian
theology, concerning precisely what Christ "emptied himself" of in the incarnation. This
entry also documents a genuine translation difference: KJV and NKJV render the key clause
"made himself of no reputation" rather than "emptied himself," a wording some scholars argue
better reflects the passage's likely original emphasis on status and reputation.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1 and 6 are presented with genuine theological diversity of
opinion rather than a single forced resolution, consistent with this project's treatment of
comparably substantive doctrinal questions elsewhere in this series.

**Verification status:** Entry 1 was checked in detail against multiple sources representing
the full range of positions in the kenosis debate, including citation to specific historic and
contemporary theological arguments on each side. The remaining entries are addressed from
standard harmonization approaches, several of which build on treatments already established in
the companion Isaiah, Joel, Romans, Ephesians, 2 Corinthians, and Luke documents.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine theological diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Interpretation

### 1. 'He emptied himself' -- of what, exactly? The kenosis debate

**References:** Philippians 2:6-8

**The claim:** Philippians 2:6-7 describes Christ as one 'who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men' -- language (the Greek verb heauton ekenosen, from which the theological term 'kenosis' derives) that has generated a substantial and long-running theological debate, extending back at least 150 years, over precisely what Christ 'emptied himself' of in becoming human.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, LSB, NLT, RSV2CE:** Most modern translations render the underlying Greek verb kenoo with some form of 'emptied himself,' while KJV and NKJV render it 'made himself of no reputation' -- a genuine translation difference some scholars argue better captures the passage's likely emphasis on status and reputation rather than a precise, literal claim about ontological attributes being removed; NIV's 'made himself nothing' represents a further distinct rendering choice among the ten translations surveyed.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant,' the more literal rendering of the underlying Greek verb (kenoo) most modern translations follow.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant,' a translation emphasizing status and reputation rather than the more literal 'emptied himself' found in most other translations.
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'but made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant,' following the same KJV tradition at this specific clause.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'but emptied Himself, taking the form of a bond-servant.'
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'rather, he made himself nothing by taking the very nature of a servant,' another distinct rendering choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and actively debated theological question with real doctrinal stakes for how Christ's incarnation and divine-human nature are understood, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. The historic, mainstream Christian position, reflected in most classical commentary and defended by conservative theologians today, holds that Christ did not, and could not, empty himself of his divine nature or essential divine attributes, which would be a metaphysical impossibility for one who remains fully God. Rather, he 'emptied himself' in the sense of setting aside the visible display, exercise, and personal advantage of his divine glory, status, and prerogatives. This means voluntarily restricting the outward manifestation and independent exercise of divine attributes such as omniscience and omnipresence, a reading some connect to Matthew 24:36's statement that the Son did not know the day or hour of his own return, while never actually ceasing to possess his full divine nature -- comparable to a king temporarily setting aside the outward trappings and exercise of royal power while remaining fully and truly king. More radical 'kenotic' theories, which gained particular prominence among nineteenth-century theologians grappling with the philosophical and critical challenges of that era, propose Christ actually divested himself of certain divine attributes altogether. These commonly distinguish 'relative' attributes, such as omniscience and omnipotence, understood as given up, from 'essential' attributes, such as holiness and love, understood as retained. Most conservative theologians reject this position as compromising Christ's full and continuous deity during the incarnation, and as resting on an arbitrary and theologically unstable distinction between which divine attributes count as 'essential' versus 'relative.' A minority, more conservative reading holds that the entire phrase functions primarily as a vivid rhetorical expression of Christ's complete self-renunciation and humility -- the totality of the humiliation the passage goes on to describe -- rather than as a precise metaphysical claim requiring further theological specification of exactly what was 'emptied' at all. This reading has some support in the KJV's own alternate translation of this clause as Christ having 'made himself of no reputation,' a wording some argue better captures the passage's likely original emphasis on status and reputation rather than ontological attributes. This project does not adjudicate between these positions, noting that this remains one of the most substantively debated Christological questions in the history of Christian theology.

### 2. 'At the name of Jesus every knee should bow' -- Isaiah 45:23's language for YHWH, applied directly to Jesus

**References:** Philippians 2:9-11; Isaiah 45:22-23

**The claim:** Philippians 2:10-11 states that God has exalted Jesus 'so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord' -- language directly echoing Isaiah 45:23's own emphatic monotheistic declaration, spoken by YHWH himself: 'To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance,' occurring in the same chapter addressed at length in the companion Isaiah document regarding Isaiah's strict monotheism (45:5-6, 21).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Philippians 2:9-11 and Isaiah 45:22-23 plainly and consistently as given, preserving the close verbal echo between the two passages; this entry addresses a deliberate and theologically significant New Testament pattern of applying YHWH-language to Jesus, addressed further in the companion Isaiah and Joel documents, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as one of the clearest and most direct examples, alongside Joel 2:32's application to Jesus in Romans 10:13 (addressed in the companion Joel document), of the New Testament's deliberate pattern of applying Old Testament language specifically reserved for YHWH directly to Jesus. Paul's citation here is widely read by scholars as a deliberate, considered theological statement rather than an incidental echo -- taking language from one of the Old Testament's own most emphatic, exclusive monotheistic declarations (Isaiah 45's repeated insistence that 'there is no other god besides me,' addressed at length in the companion Isaiah document) and applying it directly and specifically to Jesus' own exaltation and universal lordship, immediately after describing Christ's pre-existent equality with God and his voluntary self-emptying (addressed in entry 1 above). This is generally read as a significant piece of the New Testament's own developing high Christology, affirming Jesus' full participation in the very divine identity and universal sovereignty Isaiah's own text reserves exclusively for YHWH, rather than positioning Jesus as some lesser or separate figure receiving homage properly due only to God -- the passage's own concluding clause, 'to the glory of God the Father' (2:11), is generally read as clarifying that this exaltation of Jesus does not compete with or diminish the Father's own glory, but is understood as its own expression and fulfillment.

### 3. 'Work out your own salvation... for it is God who works in you' -- human effort and divine sovereignty held together explicitly

**References:** Philippians 2:12-13

**The claim:** Philippians 2:12-13 instructs, 'work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure' -- a passage that explicitly and directly holds together, within the same two verses, both a call to active human effort ('work out your own salvation') and an affirmation of God's own prior, enabling, sovereign work ('it is God who works in you'), connecting to the broader question of divine sovereignty and human responsibility addressed at greater length in the companion Romans and Ephesians documents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Philippians 2:12-13 plainly and consistently as given; this passage is widely read as itself explicitly modeling the compatibility of divine sovereignty and human responsibility, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Unlike several of the more genuinely contested passages addressed elsewhere in this project regarding the relationship between divine sovereignty and human responsibility (Romans 9's election language, addressed in its own companion document), this specific passage is widely read across a broad range of theological traditions as itself directly and explicitly modeling the compatibility of these two truths rather than presenting a tension requiring resolution -- Paul's own grammar places the divine enabling ('it is God who works in you') as the stated ground and basis for, rather than in competition with, the called-for human effort ('work out your own salvation'), with the connecting word 'for' (Greek gar) explicitly presenting God's prior work as the reason believers can and should actively work out their own salvation, not a reason they need not bother. This passage is frequently cited across denominational lines as one of the New Testament's clearest single-passage statements holding human responsibility and divine sovereignty together as mutually reinforcing rather than competing truths, without requiring the kind of extended, multi-position harmonization this project has applied to less directly self-explanatory passages addressing the same broader theological question elsewhere.

### 4. 'I can do all things through him who strengthens me' -- a verse widely quoted apart from its actual context

**References:** Philippians 4:11-13

**The claim:** Philippians 4:13 -- 'I can do all things through him who strengthens me' -- is among the most widely quoted verses in popular contemporary Christian usage, frequently applied to achieving personal ambitions, athletic victories, career success, or overcoming any general life challenge. Read in its actual, immediate context, however, the verse specifically concludes Paul's own testimony about learning contentment in both scarcity and abundance: 'I have learned, in whatever situation I am, to be content... I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound... I can do all things through him who strengthens me' (4:11-13) -- a statement specifically about contentment and endurance amid difficult or fluctuating material circumstances, not a general promise of empowered success in any undertaking.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Philippians 4:11-13 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a matter of popular application diverging from the verse's actual literary context, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable and frequently discussed case, similar to a few other instances addressed elsewhere in this project (regarding popular but contextually mistaken applications of specific verses, such as Zechariah 13:6's 'wounds in your hands'), of a memorable phrase's popular application diverging substantially from its actual, original literary context. Most careful commentators read Philippians 4:13 as Paul's specific testimony that Christ's strength enables him to endure and remain content through the full range of his own circumstances -- whether want or plenty, difficulty or ease -- rather than as a general, unqualified promise that any goal or ambition a believer sets out to accomplish will succeed through invoking Christ's strength. This is not to say the verse has no broader application (the general principle that Christ's strength sustains believers through difficult circumstances has legitimate application beyond Paul's own specific situation), but its popular use as a promise of achievement or victory in essentially any undertaking, disconnected from its actual context of learned contentment amid both scarcity and abundance, represents a significant narrowing and shift of the verse's own original, more specific meaning.

### 5. 'To depart and be with Christ, for that is far better' -- addressed at length in the companion 2 Corinthians and Luke documents

**References:** Philippians 1:21-24; 2 Corinthians 5:1-10; Luke 23:43

**The claim:** Philippians 1:21-24 has Paul express his own desire 'to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better' than remaining in earthly life -- connecting to the intermediate-state discussion addressed at greater length in the companion 2 Corinthians and Luke documents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Philippians 1:21-24 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion 2 Corinthians and Luke documents for the fuller discussion of the intermediate state.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion 2 Corinthians document (regarding 2 Corinthians 5:1-10's similar language of being 'away from the body and at home with the Lord') and the companion Luke document (regarding Luke 23:43's 'today you will be with me in paradise'), both addressing the standard understanding of a believer's immediate, conscious fellowship with Christ beginning at death, prior to and distinct from the future, final bodily resurrection. Philippians 1:21-24 is generally read as further, consistent confirmation of the same understanding found in Paul's other letters.

## Theological Tensions

### 6. 'Not that I have already... become perfect' -- Paul's own admission, vs. later doctrines of Christian perfection

**References:** Philippians 3:12-14

**The claim:** Philippians 3:12 has Paul state plainly, 'Not that I have already obtained this or am already perfect, but I press on to make it my own, because Christ Jesus has made me his own' -- Paul's own direct admission that he had not yet achieved moral or spiritual perfection, even as a mature apostle late in his ministry. This raises a question in relation to later Christian theological traditions (most notably certain strands of Wesleyan-Holiness theology) that have taught a doctrine of 'entire sanctification' or 'Christian perfection' achievable in this present life.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Philippians 3:12-14 plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here concerns the passage's relationship to later denominational doctrinal development, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not primarily treated as a contradiction within Scripture itself, but as a question of how this passage relates to later denominational doctrinal development, and this project does not adjudicate the underlying theological question, which remains a matter of genuine difference between Christian traditions. Most commentators, including many within traditions that do affirm some doctrine of entire sanctification (such as historic Wesleyan theology), note that the specific term John Wesley and later Holiness theologians used ('Christian perfection' or 'entire sanctification') was carefully and specifically defined by its own proponents as a perfection of love and intention -- freedom from willful, deliberate sin and a wholehearted love for God and neighbor -- rather than a claim of the kind of absolute, comprehensive moral and intellectual perfection Paul's own statement in Philippians 3:12 explicitly disclaims for himself (freedom from all error, mistake, and infirmity, or the completed, final resurrection perfection Paul's own surrounding context, 3:20-21, explicitly reserves for the future, final resurrection of the body). On this reading, Paul's own statement and the Wesleyan doctrine may address somewhat different specific claims (comprehensive, final perfection versus a more narrowly defined perfection of love and intention), though this project notes that other Christian traditions reject any doctrine of entire sanctification achievable in this life at all, reading Paul's own personal testimony here as normative for all believers regardless of spiritual maturity, and holds no position on which reading is correct.
