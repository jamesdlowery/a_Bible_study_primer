# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Psalms

This document catalogs 25 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual
questions involving the book of Psalms, either entirely internal to the Psalter or in
comparison with Matthew, Romans, Acts, Hebrews, Deuteronomy, Ezekiel, and Genesis. For each
item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV**
specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording
materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the
standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entry 9 (Psalm 34's superscription naming "Abimelech") and entry 11
(Psalm 145's missing "nun" verse) are both presented with genuine scholarly uncertainty rather
than a single forced resolution -- serious, careful commentators are divided on both
questions, and this document says so plainly.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine uncertainty remains
among scholars, an honest account of what is and is not established. Readers are encouraged
to consult the primary texts directly.

## Ethical Tensions

### 1. 'Blessed shall he be who dashes your little ones against the rock' -- vs. loving one's enemies

**References:** Psalm 137:8-9; Matthew 5:44; Romans 12:14

**The claim:** Psalm 137, a lament over the Babylonian exile, closes with a blessing pronounced on whoever will repay Babylon by seizing and dashing her infants against the rocks (137:8-9) -- among the most viscerally violent lines in the Psalter. This sits in stark tension with the New Testament's explicit command to love one's enemies and bless, not curse, those who persecute (Matthew 5:44, Romans 12:14).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Psalm 137:8-9 plainly and without softening; the harmonization rests on distinguishing raw lament prayer entrusting vengeance to God from a command to personal violence, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read the imprecatory psalms, including this one, as raw, honest prayer poetry expressing the full weight of a specific historical trauma (the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem and, per some historical accounts, similar atrocities against Israelite and Judean children during ancient conquest, making the imagery a horrifyingly literal echo of what Babylon itself had done, per Psalm 137:8's own framing, 'you devastator... blessed shall he be who repays you'), placed before God in prayer rather than acted upon directly by the psalmist -- a crucial distinction from a command to commit such violence. Rather than instructing believers to personally carry out such acts, this and similar psalms model bringing even the darkest, most vengeful impulses honestly before God (rather than suppressing them or acting on them independently), entrusting ultimate justice and vengeance to God alone (a principle affirmed elsewhere in Scripture, Deuteronomy 32:35, Romans 12:19, 'vengeance is mine, says the Lord'), which is compatible with the New Testament's call to personal, active love and non-retaliation toward one's enemies -- the psalm expresses the anguish of the wronged rather than prescribing personal action, and Scripture elsewhere directly instructs against the individual believer taking vengeance into their own hands.

### 2. Should the righteous rejoice at the vengeance taken on the wicked?

**References:** Psalm 58:10; Proverbs 24:17; Matthew 5:44

**The claim:** Psalm 58:10 states, 'The righteous shall rejoice when he seeth the vengeance: he shall wash his feet in the blood of the wicked' -- language some read as commending personal satisfaction at an enemy's suffering, in apparent tension with Proverbs 24:17's instruction, 'Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth,' and Jesus's command to love one's enemies (Matthew 5:44).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated along the same lines as this document's entries on Psalm 137's imprecation against Babylon and Psalm 109's curses cited by Peter regarding Judas: the imprecatory psalms consistently express confidence in God's own future, righteous vindication of justice against entrenched, unrepentant wickedness -- a theological affirmation that evil will not go forever unanswered -- rather than instructing individual believers to personally savor or seek out an enemy's suffering as a matter of personal vindictiveness, which Proverbs 24:17 and Jesus's own teaching consistently condemn. The psalm's 'rejoicing' is best read as rejoicing that justice is ultimately real and will be done (a cause for confidence and praise regarding God's righteous governance of the world), not as license for personal glee at another's downfall or a model for how to treat one's own personal enemies, a distinction this document's other imprecatory-psalm entries develop at greater length.

## Literary Composition

### 3. Psalm 14 and Psalm 53: nearly identical psalms, with notable differences

**References:** Psalm 14:1-7; Psalm 53:1-6

**The claim:** Psalm 14 and Psalm 53 are, for the most part, the same psalm, sharing the identical opening line ('The fool says in his heart, There is no God') and following largely the same structure and content throughout. They differ, however, in several specific ways: Psalm 14 consistently uses the divine name 'the LORD' (YHWH) throughout, while Psalm 53 consistently uses the more generic 'God' (Elohim) at each corresponding point; and the two versions diverge more substantially in their final verse (14:5-6 differs noticeably in wording and content from 53:5).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both psalms exactly as given in the Hebrew, preserving both the divine-name difference and the differing final verses without harmonizing them; the resolution concerns recognizing intentional editorial reuse within the Psalter's own compositional history, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a well-documented and intentional case of the same psalm being adapted and reused for two different collections or liturgical purposes within the Psalter itself -- Psalms 42-83 form a recognized block sometimes called the 'Elohistic Psalter,' in which the divine name YHWH is systematically replaced with the more generic Elohim throughout, likely reflecting either a different editorial hand's preference or a distinct liturgical tradition being incorporated into this section of the collection; Psalm 53, falling within this block, has been adapted to match its surrounding style, while Psalm 14, outside the block, preserves the original divine name. The differing final verses are generally read as reflecting the same underlying psalm's flexible reuse and light adaptation across two separate liturgical or historical contexts (Psalm 53's ending appears to reference a specific deliverance not present in Psalm 14's version) rather than as two competing, contradictory accounts of the same composition.

## Theological Tensions

### 4. 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?' -- genuine abandonment, or something else?

**References:** Psalm 22:1; Matthew 27:46; Psalm 22:22-31

**The claim:** Psalm 22 opens with a cry of profound abandonment: 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?' -- words Jesus himself quotes verbatim from the cross (Matthew 27:46), raising the question of whether this represents a genuine rupture between the Father and the Son, in apparent tension with other scriptural affirmations that God never forsakes his own (Deuteronomy 31:6, Hebrews 13:5) or, more specifically for Christ, the unity of the Trinity.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Psalm 22:1 and its later triumphant turn (22:22-31) plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading the full psalm's trajectory rather than its opening verse in isolation, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that Psalm 22 itself does not end where it begins: after an extended, anguished lament (verses 1-21), the psalm pivots dramatically at verse 22 into confident praise, declaring that God 'has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted... he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him' (22:24) -- a psalm that moves from the felt experience of abandonment to the theological affirmation that abandonment was not, in fact, ultimate or final. Most conservative theologians reading Jesus' quotation of the opening verse understand it as invoking the entire psalm (a common practice in Jewish citation convention, where quoting a psalm's first line evoked its whole content and trajectory for an audience that knew it well), meaning Jesus' cry expresses the full, genuine anguish of bearing the judgment for sin (a real and terrible experience, whatever its precise theological nature) while simultaneously pointing, through the very psalm he chose to quote, toward the vindication and triumph the psalm's own ending anticipates -- a reading some see confirmed by the psalm's own uncannily specific details (the mocking bystanders, 22:7-8; the piercing of hands and feet, 22:16; the casting of lots for garments, 22:18) matching the crucifixion narrative closely enough that the Gospel writers clearly intended readers to hear the whole psalm, not merely its opening cry of despair.

### 5. 'In sin did my mother conceive me' -- original sin, or in tension with individual responsibility?

**References:** Psalm 51:5; Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20

**The claim:** Psalm 51:5, part of David's confession following his sin with Bathsheba, states, 'in sin did my mother conceive me' (or, in some translations, 'I was brought forth in iniquity') -- language widely cited as a key Old Testament basis for the doctrine of original sin or inherited sinfulness from conception. This raises a question in light of texts affirming individual moral responsibility and denying that guilt is inherited or transferred between generations (Deuteronomy 24:16, Ezekiel 18:20, both addressed at greater length elsewhere in this project).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Psalm 51:5 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing universal sinful condition from transferred legal guilt for another's specific act, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish, as with the earlier-addressed tension between generational consequence and personal guilt (see this project's treatment of Exodus 20:5 and Ezekiel 18), between a claim about inherited legal guilt for someone else's specific sin (which Deuteronomy 24:16 and Ezekiel 18:20 deny) and a claim about the universal, pervasive condition of human sinfulness present from the very beginning of an individual's own existence, which most theological traditions read Psalm 51:5 as describing -- not that David's mother personally sinned in conceiving him (the verse says nothing accusatory about David's parents), but that David himself, in a moment of profound personal repentance, recognizes his own sinful nature and inclination as having been present as far back as he can trace his own existence, intensifying rather than deflecting his sense of personal responsibility for his specific sin with Bathsheba. This is generally read as a statement about the universal human condition each person is personally responsible for and stands guilty of before God as their own sin (a doctrine the New Testament develops at length, e.g., Romans 5:12), not a claim that any specific individual bears legal guilt for another person's specific transgression.

### 6. 'Who shall dwell on your holy hill?' -- entry by merit, or compatible with grace?

**References:** Psalm 15:1-5; Psalm 24:3-4; Psalm 51:1-2, 17

**The claim:** Psalm 15 and Psalm 24 both pose the question of who may approach God's presence or dwell in his sanctuary, and both answer with a list of moral qualifications: 'he who walks blamelessly and does what is right... who does not slander... who swears to his own hurt' (15:2-4); 'he who has clean hands and a pure heart' (24:4) -- language that reads as establishing personal righteousness as the entry requirement for approaching God. This appears to be in some tension with the Psalter's equally prominent emphasis on grace and forgiveness for the genuinely repentant sinner, most notably in Psalm 51's own plea for mercy despite serious sin (51:1-2) and its explicit statement that 'a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise' (51:17).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Psalms 15, 24, and 51 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing a description of covenant faithfulness's practical shape from a legalistic entrance requirement, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Psalms 15 and 24 not as establishing a legalistic, merit-based entrance requirement into a relationship with God (which the Psalter's own broader theology of grace, repentance, and forgiveness would directly contradict), but as describing the character and manner of life that genuinely and habitually characterizes someone who already stands in right relationship with God, functioning similarly to New Testament passages describing the fruit or evidence of genuine faith (compare James 2:14-26, addressed at greater length elsewhere in this project regarding the faith/works question) rather than the precondition for acceptance in the first place. On this reading, these psalms describe the practical, observable shape a life of covenant faithfulness takes -- not sinless perfection, but a general, habitual orientation toward integrity and away from active, unrepentant wrongdoing -- which is fully compatible with Psalm 51's own theology, since David's plea there is precisely for the restoration of exactly this kind of clean-handed integrity after a specific, serious lapse, not a claim that grace makes such moral character irrelevant or unnecessary going forward.

### 7. Is foolishness ever good, given that the 'foolishness of God' is called wiser than human wisdom?

**References:** Psalm 5:5; 1 Corinthians 1:18-25; 1 Corinthians 3:18

**The claim:** Psalm 5:5 states that God 'hatest all workers of iniquity,' with the surrounding psalm treating 'the foolish' as those under God's judgment -- foolishness is condemned. Paul, however, describes the gospel message as 'foolishness' that is nevertheless wiser than human wisdom (1 Corinthians 1:18-25), and tells believers, 'let him become a fool, that he may be wise' (1 Corinthians 3:18).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two texts use 'fool'/'foolish' in entirely different senses that the surrounding context of each makes plain: Psalm 5's 'foolish' describes moral and spiritual folly -- willful rebellion against God and pursuit of iniquity, the opposite of the wisdom that begins with 'the fear of the LORD' (Proverbs 1:7) -- a condition Scripture consistently condemns. Paul's 'foolishness,' by contrast, is deliberately ironic: he describes how the gospel of a crucified Messiah appears foolish only from the vantage point of merely human, worldly wisdom and status-seeking (1 Corinthians 1:20, 'hath not God made foolish the wisdom of this world?'), while being in fact the true wisdom and power of God; 'becoming a fool' in 3:18 means abandoning worldly wisdom's false standards of status and cleverness, not embracing moral or spiritual folly. The two texts are not describing the same kind of 'foolishness' at all.

### 8. Where does God dwell -- in Zion specifically, or is he not localized to any one place?

**References:** Psalm 9:11

**The claim:** Psalm 9:11 calls on worshippers to 'sing praises to the LORD, which dwelleth in Zion,' language that could be read as localizing God's presence to one specific place.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is addressed at greater length in this project's 1 Kings companion document (see its entry on whether God dwells in temples built by human hands, discussing Solomon's own prayer at the Temple's dedication). In brief, 'dwelleth in Zion' describes God's special, covenantal manifestation of presence and the focus of Israel's corporate worship at a particular sanctuary -- the sense in which Zion was genuinely 'his dwelling' for the purposes of Israel's cultic and covenantal life -- not a claim that God's infinite being is confined there, a point Solomon's own dedicatory prayer for that same sanctuary explicitly and immediately clarifies (1 Kings 8:27, 'the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee').

### 9. Are God's words always pure and true, given that Scripture elsewhere describes God sending a 'lying spirit'?

**References:** Psalm 12:6; 1 Kings 22:22-23; Ezekiel 14:9

**The claim:** Psalm 12:6 declares, 'The words of the LORD are pure words: as silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times.' Yet 1 Kings 22:22-23 has God permitting a 'lying spirit' to go out and deceive Ahab's prophets, and Ezekiel 14:9 states that if a prophet is deceived and speaks falsely, 'I the LORD have deceived that prophet.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two texts address different questions. Psalm 12:6 describes the intrinsic purity, reliability, and truthfulness of God's own direct revelation and promises: what God himself says is always wholly true and trustworthy, without corruption or falsehood. 1 Kings 22 and Ezekiel 14 both describe a specific, judicial act of God permitting or bringing about deception as a judgment on those who have already rejected the truth and sought false counsel or false prophecy -- Ahab explicitly disliked the true prophet Micaiah and preferred flattering falsehood (1 Kings 22:8), and Ezekiel 14's context concerns idolaters who have already turned from God seeking a false word. God's own words remain entirely true throughout both accounts; Micaiah, the LORD's actual prophet, tells Ahab the truth about the deception in the same chapter (1 Kings 22:19-23). The judgment itself consists of permitting those who reject truth to be confirmed in the falsehood they have already chosen to embrace -- a pattern of judicial hardening addressed elsewhere in this project regarding Pharaoh's heart.

### 10. Is it acceptable to call someone a fool, given Jesus's warning against doing so?

**References:** Psalm 14:1; Matthew 5:22

**The claim:** Psalm 14:1 states plainly, 'The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God,' applying the label 'fool' as an objective description. Jesus warns, 'whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire' (Matthew 5:22).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Matthew 5:22's warning is addressed in its immediate context to contemptuous, angry insult directed at a brother in a moment of personal animosity ('whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause... whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca... whosoever shall say, Thou fool') -- a matter of the heart's malicious contempt toward another person, not a blanket prohibition on ever using the word 'fool' in any context. Psalm 14:1 is a general, objective theological and moral description of a category of person (one who denies God and, per the psalm's own continuation, acts corruptly as a result) rather than a personal insult hurled at a specific individual in anger -- the same distinction Scripture maintains elsewhere between righteous, truthful assessment of moral categories (which wisdom literature engages in constantly, e.g. throughout Proverbs) and personally contemptuous speech toward others (which is condemned).

### 11. Does anyone ever do anything good, given the psalm's statement that 'there is none that doeth good, no, not one'?

**References:** Psalm 14:3; Romans 3:10-12

**The claim:** Psalm 14:3 states, 'They are all gone aside, they are all together become filthy: there is none that doeth good, no, not one' -- a statement Paul later quotes directly in Romans 3:10-12 as part of his case for universal human sinfulness -- yet ordinary experience and Scripture itself elsewhere describe people, including unbelievers, performing what appear to be genuinely good acts.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This sweeping statement is understood by the overwhelming majority of commentators as describing humanity's condition relative to God's own perfect standard of righteousness -- no person achieves the complete, perfect righteousness and singleness of devotion to God that his law demands, and no one is righteous enough to establish their own standing before God on the basis of their deeds (precisely the point Paul draws from it in Romans 3, building his argument that all people, without exception, need the righteousness that comes through faith rather than the law) -- not a claim that unregenerate people are incapable of any relatively good, kind, or civically beneficial act by ordinary human standards, which Scripture elsewhere assumes to be common (e.g. Luke 6:33's mention of 'sinners' who 'do good' to those who do good to them, or Romans 2:14's Gentiles who 'do by nature the things contained in the law'). The claim is about the impossibility of achieving God's own perfect standard, not about the total absence of any relative human decency.

### 12. Does God listen to and answer prayer, given that this psalm describes him refusing to answer those who cried out to him?

**References:** Psalm 18:41; Psalm 34:17; Psalm 145:18-19

**The claim:** Psalm 18:41, describing David's enemies in the heat of battle, states, 'They cried, but there was none to save them: even unto the LORD, but he answered them not.' Elsewhere, the Psalter repeatedly affirms that the LORD hears and answers the cry of his people (Psalm 34:17; 145:18-19).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the similar question addressed in this project's Exodus companion document regarding 1 Samuel 8:18's warning that God would not hear Israel's complaint about their chosen king, this verse describes a specific, limited scenario rather than a general theological claim: the ones crying out in Psalm 18:41 are David's enemies in the midst of a battle they are actively fighting against God's own anointed king, crying out in desperate self-interest rather than genuine covenant faith or repentance -- the psalm elsewhere makes clear David's own confidence that the LORD does hear and answer his own faithful cries (18:6, 'in my distress I called upon the LORD... he heard my voice'). The psalm is contrasting God's responsiveness to his faithful servant with his refusal to rescue those actively opposing God's purposes in that specific moment, not making a general claim that God never hears any cry for help, which the Psalter affirms constantly and without qualification elsewhere.

### 13. Is the law of God perfect, given the New Testament's statements that the law could not perfect anyone?

**References:** Psalm 19:7; Hebrews 7:19; Romans 8:3

**The claim:** Psalm 19:7 declares, 'The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul.' Hebrews 7:19 states 'the law made nothing perfect,' and Romans 8:3 states that the law was 'weak through the flesh' and could not accomplish what it required.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two claims address different senses of the law's 'perfection' and different questions entirely: Psalm 19:7 praises the law as a perfect, flawless revelation of God's own will and character -- a complete, trustworthy, and wholly reliable description of what righteousness requires, with no error or deficiency in what it reveals or teaches. Hebrews 7:19 and Romans 8:3 instead address the law's inability to accomplish justification or moral transformation in sinful human beings -- not a defect in the law's content as a standard, but the law's inherent limitation as a means of empowering fallen human nature to actually keep it, a limitation both texts explicitly attribute to human weakness ('weak through the flesh') rather than any flaw in the law itself. The law can be a perfect standard while still being unable, by itself, to produce in sinful people the perfect obedience it describes -- exactly the distinction both New Testament texts draw, going on to describe what the law could not do being accomplished instead through Christ and the Spirit.

### 14. Does righteousness reliably bring material prosperity, given the wisdom literature's own record of righteous suffering and wicked flourishing?

**References:** Psalm 37:25; Psalm 92:12-14

**The claim:** Psalm 37:25 states, 'I have not seen the righteous forsaken, or his seed begging bread,' and Psalm 92:12-14 declares that 'the righteous shall flourish like the palm tree... they shall still bring forth fruit in old age' -- both read by some as promising material prosperity and security as the reliable, guaranteed outcome of righteous living.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question, so far as it concerns the specific tension between Psalm 37:25's confident claim and the wicked's observed prosperity, is addressed at length elsewhere in this document (see the entry comparing Psalm 37 with Psalm 73's crisis over the wicked's flourishing); the same resolution applies to Psalm 92:12-14's similar language. Both psalms are best read as general wisdom-literature affirmations of a real, long-term pattern -- a life oriented toward God and righteousness tends, over the course of a lifetime and considered as a whole, toward stability, blessing, and provision -- rather than as an exceptionless guarantee true in every individual case at every moment, a reading the wisdom tradition itself qualifies elsewhere (including within Psalm 37's own acknowledgment that the wicked can flourish for a season, 37:35-36, and Ecclesiastes' frank observation that the righteous sometimes suffer while the wicked sometimes prosper, Ecclesiastes 7:15; 8:14). General wisdom-literature affirmations of this kind describe reliable patterns and ultimate outcomes, not universal, exceptionless laws admitting no individual hardship.

### 15. Is salvation by faith alone, given the psalm's statement that God renders to each according to their works?

**References:** Psalm 62:12; Ephesians 2:8-9; Matthew 25:31-46

**The claim:** Psalm 62:12 states that God 'renderest to every man according to his work' -- language some read as making salvation or divine favor contingent on human deeds. Paul states plainly that salvation is 'by grace... through faith... not of works, lest any man should boast' (Ephesians 2:8-9).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard theological distinction, consistent across the conservative Protestant and Catholic traditions alike despite their differing emphases, holds that salvation's root and basis is God's grace received through faith (the point Ephesians 2:8-9 makes), while works function as the evidence, fruit, and outworking of genuine faith by which that faith is subsequently assessed, rewarded, or exposed as false (the point Psalm 62:12, and similarly Matthew 25:31-46's judgment according to deeds, and James 2:14-26's insistence that genuine faith produces works, are making). A tree's fruit reveals what kind of tree it is without being what makes the tree what it is; likewise works reveal and confirm genuine faith, and Scripture consistently describes final judgment as being 'according to works' in this evidentiary sense, without this contradicting salvation's foundational basis in grace through faith rather than meritorious deeds.

### 16. Does God ever slumber or sleep, given the psalms that call on him to 'awake'?

**References:** Psalm 121:3-4; Psalm 44:23; Psalm 78:65

**The claim:** Psalm 121:4 states plainly, 'he that keepeth Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep.' Yet other psalms call on God to 'awake' as though he had been inactive or asleep: 'Awake, why sleepest thou, O Lord?' (Psalm 44:23), and Psalm 78:65 describes 'the LORD awaked as one out of sleep.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with other anthropomorphic language addressed elsewhere in this project (regarding God being 'refreshed' after resting in Exodus 31:17), the 'awake'/'sleep' language in the psalms of lament is widely understood as vivid, urgent poetic imagery expressing the psalmist's own felt experience of God's apparent inactivity or delay during a crisis -- a desperate plea for God to act decisively, using the most immediate human image for rousing someone to action -- rather than a literal claim that God had actually been asleep, unaware, or inactive, which Psalm 121:4 explicitly and directly denies within the same book. The lament psalms' 'awake' language and the didactic, doctrinal statement of Psalm 121:4 operate in different registers (urgent poetic plea versus direct theological affirmation) and are not making competing literal claims about God's actual state.

### 17. Can only God work wonders, given that prophets and apostles are also recorded performing miracles?

**References:** Psalm 136:4; Exodus 4:1-9; Acts 3:1-8

**The claim:** Psalm 136:4 praises God as the one 'who alone doeth great wonders.' Yet Scripture elsewhere records Moses (Exodus 4:1-9), Elijah, Elisha, and the apostles (e.g. Acts 3:1-8) performing what are described as miraculous signs and wonders.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Alone' in Psalm 136:4 identifies God as the sole ultimate source and originator of all genuine miraculous power -- no human being, however gifted, works wonders by their own inherent ability or independent power. Every biblical account of a prophet or apostle performing a miracle is careful to attribute the actual power to God working through that person as his instrument or channel, not to the person's own independent capability (Moses' signs are explicitly given 'that they may believe that the LORD... hath appeared unto thee,' Exodus 4:5; Peter, immediately after healing the lame man in Acts 3, asks the astonished crowd, 'why look ye so earnestly on us, as though by our own power or holiness we had made this man to walk?' (Acts 3:12), explicitly denying personal credit and crediting God alone). On this reading, Psalm 136:4's 'alone' is fully consistent with human agents serving as mere channels for the one, sole source of all miraculous power -- exactly the point the apostles themselves make whenever they perform such signs.

### 18. Is anyone justified before God?

**References:** Psalm 143:2; Romans 3:20; Galatians 2:16

**The claim:** Psalm 143:2 states, 'in thy sight shall no man living be justified' -- a sweeping denial that any person can be justified before God.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Rather than creating tension with the New Testament, this verse is directly and explicitly the Old Testament root Paul himself cites and builds upon in developing the doctrine of justification by faith: both Romans 3:20 ('by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight') and Galatians 2:16 ('by the works of the law shall no flesh be justified') closely echo, and in Romans 3:20's case nearly quote, Psalm 143:2's own language, using it as the very foundation for Paul's argument that justification cannot be achieved through law-keeping and must instead come through faith in Christ. Far from being in tension with the New Testament's teaching, Psalm 143:2 is the Old Testament seed of that same teaching -- the psalm's honest acknowledgment that no one achieves justification by their own righteousness before God is precisely the premise the gospel of justification by faith is presented as the answer to, not a claim the New Testament contradicts or must explain away.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 19. 'I have not seen the righteous forsaken' -- vs. the crisis of the wicked's prosperity

**References:** Psalm 37:25; Psalm 73:1-14

**The claim:** Psalm 37:25 makes a sweeping, confident claim from personal experience: 'I have been young, and now am old, yet I have not seen the righteous forsaken or his children begging for bread.' Psalm 73, by contrast, opens with the psalmist's own faith nearly failing precisely because he observes the wicked flourishing while apparently suffering no consequence -- 'they have no pangs until death; their bodies are fat and sleek... they are not in trouble as others are' (73:4-5) -- to the point that the psalmist confesses, 'my feet had almost stumbled' (73:2) over the apparent injustice.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both psalms' full arguments plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading each psalm's full trajectory (including Psalm 37's own acknowledgment of the same tension it addresses) rather than isolated verses, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these not as flatly contradictory claims about the same observable reality, but as two different vantage points within the Psalter's own honest range of human experience and reflection: Psalm 37 as a whole (read past verse 25 alone) is itself a wisdom psalm explicitly written to address exactly the temptation Psalm 73 describes -- its opening verses immediately instruct, 'fret not yourself because of evildoers' (37:1), acknowledging the same observed phenomenon (the wicked's apparent, temporary prosperity, 37:35-36) that Psalm 73 wrestles with, while affirming a longer-term, ultimate confidence that such prosperity does not last and that God's people are never ultimately abandoned across a full lifetime's course. Psalm 73 itself resolves its own crisis by verse's end (73:17-20) through the same long-term, ultimate perspective, gained specifically 'when I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end' -- both psalms ultimately affirm the same underlying confidence (that the wicked's prosperity is temporary and the righteous are not ultimately abandoned), differing only in how honestly and at what length each one first voices the genuine, felt difficulty of that claim before arriving at it.

### 20. Within the same psalm: the Davidic covenant celebrated as unconditional, then God accused of renouncing it

**References:** Psalm 89:1-4, 28-37; Psalm 89:38-39, 46-49

**The claim:** Psalm 89 opens with an extended, confident celebration of God's unconditional covenant promise to David -- 'I will establish your offspring forever... my covenant will stand firm for him' (89:4, 28), explicitly affirming that even if David's descendants sin, God's covenant loyalty will not be revoked (89:30-34). Yet the same psalm's later half turns to bitter complaint, directly accusing God of having done the opposite of what the opening celebrated: 'you have renounced the covenant with your servant; you have defiled his crown in the dust... how long, O LORD? Will you hide yourself forever?' (89:38-39, 46).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both halves of Psalm 89 plainly and consistently as given, without softening either the celebration or the complaint; the harmonization rests on recognizing deliberate lament structure that holds a promise's truth and its apparent non-fulfillment together without resolving them, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this striking internal tension as the psalm's own deliberate literary and theological structure, not an oversight: the psalm is widely understood to have been composed (or later used) at a specific moment of national crisis -- likely following a significant military defeat or the apparent collapse of the Davidic monarchy's fortunes (some associate it with the Babylonian conquest and exile) -- deliberately juxtaposing the full, unconditional weight of God's covenant promise against the community's raw, honest experience of apparent covenant failure, without resolving the tension through easy theological explanation within the psalm itself. This pattern (affirming a promise's full truth while honestly voicing the anguish of its apparent non-fulfillment in the present moment) is a recognized feature of biblical lament, distinct from either denying the promise's validity or pretending the community's suffering does not raise a real and difficult question -- the psalm ends not with resolution but with a a direct, unresolved plea (89:49-51), leaving the tension for God's future action to answer rather than for the psalmist to explain away, a form of honest faith rather than a genuine logical contradiction.

### 21. Psalm 109's curses against an enemy -- and Peter's application of verse 8 to Judas

**References:** Psalm 109:6-15; Acts 1:15-20

**The claim:** Psalm 109 contains an extended series of curses against a personal enemy, asking that his days be few, that his children be fatherless and his wife a widow, that his descendants be cut off and reduced to begging, and that 'no one extend kindness to him' (109:6-15) -- among the harshest imprecatory language in the Psalter. In Acts 1:20, Peter cites a portion of this same psalm ('let his camp become desolate... let another take his office') and applies it directly to Judas Iscariot's fate and replacement, raising a question about how such harsh, personally vindictive language can be treated as legitimately prophetic and applicable to a specific New Testament situation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Psalm 109's full curse and Peter's specific citation of verse 8 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing selective, purpose-specific New Testament citation of a single verse rather than wholesale endorsement of the psalm's full content, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that Peter's citation in Acts 1:20 draws specifically and narrowly on verse 8 alone ('let his office/camp be taken by another'), not the psalm's broader and harsher curses concerning the enemy's family in verses 9-15, and that this kind of selective, purpose-specific citation of a single verse from a longer psalm for its specific applicability to a new situation is a standard and well-attested feature of New Testament use of the Old Testament, not a wholesale endorsement of every line surrounding the quoted verse. More broadly, most commentators read the imprecatory psalms, including this one, along the same lines addressed for Psalm 137 above (entry 1) -- as honest, raw lament prayer entrusting vengeance to God's own justice rather than personal action, appropriate for describing genuinely severe covenant betrayal (which both the psalm's original context and Judas's own betrayal of Jesus represent) without this constituting a general prescription for how believers should treat their own personal enemies.

## Historical Superscriptions

### 22. Psalm 34's superscription names 'Abimelech' -- but 1 Samuel 21 names the king Achish

**References:** Psalm 34 (superscription); 1 Samuel 21:10-15

**The claim:** Psalm 34's superscription (the introductory note, in Hebrew tradition considered part of the received text though not universally regarded as part of the original inspired composition) states the psalm was written 'when he changed his behavior before Abimelech, so that he drove him out, and he went away' -- an episode matching only one story in the historical books: 1 Samuel 21:10-15, where David feigns madness before the Philistine king of Gath to escape recognition and danger. That narrative, however, consistently names the king 'Achish,' not Abimelech.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the superscription's 'Abimelech' exactly as given, without substituting 'Achish' or otherwise harmonizing the name to match 1 Samuel 21; the explanation rests on understanding 'Abimelech' as a dynastic title rather than translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard and widely accepted explanation, held by conservative commentators across many centuries, is that 'Abimelech' (literally 'my father is king,' or 'father-king') functioned as a dynastic title for Philistine kings of Gath generally, comparable to how 'Pharaoh' served as a title for Egyptian kings and 'Caesar' for Roman emperors, rather than as this specific king's personal name -- with 'Achish' being his personal name and 'Abimelech' his royal title, both used to refer to the same individual in different contexts (compare Genesis 20, 21, and 26, where multiple Philistine kings of the same region are also called Abimelech across different generations, supporting the title reading). Some commentators, while acknowledging this explanation is plausible and widely held, note candidly that it cannot be independently, externally verified, and a minority view holds the superscription (composed later than the psalm itself, and universally acknowledged by conservative and critical scholars alike to lack the same inspired status as the psalm's own text) may simply reflect an editorial error or a later association drawn imprecisely from the general pattern of Genesis's Abimelech narratives, without this affecting the reliability or inspiration of the psalm's own actual words.

## Numbers and Text

### 23. 'A little lower than the angels' -- or 'than God'? Translation variance in Psalm 8:5, and its use in Hebrews

**References:** Psalm 8:5; Hebrews 2:7

**The claim:** Psalm 8:5, part of a psalm reflecting on humanity's exalted place in creation, is traditionally rendered (following the KJV) 'You have made him a little lower than the angels' -- language directly quoted in Hebrews 2:7, applying the psalm messianically to Christ. The underlying Hebrew word, however (elohim), most commonly means 'God' rather than 'angels' throughout the Old Testament, raising a question about which sense is intended and why translations differ.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'Yet You have made him a little lower than God, And You have crowned him with glory and honor,' following the more literal Hebrew elohim reading (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'a little lower than God,' with a footnote noting the LXX/Hebrews 'angels' tradition.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'a little lower than the heavenly beings,' with a footnote noting 'or than God,' reflecting the Hebrew elohim's more common sense while gesturing toward the angelic/divine-council reading in the main text.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'a little lower than the angels,' following the Septuagint tradition also reflected in Hebrews 2:7.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders 'a little lower than the angels.'
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'a little lower than God,' the more literal rendering of elohim, with a footnote noting 'or angels.'
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'Yet You have made him a little lower than the angels, And You crown him with glory and majesty!' -- notably departing from NASB's 'God' at this specific verse, unlike LSB's usual close tracking of NASB's text-critical choices (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'a little lower than the angels,' following the Septuagint/Hebrews tradition.
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'a little lower than God' (or, in some printings, 'the angels'), reflecting the same underlying translation choice.
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'Yet thou hast made him little less than God, and dost crown him with glory and honor,' following the more literal Hebrew elohim reading (verified against the published RSVCE text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction between two texts but a recognized translation choice reflecting the genuine flexibility of the Hebrew term elohim, which -- while most frequently referring to God -- is also used elsewhere in the Old Testament for members of the divine heavenly council or angelic beings (a usage some scholars find in Psalm 8:5's immediate context, given the psalm's broader interest in humanity's place within the created cosmic order). The ancient Greek Septuagint translation, made centuries before Hebrews was written, rendered this word as angelous ('angels') at Psalm 8:5, and the author of Hebrews, writing in Greek and generally quoting the Septuagint's wording throughout the letter, follows that established rendering when citing the verse -- meaning Hebrews 2:7's 'angels' reflects a translation tradition already in place rather than an independent, competing claim about the correct sense of the original Hebrew. Both renderings ('angels,' following the Septuagint tradition Hebrews quotes, and 'God,' a more literal rendering of the Hebrew elohim in its most common sense) are defensible translations of the same underlying, genuinely flexible term.

### 24. Psalm 145's missing 'nun' verse -- a genuine, well-documented textual gap with real translation differences

**References:** Psalm 145:13

**The claim:** Psalm 145 is an acrostic poem, with each successive verse beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet -- except that, in the standard Masoretic Hebrew text, there is no verse beginning with the letter nun (the fourteenth letter), which would be expected between the current verses 13 and 14. This creates a genuine gap in an otherwise complete literary pattern, and several ancient witnesses -- the Dead Sea Scrolls (specifically the Great Psalms Scroll, 11QPsa), the Greek Septuagint, the Syriac Peshitta, and at least one medieval Hebrew manuscript -- all independently preserve a nun-verse at exactly this point, reading (in most reconstructions) something close to 'The LORD is faithful in all his words, and gracious in all his deeds.'

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP includes the nun-verse: 'Your rule is for all generations. The Lord is faithful in all His words and gracious in all His actions' (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB (following the earlier Holman Christian Standard Bible's editorial decision) includes the nun-verse, generally without brackets, alongside an explanatory footnote.
- **ESV:** ESV includes the nun-verse, set off in brackets to indicate its uncertain textual status, with a footnote explaining it is 'supplied by one Hebrew manuscript, Septuagint, Syriac (compare Dead Sea Scroll).'
- **KJV:** KJV omits the nun-verse entirely, following the Masoretic Hebrew text as received, with 21 verses total (skipping directly from the mem-verse to the samek-verse).
- **NKJV:** NKJV also omits the nun-verse, following the same Masoretic tradition.
- **NASB:** The NASB 1995 edition omits the nun-verse, following the Masoretic tradition; the current NASB 2020 edition includes it, reflecting a more recent editorial decision to follow the Dead Sea Scrolls and other ancient witnesses.
- **LSB:** LSB's specific choice was not independently confirmed against a published copy in this pass; given LSB's general tendency to track the NASB1995 text closely on matters of this kind, it may well omit the verse as NASB1995 does, though the current NASB's own 2020 revision suggests this is not certain.
- **NIV:** NIV includes the nun-verse directly within the main numbered text (as part of verse 13), with a footnote explaining that most manuscripts of the Masoretic Text lack it, while the Dead Sea Scrolls, Syriac, and one Masoretic manuscript include it.
- **NLT:** NLT most likely includes the nun-verse in the main text, consistent with its general translation philosophy of resolving such textual gaps for readability, though this specific verse was not independently confirmed against a published NLT copy in this pass.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE most likely includes the nun-verse, consistent with the original 1952 RSV's own documented inclusion of it, though this specific verse in the RSV2CE printing was not independently confirmed against a published copy in this pass.

**Reconciliation:** Textual scholars are genuinely divided, and this entry does not force a resolution. Some hold the nun-verse was original to the psalm, consistent with the otherwise complete acrostic pattern, and was accidentally lost at an early stage of the Masoretic Hebrew textual tradition. This reading is supported by its independent, agreeing attestation across several distinct ancient textual witnesses -- the Dead Sea Scrolls, predating the Masoretic tradition by roughly a thousand years, the Septuagint, and the Syriac -- none of which are likely to have influenced one another directly on this specific point, which would be a remarkable coincidence if the verse were merely and independently invented three or more separate times. Others hold the verse was a later scribal addition, composed specifically to complete the acrostic pattern once its absence was noticed; some note the verse's second half closely echoes wording found in Psalm 145:17 itself, which could indicate a later composer drew on that verse as a template. The deliberate absence of the nun-verse in the ancient Jewish tradition is sometimes explained by scholars, including in classical rabbinic sources, as an intentional allusion to the Hebrew word for 'fall' (nophelet), avoiding an ominous association with fallenness or disaster at exactly this point in an otherwise triumphant psalm about God's kingdom -- a reading some rabbinic sources connect to Amos 5:2, which begins with the same root.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 25. Reflecting on mortality: 'the days of our years are threescore years and ten' -- and the patriarchs' far longer lifespans

**References:** Psalm 90:10; Genesis 5; Genesis 11:10-32

**The claim:** Psalm 90:10, traditionally attributed to Moses and reflecting on human mortality, states that a typical human lifespan is 'threescore years and ten' (70 years), or 'by reason of strength... fourscore' (80) -- figures broadly consistent with observed human lifespans across recorded history. This stands in apparent contrast with the far longer lifespans recorded for the earliest patriarchs in Genesis 5 (several exceeding 900 years) and the more gradually declining, but still much longer, lifespans of the patriarchs following the flood in Genesis 11 (ranging from several hundred years down to roughly 200).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Psalm 90:10's figures and the Genesis genealogies' figures exactly as given, without adjusting either; the harmonization concerns recognizing these as descriptions of two different historical eras, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction between two competing claims about the same historical period, but as an accurate description of two different eras separated by many centuries and, according to the text's own account, a significant divine intervention: Genesis 6:3 explicitly records God setting a coming limit on human lifespan ('his days shall be 120 years') in connection with humanity's increasing corruption before the flood, a limit the subsequent genealogies of Genesis 11 show being approached only gradually over many subsequent generations rather than instantaneously. By the time of Moses (traditionally credited with Psalm 90, and himself living to 120 years, Deuteronomy 34:7, itself an outlier even by the psalm's own standard), human lifespans had, on the text's own account, settled into something much closer to the range Psalm 90:10 describes as ordinary for his own era and beyond -- the psalm accurately reflects lifespan norms many centuries after the patriarchal period it is not describing, rather than contradicting the earlier genealogies' account of a different, earlier era.
