# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 Corinthians

This document catalogs 11 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 1 Corinthians,
either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with 2 Corinthians and the companion
2 Maccabees and Acts documents. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses one of the most
substantively debated internal tensions in the entire Pauline corpus (women's silence in 14:34-
35 against the assumption of women praying and prophesying in 11:5), involving both a genuine
textual-critical dimension (a documented, though minority, manuscript variant in verse
placement) and multiple seriously held interpretive positions, including an active
interpolation theory defended by named textual scholars. Entry 3 addresses one of the most
genuinely mysterious individual verses in the New Testament -- "baptized on behalf of the
dead" -- which has generated an estimated forty distinct scholarly interpretations and forms
the primary scriptural basis for a specific modern religious practice.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 3, 4, and 5 are all presented with genuine scholarly
diversity of opinion, including on entry 5's underlying doctrinal question, which this
project does not adjudicate consistent with its treatment of the same question in the
companion 2 Maccabees document.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. 'Women should keep silent in the churches' -- vs. this letter's own earlier assumption that women pray and prophesy publicly

**References:** 1 Corinthians 14:34-35; 1 Corinthians 11:2-16

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 instructs, 'the women should keep silent in the churches. For they are not permitted to speak, but should be in submission... it is shameful for a woman to speak in church.' Yet the same letter, only three chapters earlier, explicitly addresses how women should conduct themselves while praying and prophesying in the gathered assembly (11:5, 'every wife who prays or prophesies with her head uncovered dishonors her head') -- an activity chapter 11 regulates rather than forbids, apparently in direct tension with chapter 14's own, considerably more absolute-sounding prohibition on women speaking at all. This is one of the most substantively debated internal tensions in the entire Pauline corpus, with significant, ongoing implications for church practice.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 in its standard location (after 14:33), consistent with the great majority of Greek manuscripts; none of the ten translations relocates the passage to follow 14:40, though some include a footnote noting this variant placement exists in a minority of ancient manuscripts.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and extensively debated question with several seriously held positions, and this entry presents the range rather than a single certain answer, addressing both a textual-critical and an interpretive dimension. On the textual question: a small number of ancient Western manuscripts (and the fourth-century commentator Ambrosiaster) place these two verses after 14:40 rather than in their usual location, a genuine manuscript variation that has led a number of scholars -- including Gordon Fee, Philip Payne, and, more recently, a detailed academic study specifically analyzing this passage alongside a related textual anomaly at 16:19 -- to argue the verses were not originally written by Paul but were added by a later scribe or editor, noting the passage's own abrupt interruption of Paul's otherwise continuous argument about prophecy (which resumes smoothly if 14:34-35 is removed, flowing directly from 14:33 to 14:36) and its use of some vocabulary uncharacteristic of Paul's writing elsewhere. Other serious textual scholars and commentators (including Bruce Winter, Thomas Schreiner, Anthony Thiselton, D.A. Carson, Ben Witherington, and Craig Keener, among others) reject the interpolation theory, noting that literally every surviving Greek manuscript of 1 Corinthians includes these verses somewhere in the text (unlike the passages addressed regarding Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11 in their own companion documents, where entire manuscripts exist that omit the material completely), and that the displacement to after verse 40 in a handful of manuscripts is better explained as a scribal attempt to improve the passage's placement within Paul's argument, not as evidence the material was independently inserted at two different points. Among those who accept the passage as authentically Pauline, the leading interpretive harmonizations include: that 14:34-35 addresses a specific, local disruption (perhaps married women interrupting the service to ask their husbands questions, or a specific instance of disorderly conduct during the evaluation of prophecies, 14:29, rather than a categorical ban on all female speech); that Paul may be quoting and then refuting a slogan the Corinthians themselves had written to him (a documented rhetorical pattern elsewhere in this letter, e.g., 6:12, 10:23); or that 14:34-35's silence specifically concerns a particular kind of disruptive speech (such as public evaluation or judgment of prophetic utterances, which 14:29 assigns to 'the others' to weigh) rather than the praying and prophesying chapter 11 already assumes and regulates. This project does not adjudicate between the interpolation theory and these various harmonizations of an authentic text, noting that this remains one of the most substantively debated passages in Pauline studies.

### 2. 'Hand this man over to Satan' -- and his apparent later restoration in 2 Corinthians

**References:** 1 Corinthians 5:1-5; 2 Corinthians 2:5-11

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 5:1-5 addresses a specific case of serious sexual immorality within the Corinthian church (a man in a relationship with his father's wife), instructing the church to 'deliver this man to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord' -- a severe act of church discipline involving formal exclusion from the community. 2 Corinthians 2:5-11, written to the same church some time later, appears to reference a disciplined offender (possibly, though not certainly, the same individual) whose punishment 'by the majority is enough,' and instructs the church to 'forgive and comfort him, or he may be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow,' urging the church to 'reaffirm your love for him.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading the two passages as sequential stages of a single disciplinary process, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not read these two passages as contradictory, but as depicting sequential stages of a single, coherent process of church discipline working exactly as intended. 1 Corinthians 5's severe discipline -- formal exclusion, described starkly as being 'handed over to Satan,' meaning removal from the protective fellowship and spiritual covering of the church community -- was always explicitly stated to have a restorative goal, 'so that his spirit may be saved' (5:5), not permanent, unqualified condemnation. 2 Corinthians 2:5-11 is generally read as describing the successful outcome of that same disciplinary process. The offender has evidently repented in response to the church's discipline, and Paul now instructs the church to complete the process appropriately by extending forgiveness and restoring him to fellowship. He explicitly warns that continued, indefinite exclusion beyond the point of genuine repentance risks 'excessive sorrow' and could itself become a pastoral failure (2:7), rather than genuine discipline. This is generally read as depicting church discipline functioning exactly as intended across two letters -- firm correction leading to repentance, followed by full restoration -- rather than as two conflicting instructions about the same situation, though some scholars note the identification of the two offenders as definitely the same individual cannot be established with absolute certainty from the text alone.

### 3. Did Paul actually see the risen Jesus, given the differences between his Damascus road encounter and the earlier resurrection appearances?

**References:** 1 Corinthians 15:3-8; Acts 9:3-9; 1 Corinthians 9:1

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 15:5-8 lists Paul's own encounter with the risen Christ alongside the appearances to Peter, the Twelve, and five hundred witnesses, stating 'last of all he was seen of me also, as of one born out of due time.' Some have questioned whether Paul's Damascus road experience (Acts 9:3-9, involving a blinding light and a voice from heaven, occurring after Jesus' ascension) belongs in the same category as the earlier, more directly physical post-resurrection appearances to the other apostles.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Paul himself consistently and repeatedly treats his Damascus road encounter as a genuine, objective appearance of the risen, glorified Christ -- not a subjective vision, dream, or hallucination -- explicitly grounding his own claim to apostolic authority on this basis: 'Am I not an apostle? have I not seen Jesus Christ our Lord?' (1 Corinthians 9:1). While the manner and timing of Paul's encounter differed from the earlier appearances (occurring later, after the ascension, and involving Christ's glorified, heavenly form rather than the same kind of earthly post-resurrection encounters the Eleven experienced, which Paul himself signals by calling it an appearance 'as of one born out of due time' -- an unusual, later-than-expected birth), he places it within the same fundamental category of genuine, historical resurrection appearances rather than a different kind of experience, and 1 Corinthians 15:8 itself explicitly lists it in sequence alongside the other apostolic witnesses as part of the same unified testimony to the resurrection.

## Numbers and Text

### 4. 'Baptized on behalf of the dead' -- one of the most genuinely mysterious verses in the New Testament

**References:** 1 Corinthians 15:29

**The claim:** In the midst of his extended defense of the bodily resurrection, Paul asks, seemingly in passing and without further explanation, 'Otherwise, what do people mean by being baptized on behalf of the dead? If the dead are not raised at all, why are people baptized on their behalf?' (15:29) -- a fleeting, unexplained reference to an apparent practice of proxy baptism performed on behalf of deceased individuals, mentioned nowhere else in the entire New Testament, and offered without any accompanying instruction, endorsement, or condemnation. This verse has generated an unusually large number of scholarly interpretations (commonly estimated at around forty distinct proposals) and, notably, forms the primary scriptural basis cited by the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Mormons) for its own practice of proxy baptism for deceased ancestors.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders 'Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead,' preserving the third-person 'they/them' pronoun shift discussed above.

**Reconciliation:** This is among the most genuinely disputed and difficult individual verses addressed anywhere in this project, and this entry presents the range of leading proposals rather than a single confident answer, since serious scholars remain divided even after extensive study. Several major lines of interpretation exist. Many mainstream and evangelical commentators read Paul as describing, without endorsing, an actual practice among some Corinthians. One source specifically notes the verse's own grammatical shift from Paul's consistent first-person 'we/us/you' pronouns throughout the surrounding argument to a third-person 'they/them' specifically and only at this verse. On this reading, Paul may be citing a fringe group who did not themselves believe in bodily resurrection, pointing to their own practical inconsistency: if they deny the resurrection, why would they bother being baptized on behalf of already-dead people at all? This treats the verse as reporting a genuine but likely misguided local practice, cited rhetorically to expose a logical inconsistency in the resurrection-deniers' own conduct, rather than as approval of the practice itself. Other scholars read 'the dead' more generally to refer to the mortal bodies of living believers themselves, rather than to deceased individuals, understanding the phrase as describing living Christians being baptized in light of, or in anticipation of, their own future bodily death and resurrection. A minority reading connects the passage to a documented, similar practice in 2 Maccabees 12:44-45, addressed at length in this project's companion 2 Maccabees document, regarding prayers and sacrifice offered for the sins of the already-dead -- suggesting a continuity of Jewish and early Christian concern for benefiting the deceased through the actions of the living, though without the same specific ritual, baptism, being described in the earlier text. The great majority of mainstream biblical scholars and virtually all denominational traditions outside the Latter-day Saints movement, including scholars with no particular polemical stake in the outcome, reject the specific reading that Paul is here endorsing or instructing a normative practice of proxy baptism securing salvation for the unbaptized dead. They note that such a practice would sit in substantial tension with Paul's own consistently articulated theology of salvation by grace through each individual's own personal faith found throughout his other letters, and that the church, for the first centuries of its history, associated this specific practice only with a small number of groups later regarded as heterodox. This project does not resolve every remaining detail of this genuinely difficult verse. Even among those who reject the Latter-day Saints' specific reading, real scholarly disagreement remains about precisely what practice, if any, Paul is describing.

## Theological Interpretation

### 5. 'A symbol of authority on her head, because of the angels' -- a notoriously difficult phrase

**References:** 1 Corinthians 11:2-16; 1 Corinthians 11:10

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 11:10, within Paul's extended discussion of head coverings and gender distinctions in corporate worship, gives a specific, otherwise unexplained reason for a woman to have 'a symbol of authority on her head': 'because of the angels' -- a brief, cryptic phrase Paul does not further elaborate, and which has generated substantial scholarly disagreement about its intended meaning.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Corinthians 11:10's phrase 'because of the angels' consistently, without further clarification in the text itself; the interpretive question addressed here remains open regardless of translation, and is not resolved by any of the ten translations' specific wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely difficult and much-discussed phrase, and this entry presents the leading proposals rather than a single confident answer. Some commentators connect the phrase to Genesis 6:1-4's obscure account of 'the sons of God' (sometimes understood as fallen angels) taking human wives, reading Paul as warning against a similar kind of impropriety or spiritual vulnerability in corporate worship, where angels are understood to be present and observing. Others read 'angels' here as referring to good, holy angels who observe and participate in the church's worship (compare 1 Corinthians 4:9, 1 Timothy 5:21, both referencing angels as witnesses to church conduct), with the woman's head covering functioning as an appropriate sign of created order and modesty befitting worship conducted in the angels' own observing presence, rather than addressing any specific danger from fallen spiritual beings. Still others propose the angels in view are human messengers (the Greek word angelos can denote either angels or human messengers in different contexts) -- perhaps visiting representatives from other churches, whose presence would call for decorum consistent with wider church practice. This project does not adjudicate between these proposals, noting only that the phrase's brevity and Paul's own lack of further explanation have left its precise meaning a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than settled consensus.

### 6. 'He himself will be saved, but only as through fire' -- a further connection to the purgatory discussion

**References:** 1 Corinthians 3:10-15

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 3:10-15 describes a future judgment in which each believer's own works (built upon the foundation of Christ) will be tested 'by fire,' with some works surviving and being rewarded, and others being 'burned up,' resulting in loss for that person -- yet Paul adds, 'he himself will be saved, but only as through fire' (3:15), language some readers connect to the broader question of purgatorial purification after death, addressed at greater length in this project's companion 2 Maccabees document regarding that book's own prayers and sacrifice offered for the dead.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Corinthians 3:10-15 plainly and consistently as given; the theological question addressed here remains genuinely and sincerely contested among major Christian traditions, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This passage is understood differently across major Christian traditions, and this project does not adjudicate the underlying doctrinal question, consistent with its treatment of the comparable passage in the companion 2 Maccabees document. Most Protestant commentators read this passage as describing a metaphorical testing and evaluation of a believer's works and ministry at the final judgment (with 'fire' functioning as an image of testing and refining, not literal purgatorial suffering), affecting the believer's eternal reward but not their fundamental salvation, which the passage's own wording ('he himself will be saved') already secures regardless of the specific outcome of this testing. Catholic and Orthodox theology, by contrast, has historically read this passage (often alongside 2 Maccabees 12:38-46) as offering some scriptural support for the doctrine of purgatory -- a state of purification for those who die in a state of grace but are not yet fully purified, prior to entering the fullness of heaven -- though even within these traditions, this passage is typically treated as suggestive rather than as the primary or most explicit scriptural basis for the doctrine. This project takes no position on which reading is correct.

### 7. What is the foundation of the church -- Christ alone, or the apostles as well?

**References:** 1 Corinthians 3:10-11; Ephesians 2:20; Matthew 16:18

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 3:11 states, 'other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ,' identifying Christ as the church's sole foundation. Ephesians 2:20, however, describes believers as 'built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ himself being the chief corner stone' -- naming the apostles and prophets as part of the foundation alongside Christ. Separately, Matthew 16:18 has Jesus tell Peter, 'thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church' -- a statement whose precise referent (Peter himself, or Peter's confession of faith) remains a significant point of difference between major Christian traditions.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Corinthians 3:11 and Ephesians 2:20 are read by most commentators as using 'foundation' in two related but distinguishable senses rather than competing for a single exclusive claim: Christ alone is the foundation in the ultimate, exclusive sense of being the sole basis and source of salvation and the church's very existence (the point 1 Corinthians 3:11 makes, in a context specifically warning against building the church's ministry on any other spiritual basis) -- a foundation no other person or teaching can replace. Ephesians 2:20's reference to the apostles and prophets describes a distinct, secondary sense of 'foundation' -- the foundational teaching and authoritative ministry through which the church was historically established and continues to be built up, with Christ himself still explicitly identified in the very same verse as the 'chief corner stone' binding and anchoring that entire structure, retaining ultimate primacy even within this secondary sense. On Matthew 16:18's specific reference to Peter, this touches a further, historically significant point of difference between Catholic and Protestant traditions: Catholic theology has generally read Jesus' statement as identifying Peter himself (and derivatively, the papacy as his continuing office) as the specific 'rock' granted a unique, ongoing governing authority in the church; most Protestant traditions instead read the 'rock' as either Peter's confession of Jesus as the Christ (the statement immediately preceding, 16:16) or Peter considered specifically as the first and representative confessor of that apostolic faith, without this establishing an ongoing, transferable office of singular authority. This document does not adjudicate this specific denominational disagreement, noting only that both traditions affirm Christ's own ultimate primacy as the church's sole and sufficient saving foundation in the sense 1 Corinthians 3:11 describes.

## Theological Tensions

### 8. Food offered to idols -- addressed at length in the companion Acts document

**References:** 1 Corinthians 8:1-13; 1 Corinthians 10:14-33; Acts 15:19-20, 28-29

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 8 and 10:14-33 contain Paul's own fullest treatment of the question addressed at length in the companion Acts document -- how believers should regard meat that had been offered to idols before reaching the marketplace, and whether the Jerusalem Council's decree on this subject (Acts 15:29) is compatible with Paul's own more nuanced guidance.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Corinthians 8 and 10:14-33 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Acts document for the fuller discussion of this question's relationship to the Jerusalem Council's decree.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Acts document, which addresses the standard resolution: the Jerusalem decree's prohibition targets actual participation in pagan cultic worship and temple feasts, while Paul's guidance in 1 Corinthians addresses the separate, more mundane question of ordinary meat purchased in the marketplace or served at a private meal, which had likely originated from temple sacrifice as a normal feature of the ancient meat market but was not itself being consumed within any actual idolatrous ritual -- both texts consistently prohibit genuine cultic participation while allowing ordinary consumption of meat with an uncertain history.

### 9. Is God the author of confusion, given the several instances of God bringing confusion upon his enemies?

**References:** 1 Corinthians 14:33; Judges 7:22; 1 Samuel 14:20; Genesis 11:7-9

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 14:33 states plainly, 'God is not the author of confusion, but of peace,' in the context of Paul's instructions for orderly corporate worship. Elsewhere, Scripture describes God directly causing confusion: setting 'every man's sword against his fellow' in the Midianite camp (Judges 7:22) and among the Philistines (1 Samuel 14:20), and confusing human language at Babel (Genesis 11:7-9).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Corinthians 14:33 addresses a specific context -- the orderly conduct of Christian worship, where Paul is correcting a chaotic, disorderly use of spiritual gifts within the gathered church -- affirming that God's own character and purposes for his people's worship are oriented toward peace and order, not disorder, as the basis for the practical instructions that follow. The instances of God causing confusion among Israel's enemies (Judges 7; 1 Samuel 14) and at Babel (Genesis 11) describe a specific, targeted act of divine judgment against opposing armies or against human rebellion, a different category of action entirely -- bringing disorder upon God's enemies as an act of judgment is not in tension with God desiring and ordaining peace and order within his own people's worship and community life, the specific concern 1 Corinthians 14:33 addresses.

## Literary Composition

### 10. 'Not I, but the Lord' vs. 'I, not the Lord' -- Paul's own explicit distinction between dominical and apostolic instruction

**References:** 1 Corinthians 7:10-12

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 7:10-12 contains a striking, self-aware distinction Paul draws within his own teaching on marriage: regarding a believing couple's obligation not to divorce, he states, 'I give this charge, not I, but the Lord' (7:10, explicitly invoking Jesus' own recorded teaching on divorce), but regarding believers married to unbelieving spouses, a situation Jesus' own recorded teaching did not directly address, Paul states, 'I say, not the Lord' (7:12) -- explicitly distinguishing his own apostolic judgment on a new question from a direct citation of Jesus' own words.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Corinthians 7:10-12 plainly and consistently as given, preserving Paul's own explicit distinction; this entry addresses a notable, self-aware feature of Paul's own teaching regarding the sources of his authority, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a distinctive and theologically significant feature of Paul's own self-understanding of his apostolic authority and its relationship to Jesus' own recorded teaching, worth noting explicitly. Rather than creating two different tiers of authority, with 7:12's guidance somehow less binding or inspired than 7:10's, most commentators read Paul's own careful distinction as reflecting his awareness of, and honesty about, which specific teachings had direct precedent in Jesus' own recorded words, available to Paul through the developing oral and written tradition of Jesus' teaching, and which represented his own apostolic application and judgment extending that teaching to new circumstances Jesus had not directly addressed. Both categories, in traditional Christian theological understanding, are received as authoritative and inspired guidance for the church; Paul's own letters elsewhere claim apostolic authority for his own teaching more generally, e.g. 1 Corinthians 14:37, 'the things I am writing to you are a command of the Lord.' Paul's own transparent distinction here has been read by many commentators as a valuable, self-aware witness to how the earliest church itself understood the relationship between Jesus' own direct teaching and the apostles' own Spirit-guided extension and application of it to new situations.

## Ethical Tensions

### 11. Should believers speak in tongues, or does Paul restrict the practice?

**References:** 1 Corinthians 14:39; 1 Corinthians 14:19, 27-28

**The claim:** 1 Corinthians 14:39 instructs, 'forbid not to speak with tongues.' Yet the same chapter has Paul say he would 'rather speak five words with my understanding' than 'ten thousand words in an unknown tongue' in the assembly (14:19), and instructs that if there is no interpreter present, 'let him keep silence in the church' regarding tongues (14:27-28) -- language read by some as restricting or discouraging the very practice 14:39 says should not be forbidden.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Paul's instruction is consistently regulatory rather than prohibitive throughout the chapter, and 14:39 explicitly confirms this: he does not forbid the gift of tongues as such (affirming its legitimacy and continued place among the Spirit's gifts, and even noting his own personal practice of it, 14:18), while regulating its use in the specific context of gathered, public worship for the sake of the whole church's edification and the presence of unbelieving outsiders who would not understand an untranslated utterance (14:23's concern that outsiders would think the church 'mad'). His stated preference for intelligible speech in the assembly (14:19) and his requirement of interpretation for public tongues-speaking (14:27-28) are not a ban on tongues, but specific, practical conditions for its edifying and orderly use in that particular setting -- fully consistent with, rather than contradicting, 14:39's instruction not to forbid the gift altogether, since the whole chapter's stated governing principle is that 'all things be done unto edifying' (14:26).
