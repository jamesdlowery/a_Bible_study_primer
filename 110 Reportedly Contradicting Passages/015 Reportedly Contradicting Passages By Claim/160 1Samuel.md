# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 Samuel

This document catalogs 26 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of 1
Samuel, either entirely internal to 1 Samuel or between 1 Samuel and a directly related
passage elsewhere (Deuteronomy, 2 Samuel, 1 Chronicles). For each item, this document states
the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entry 9 (the witch of Endor) is presented without a single, confident
resolution, since serious interpreters have long disagreed about whether Samuel's spirit
genuinely appeared, a deception occurred, or something else -- consistent with this project's
practice of flagging live disagreements rather than manufacturing false certainty.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where none commands real consensus,
an honest account of the live disagreement. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary
texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. How did Saul become king -- private anointing, public lot, or acclamation after victory?

**References:** 1 Samuel 9:15-10:1; 1 Samuel 10:17-25; 1 Samuel 11:12-15

**The claim:** 1 Samuel describes what looks like three distinct occasions on which Saul becomes king: Samuel privately anoints him in advance, with no public audience (9:15-10:1); Samuel later has Saul publicly selected by sacred lot before the assembled tribes at Mizpah, where Saul is found hiding among the baggage (10:17-24); and, after Saul's decisive victory over the Ammonites at Jabesh-gilead, the people gather at Gilgal and 'made Saul king before the LORD' a third time (11:14-15).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the three episodes plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing a staged accession process, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these not as three competing, contradictory accounts of a single event but as three distinct, sequential stages of a single process by which Saul's kingship was established and progressively confirmed to different audiences: the private anointing (9:15-10:1) establishes God's choice to Saul and Samuel alone; the public lot (10:17-24) reveals that choice to the nation as a whole, though evidently without full acceptance (10:27 notes 'some worthless fellows' who despised him); and the Gilgal ceremony (11:14-15), following Saul's proven military success, secures broad, enthusiastic national acclamation and formal installation. This kind of staged, multi-stage accession -- private designation, public revelation, and final confirmation following a demonstrated success -- has some parallel elsewhere in the ancient Near East and is the reading nearly all conservative and most critical commentators adopt, even those who otherwise attribute the three episodes to different underlying source traditions later combined by an editor.

### 2. Did Saul already know David -- or not? (1 Samuel 16 vs. 17:55-58)

**References:** 1 Samuel 16:14-23; 1 Samuel 17:55-58

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 16:14-23 has David brought to Saul specifically as a skilled musician to soothe him, with the text stating Saul 'loved him greatly' and made him his armor-bearer, sending a formal request to Jesse for David to remain in his service. Yet in 17:55-58, immediately after David kills Goliath, Saul watches him and asks his commander Abner, 'Whose son is this youth?' -- and after David is brought to him, asks David directly, 'Whose son are you, young man?' -- apparently not recognizing someone the text had just described as his own beloved armor-bearer.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations follow the fuller Masoretic Hebrew text, including 17:55-58, in their main text without omission or a bracketed alternative; none of the ten adopts the shorter Septuagint form some scholars propose as an explanation for the tension. The harmonization is exegetical and text-critical, not a matter of any translation's specific wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the more substantial and long-recognized narrative tensions in 1 Samuel, and conservative and critical scholars alike take it seriously rather than dismissing it. Several harmonizations are offered. First, Saul's question may concern not David's identity as a person, whom he already knew, but specifically David's family and lineage -- information relevant to the reward Saul had promised for defeating Goliath, which included exempting the victor's father's house from taxation (17:25). On this reading, 'whose son is he' is a natural administrative question about parentage, not a claim of not recognizing David's face. Second, some commentators propose that Saul's mental and emotional instability, explicitly attributed throughout this section of the narrative to a tormenting spirit (16:14, 18:10), may account for a genuine lapse in recognizing someone he had met before, particularly if a substantial gap in time separated the events of chapter 16 from chapter 17 and Saul had only briefly employed David as a musician. Third, a minority of textual scholars note that the ancient Greek Septuagint's earliest and shortest form of 1 Samuel actually omits verses 17:55-58 and a substantial portion of chapter 17 entirely. This has led some scholars to propose that the Hebrew (Masoretic) text as we have it combines two originally separate and not fully harmonized source accounts of David's introduction to Saul's court -- a proposal not adopted by any of the ten translations surveyed here, all of which follow the fuller Masoretic Hebrew text in their main text.

### 3. Two rejections of Saul as king (1 Samuel 13 and 15) -- one event told twice, or two distinct failures?

**References:** 1 Samuel 13:8-14; 1 Samuel 15:2, 10-29

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 13:13-14 has Samuel tell Saul that because he did not wait for Samuel and instead offered the burnt offering himself, his kingdom 'shall not continue' and the LORD has sought out 'a man after his own heart' to replace him. Two chapters later, 1 Samuel 15:22-23 and 15:26-28 has Samuel again pronounce Saul's rejection as king, this time over his failure to fully carry out the command to destroy the Amalekites and their livestock -- raising a question of whether these are two literary accounts of a single rejection, or two separate historical incidents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both episodes plainly and consistently as given, preserving their distinct locations, offenses, and outcomes; the harmonization is a matter of narrative and historical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The majority reading treats these as two distinct incidents, separated by time and involving different specific offenses, that together form an escalating pattern rather than a single event told twice: chapter 13's rejection is announced but Saul is not immediately removed, continuing to reign for a substantial further period, which is consistent with 13:14's language being a first, serious warning of an already-determined future outcome (the eventual end of his dynastic line) rather than an immediate deposition; chapter 15's rejection, following a second and different act of disobedience, functions as the decisive, final confirmation of that outcome, marking the point after which Samuel 'did not see Saul again until the day of his death' (15:35) and immediately proceeds to anoint David (chapter 16). Two escalating divine warnings followed by a final, decisive one is a recognized pattern elsewhere in the Old Testament's treatment of persistent covenant unfaithfulness (compare the repeated warnings preceding the fall of the northern and southern kingdoms centuries later), not evidence of a duplicated single event.

### 4. Who killed Goliath -- David, or Elhanan?

**References:** 1 Samuel 17:1-51; 2 Samuel 21:19; 1 Chronicles 20:5

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 17 credits David with killing Goliath the Gittite in single combat. 2 Samuel 21:19 (Masoretic Hebrew, read literally) states that Elhanan killed Goliath the Gittite in a later battle.

**How the ten translations render it:** See the fuller treatment and full ten-translation breakdown in the general document set's entry on this question, since the decisive textual variant occurs at 2 Samuel 21:19 rather than within 1 Samuel 17 itself; 1 Samuel 17's own account of David and Goliath is rendered identically across all ten translations.

**Reconciliation:** This entry is treated at greater length in the general document set covering the wider ten-translation textual-variant project. In brief, 1 Chronicles 20:5, describing the same later battle, states Elhanan killed 'Lahmi, the brother of Goliath,' and most textual scholars conclude 2 Samuel 21:19 suffered a scribal mix-up in transmission (the Hebrew word for 'the brother of' and the accusative particle before a name closely resemble each other), with Chronicles most likely preserving the original sense. The ten translations in this survey genuinely differ on how they handle 2 Samuel 21:19 -- KJV, NKJV, and the current (2011 and later) NIV supply 'the brother of,' harmonizing directly; ESV, NASB, CSB, LSB, AMP, and RSV2CE render the verse literally, leaving the tension unresolved in the text itself (the earlier 1984 NIV printing was also literal, before the 2011 revision added the harmonized reading); NLT translates 'the brother of Goliath' directly.

### 5. How did Saul die -- by his own hand, or an Amalekite's?

**References:** 1 Samuel 31:3-6; 2 Samuel 1:6-10; 2 Samuel 21:12; 1 Chronicles 10:4-5

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 31:3-6 narrates Saul's death directly: mortally wounded by Philistine archers, he asks his armor-bearer to kill him rather than let the enemy capture and abuse him; when the armor-bearer refuses, 'Saul took his own sword and fell on it,' and his armor-bearer, seeing him dead, does the same. Only twenty verses later, in 2 Samuel 1:6-10, an Amalekite arrives at David's camp claiming a very different story: he found Saul still alive, leaning on his spear, and at Saul's own request finished him off and took his crown and armband as proof. 1 Chronicles 10:4-5, retelling the same events, follows 1 Samuel's account exactly and does not mention the Amalekite's claim at all. A third, much briefer note (2 Samuel 21:12) simply attributes Saul's death to 'the Philistines,' without specifying the manner. This is one of the most frequently cited alleged contradictions in the Old Testament, appearing on numerous published lists of Bible difficulties.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders 1 Samuel 31:4, 'Therefore Saul took a sword, and fell upon it,' and the Amalekite's account at 2 Samuel 1:9-10, 'he stood upon me, and slew me... and I stood upon him, and slew him,' with Saul described in verse 6 as 'leaning upon his spear' (verified against the published text). 1 Chronicles 10:4 repeats the 1 Samuel account nearly verbatim, without the Amalekite episode.

**Reconciliation:** This is among the most commonly cited Old Testament 'contradictions' on published skeptical lists. Several harmonizations have been proposed. The most common holds that the Amalekite's story is a fabrication -- he came upon Saul's body (or the dying king) after the fact, invented a story of mercy-killing to ingratiate himself with David as Saul's rival, and was executed by David precisely because his own testimony convicted him of striking down the LORD's anointed (2 Samuel 1:14-16), regardless of whether the claim was true. A second harmonization treats both accounts as compatible in sequence: Saul's armor-bearer fled after refusing the mercy-killing request, Saul then fell on his own sword as 1 Samuel states, and the Amalekite arrived afterward to find him still breathing, delivering the final blow at Saul's own request before the armor-bearer returned, saw Saul dead, and fell on his own sword in turn -- a sequence resembling the explanation offered by the first-century historian Josephus. Under either harmonization, 1 Chronicles 10's silence on the Amalekite's claim is explained by the Chronicler's own selective, theologically-focused editorial practice throughout his history, which regularly omits episodes not central to his purposes; and 2 Samuel 21:12's brief attribution to 'the Philistines' is naturally read as describing the ultimate cause (Saul died in a battle against the Philistines) rather than specifying the immediate agent of the fatal blow.

### 6. 'The Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret' -- stated in the very chapter that twice says the LORD regretted making Saul king

**References:** 1 Samuel 15:11; 1 Samuel 15:29; 1 Samuel 15:35

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 15:11 opens with the LORD telling Samuel, 'I regret that I have made Saul king,' and the same chapter closes with the narrator's own statement that 'the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king over Israel' (15:35). Yet in between, at the chapter's own dramatic turning point, Samuel tells Saul, 'the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret' (15:29) -- an emphatic denial of the very quality the same chapter twice attributes to God only verses earlier and later.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders 15:29 as 'the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent,' using 'repent' where other translations use 'have regret' or 'change his mind' -- the same underlying Hebrew term rendered 'repented' at 15:11 and 15:35 in the KJV as well, preserving the same word-level tension in English that the Hebrew itself contains.

**Reconciliation:** This is a striking case where the apparent tension sits within a single chapter. The standard harmonization -- addressed at greater length for the general form of this question elsewhere in this project (Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 versus Genesis 6:6, Exodus 32:14, and Jonah 3:10) -- distinguishes two different senses of the Hebrew word involved, nacham. 15:29 affirms God's settled, unchanging character, purpose, and integrity: God will not go back on his word or act capriciously and arbitrarily the way a fickle human ruler might, which is the entire point Samuel is making to Saul in context, refusing to let Saul manipulate or delay the consequences of his disobedience. 15:11 and 15:35, by contrast, describe God's genuine, relational grief and altered course of action in response to real human choices within history -- real sorrow over Saul's persistent unfaithfulness and a real change in God's dealings with him, fully compatible with God's character never having been arbitrary or deceptive in the first place. The chapter's own structure, placing 15:29's affirmation of God's unchanging reliability at the exact moment Samuel refuses to let Saul wriggle out of the consequences God had firmly decided upon, is widely read as intentional -- using the same Hebrew root in two different senses to make its rhetorical point sharper, not as an unnoticed inconsistency.

### 7. Was Samuel's father an Ephraimite, or a Levite?

**References:** 1 Samuel 1:1; 1 Chronicles 6:16, 22, 33-38

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 1:1 identifies Samuel's father Elkanah as 'an Ephrathite' of Ramathaim-zophim, in the hill country of Ephraim -- language some read as identifying him by the tribe of Ephraim. 1 Chronicles 6:33-38, tracing Samuel's ancestry, places him squarely within the tribe of Levi, through the Kohathite line.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Ephrathite' here describes Elkanah's place of residence -- the territory of Ephraim -- rather than his tribal descent, a distinction the text itself supports elsewhere (the same term describes Boaz's Bethlehem-adjacent origins in a different, non-tribal sense at Ruth 1:2, and 'Ephrathite' is used regionally rather than tribally in several other Old Testament passages). Since the Levites, per God's own instruction, received no independent tribal land allotment but were instead given specific cities scattered throughout the other tribes' territories (Joshua 21, which explicitly lists Kohathite Levitical cities within Ephraim's territory, 21:20-22), a Levite family residing in the hill country of Ephraim -- as Elkanah's family did -- would naturally and accurately be described as being 'of Ephraim' in a geographic sense while remaining genealogically Levite, exactly as 1 Chronicles 6 records.

### 8. Were the Philistines subdued 'all the days of Samuel,' given their renewed threat only a few chapters later?

**References:** 1 Samuel 7:13-14; 1 Samuel 13:3-5, 19-23

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 7:13 states that after Israel's victory at Ebenezer, 'the Philistines were subdued, and they came no more into the coast of Israel: and the hand of the LORD was against the Philistines all the days of Samuel.' Yet 1 Samuel 13, still within Samuel's lifetime and active ministry, describes a massive renewed Philistine military threat, with garrisons stationed within Israelite territory and an army 'as the sand which is on the sea shore in multitude' (13:5).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization reads 7:13's 'subdued... all the days of Samuel' as describing the decisive check on Philistine dominance and territorial aggression that followed the specific victory at Ebenezer, ending the kind of large-scale Philistine incursion and control that had characterized the earlier period (compare the ark's capture in chapters 4-6). It is not read as a claim that Philistine hostility of every kind ceased permanently for the whole of Samuel's long subsequent ministry, which spanned decades, including the entirety of Saul's reign. 'All the days of Samuel' most plausibly describes the general, ongoing pattern established from that point forward -- no renewed Philistine subjugation of Israel of the earlier kind -- within which specific later flare-ups, campaigns, and battles, like the one narrated in chapter 13, can and do still occur without contradicting the general characterization. A period broadly described as one of 'peace' or 'security' in ordinary historical writing can likewise still contain specific military engagements.

### 9. Who was Samuel's firstborn son -- Joel, or Vashni?

**References:** 1 Samuel 8:2; 1 Chronicles 6:28

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 8:2 names Samuel's sons plainly: 'the name of his firstborn was Joel; and the name of his second, Abiah.' 1 Chronicles 6:28, in the Masoretic Hebrew text as it has come down to us, lists Samuel's sons as 'Vashni' and Abiah, without mentioning Joel at all in the firstborn position.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is widely recognized among textual scholars as a scribal transmission error in 1 Chronicles 6:28 rather than a genuine claim that Samuel had a son named Vashni in place of Joel: the Hebrew phrase vashni ('and the second') is nearly identical in form to a name, and the most probable original reading is that a copyist's eye skipped from 'his firstborn, Joel, and the second' to simply 'and the second, Abiah,' losing Joel's name in the process and leaving the connecting phrase 'and the second' to be mistakenly read and transmitted as though it were itself a proper name. Several modern translations and most textual commentaries accordingly treat 1 Chronicles 6:28 as textually corrupted at this specific point, with 1 Samuel 8:2's fuller, uncorrupted reading (also reflected in the parallel genealogy at 1 Chronicles 6:33, which independently confirms Joel as Samuel's son and Heman's father) preserving the original text.

### 10. Was Kish's father Abiel, or Ner?

**References:** 1 Samuel 9:1; 1 Chronicles 8:33; 1 Chronicles 9:39

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 9:1 names Kish's father (Saul's grandfather) as 'Abiel.' 1 Chronicles 8:33 and 9:39, giving a genealogy of the same family, instead name Kish's father as 'Ner.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with several similar genealogical questions addressed elsewhere in this project, 'father' in these ancestor lists is regularly used loosely for any male ancestor rather than exclusively a direct, one-generation parent -- the most common harmonization holds that Ner was actually Kish's grandfather (with Abiel, as 1 Samuel 9:1 states, being Kish's immediate father and Ner's son), and that 1 Chronicles 8:33/9:39, in the compressed style typical of ancient genealogical summaries, names Kish by reference to his more prominent grandfather Ner rather than repeating every intervening generational link -- consistent with 1 Chronicles 9:36's separate mention of a 'Ner' as a more distant ancestor in the same family line, one generation earlier than Kish's immediate father.

### 11. What was Jonathan's first strike against the Philistines -- at Geba, or at the Michmash pass?

**References:** 1 Samuel 13:3; 1 Samuel 14:1-14

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 13:3 has Jonathan striking 'the garrison of the Philistines that was in Geba,' an action that provokes the larger Philistine mobilization described in the rest of chapter 13. 1 Samuel 14:1-14 then narrates a separate, more detailed episode in which Jonathan and his armor-bearer alone climb up to a Philistine garrison at the Michmash pass and strike down about twenty men -- raising a question, for some readers, about which of these two actions was Jonathan's actual 'first' engagement.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two passages describe two sequential engagements in the same extended campaign rather than two competing accounts of a single event: 13:3 records Jonathan's initial strike on the garrison at Geba, which precipitates the large-scale Philistine buildup and the tense standoff that occupies the remainder of chapter 13; chapter 14 then narrates a distinct, later action -- Jonathan's daring, unauthorized two-man raid on a different, specifically named garrison position at the Michmash pass -- undertaken separately from and considerably later than the initial Geba strike, with its own distinct casualty count (about twenty men, 14:14) reported for this specific engagement. Nothing in the text requires reading these as the same event narrated twice; the campaign narrative simply reports two of Jonathan's actions in sequence.

### 12. Who were Saul's sons -- did 'Ishvi' survive to become king after Saul's death?

**References:** 1 Samuel 14:49; 1 Samuel 31:2; 2 Samuel 2:8-10; 1 Chronicles 8:33; 1 Chronicles 9:39

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 14:49 names Saul's sons as 'Jonathan, and Ishui, and Melchishua.' 1 Samuel 31:2, narrating the battle in which Saul and his sons die, instead names those who fell alongside Saul as 'Jonathan, and Abinadab, and Malchishua' -- 'Ishui' has become 'Abinadab.' 1 Chronicles 8:33 and 9:39 give a fourth name, 'Eshbaal,' alongside Jonathan, Malchishua, and Abinadab. 2 Samuel 2:8-10, meanwhile, records a son named 'Ish-bosheth' surviving Saul's death and briefly reigning over Israel afterward.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard, widely accepted harmonization identifies 'Ishvi' (14:49), 'Eshbaal' (1 Chronicles 8:33; 9:39), and 'Ish-bosheth' (2 Samuel 2:8) as three names for the same son of Saul -- 'Eshbaal' ('man of Baal,' his likely original name or throne name) being deliberately altered to the deliberately unflattering 'Ish-bosheth' ('man of shame') by later scribes uncomfortable preserving the Canaanite deity-name 'Baal' in a Israelite prince's name (a documented scribal practice elsewhere in these same genealogies, e.g. Mephibosheth/Meribbaal), with 'Ishvi' most likely a further variant or corruption of the same underlying name. On this reading, Saul had at least four sons -- Jonathan, Malchishua, Abinadab, and Eshbaal/Ish-bosheth -- of whom the first three died alongside Saul at Gilboa (1 Samuel 31:2, consistent with 'Abinadab' appearing there rather than 'Ishvi,' since they are different sons), while the fourth, Eshbaal/Ish-bosheth, was apparently not present at that battle and survived to be made king over the northern tribes shortly afterward, exactly as 2 Samuel 2 records.

### 13. Did Saul and Samuel destroy all the Amalekites, given their continued presence in the very same book?

**References:** 1 Samuel 15:7-8, 20; 1 Samuel 27:8; 1 Samuel 30:1-20

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 15:7-8 and 15:20 both state that Saul 'utterly destroyed all the people' of Amalek, sparing only King Agag and the best of the livestock. Yet the same book later records the Amalekites as a substantial, active raiding people: David fights them at 27:8, and 1 Samuel 30:1-20 has an Amalekite raiding party burning Ziklag and taking captives, with David subsequently pursuing and defeating a large Amalekite force -- all within the same book, only a few years after their supposed total destruction.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As addressed in the related entry on Haman 'the Agagite' elsewhere in this document, and consistent with the pattern of totalizing conquest language discussed at greater length across this project (regarding similar 'all the people'/'all that breathed' language in Joshua and Numbers), 1 Samuel 15's 'all the people' is best read as describing a decisive, comprehensive victory over the specific Amalekite forces and territory Saul's campaign targeted, in keeping with the ordinary hyperbolic conventions of ancient Near Eastern military narrative, rather than the literal extinction of every Amalekite individual across the entire, widely-dispersed Amalekite people, who inhabited a broad desert region stretching well beyond the specific area of Saul's campaign. The book's own subsequent, unremarked references to ongoing Amalekite raiders (27:8; 30:1-20) are the clearest internal evidence that its own author did not understand 15:7-8's language as a claim of total, permanent extermination, since he continues to narrate the Amalekites as a going concern within the very same book without comment or need for explanation.

### 14. When exactly did Saul become David's enemy?

**References:** 1 Samuel 18:8-9; 1 Samuel 18:28-29

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 18:8-9 has Saul's jealousy of David ignited immediately after the women's victory song comparing Saul's thousands to David's ten thousands, with the text noting 'Saul eyed David from that day and forward.' Yet 18:29 states, seemingly as a fresh development considerably later in the same chapter (after David's marriage to Michal), that 'Saul was yet the more afraid of David; and Saul became David's enemy continually' -- language some read as marking an entirely new starting point for the hostility already described earlier in the same chapter.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The chapter itself narrates a clear, escalating progression rather than two competing claims about a single starting point: Saul's initial jealousy and suspicious watching begins at 18:8-9; it develops into direct, murderous intent by 18:10-11 (Saul hurling a spear at David); continues as fear and strategic maneuvering through 18:12-17 (including Saul's calculated attempt to have David killed by the Philistines via the bride-price arrangement); and 18:29's statement that Saul 'became David's enemy continually' is best read as the narrator's summary conclusion to this entire escalating sequence -- marking the point at which Saul's hostility, having built steadily across the whole chapter, became a settled, permanent disposition rather than the intermittent jealousy of its opening verses. 'Yet the more afraid' (18:29) explicitly signals continuity and intensification of an already-established fear, not a new, unconnected beginning.

## Numbers and Text

### 15. Saul's age and length of reign: a famously incomplete Hebrew text

**References:** 1 Samuel 13:1

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 13:1 follows the Old Testament's standard formula for introducing a king's reign ('X was ___ years old when he began to reign, and he reigned ___ years'). The Masoretic Hebrew text here reads, quite literally, 'Saul was a son of a year when he became king, and he reigned two years over Israel' -- which would mean Saul became king as a one-year-old infant and reigned only two years, both of which are flatly impossible given the rest of 1 Samuel's own narrative (13:2 already has Saul's son Jonathan commanding troops).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads 'Saul was thirty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned forty-two years over Israel,' supplying the same numbers as NASB (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB reads 'Saul was 30 years old when he became king, and he reigned 42 years over Israel,' supplying the same numbers as NASB/NIV/NLT/AMP/LSB (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV leaves both numbers blank in the main text -- 'Saul was [ ] years old when he began to reign, and he reigned [ ] and two years over Israel' -- with a footnote stating the numbers are lacking in the Hebrew (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'Saul reigned one year; and when he had reigned two years over Israel,' an interpretive rendering that avoids the 'years old' formula and its apparent impossibility, but does not represent the normal grammatical sense of the Hebrew phrase (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders identically to the KJV: 'Saul reigned one year; and when he had reigned two years over Israel' (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB supplies 'thirty' and 'forty-two,' with a footnote indicating these numbers are not in the Hebrew text and have been added based on other considerations (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB reads 'Saul was [thirty] years old when he began to reign, and he reigned [forty-]two years over Israel,' with a footnote reading 'As in some mss of the LXX; M.T. omits thirty' -- following NASB's approach exactly (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV supplies 'thirty' and 'forty-two' directly in the main text, with a footnote on the missing Hebrew numbers (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT reads 'Saul was thirty years old when he became king, and he reigned for forty-two years,' with a footnote noting the number 'thirty' follows a few Greek manuscripts since it is missing in the Hebrew, and that the length-of-reign figure is likewise incomplete in the Hebrew (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders this 'Saul was . . . years old when he began to reign; and he reigned . . . and two years over Israel,' leaving both numbers as an untranslated gap marked by ellipses (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most widely acknowledged textual difficulties in the entire Hebrew Bible, and is treated by virtually all textual scholars, of every theological persuasion, as a case where a number (or numbers) has been lost from the text in the course of its transmission -- not a claim that Saul was actually a one-year-old king, which no serious interpreter of any background has ever proposed. Most Septuagint manuscripts omit the verse entirely, which is itself evidence of ancient translators or copyists recognizing the same difficulty rather than a straightforward alternative reading to adopt. There is no scholarly consensus on what the original, lost numbers were; proposals include the historian Josephus's and Acts 13:21's roughly 40-year figure for Saul's total reign, a small number of late Greek manuscripts reading 'thirty' for Saul's age, and other reconstructions -- none commanding a clear majority.

### 16. Michal's bride-price: one hundred Philistine foreskins, or two hundred?

**References:** 1 Samuel 18:25; 1 Samuel 18:27; 2 Samuel 3:14

**The claim:** Saul sets the bride-price for his daughter Michal at one hundred Philistine foreskins (18:25), hoping David will die in the attempt. David and his men go on to kill two hundred Philistines and deliver two hundred foreskins -- double what was required (18:27). Later, when David demands Michal's return from Ish-bosheth, he cites the bride-price he originally paid as 'a hundred foreskins of the Philistines' (2 Samuel 3:14) -- the original required amount, not the larger amount he actually delivered.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render '100' at 18:25 and 2 Samuel 3:14, and '200' at 18:27, exactly and consistently; no translation differences are involved in this discrepancy.

**Reconciliation:** This is not generally treated as a genuine numerical contradiction, since both figures are internally consistent with their own immediate context and neither text claims the other figure is false: 18:25 states the price Saul demanded (100), 18:27 states the larger amount David actually delivered (200, an act of demonstrative over-fulfillment consistent with David's characteristic zeal and desire to remove any doubt about having met the requirement), and 2 Samuel 3:14, years later, has David reference the original contractual price that had been set and agreed to -- the number that mattered legally for establishing the marriage claim -- rather than restating the larger sum he had actually paid. Citing the agreed price rather than the (larger) amount actually rendered is not a discrepancy in the way, for example, two different reports of a single historical death toll would be; it is simply referencing two different, individually accurate figures (the price demanded, and the price paid) for two different rhetorical purposes.

### 17. Was the ark of the covenant kept at Kiriath-jearim for only twenty years, or for decades longer, until David retrieved it?

**References:** 1 Samuel 7:1-2; 2 Samuel 6:1-3

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 7:2 states that the ark remained at Abinadab's house in Kiriath-jearim for 'twenty years,' during which 'all the house of Israel lamented after the LORD.' Yet the ark is not actually moved from that location until David retrieves it in 2 Samuel 6, an event that -- given the length of Saul's reign and the early years of David's own reign that preceded it -- occurred considerably more than twenty years after the ark's arrival at Kiriath-jearim, raising a question about the stated duration.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The text's stated 'twenty years' is most naturally read, in its own immediate context, as describing the specific period leading up to the renewal of Israel's devotion to the LORD under Samuel's leadership narrated in the very next verses (7:3-6, culminating in the victory at Ebenezer, 7:10-14) -- a discrete period this passage is specifically measuring, rather than a claim about the ark's total, uninterrupted stay at Kiriath-jearim across the many additional decades that followed, through the remainder of Samuel's judgeship, the whole of Saul's reign, and the first part of David's. The ark's continued presence at Kiriath-jearim (and later, briefly, at a related location, 2 Samuel 6:3-4's mention of Abinadab's house 'in Gibeah') for a much longer total span before David's retrieval is not itself disputed by any text; 7:2 simply is not making a claim about that longer, later span at all.

### 18. Was David the seventh son of Jesse, or the eighth?

**References:** 1 Samuel 16:10-11; 1 Chronicles 2:13-15

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 16:10-11 has Samuel review seven of Jesse's sons before asking, 'are here all thy children?' and Jesse replying that 'there remaineth yet the youngest' (David) -- making David the eighth son in total. 1 Chronicles 2:13-15, however, lists only seven sons of Jesse, with David named as the seventh.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a recognized minor discrepancy in the Old Testament's genealogical record, and this document does not claim full certainty about its precise resolution, consistent with this project's practice of candor where the evidence does not settle a question completely. The most commonly offered explanation holds that one of Jesse's sons, counted in 1 Samuel's narrative total of eight, died without descendants or otherwise dropped out of the family's later genealogical record before 1 Chronicles was compiled centuries afterward -- ancient genealogical lists, including several others addressed elsewhere in this project, are not infrequently selective, typically preserving lines with continuing significance for inheritance, priesthood, or royal descent rather than functioning as an exhaustive census of every child ever born, which would explain a discrepancy of exactly one between a full narrative headcount and a later, selectively compiled family register.

## Miracles and Interpretation

### 19. The witch of Endor: was it really Samuel's spirit that appeared?

**References:** 1 Samuel 28:3-19

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 28:3 states plainly that 'Samuel had died,' and that Saul himself 'had removed the mediums and necromancers from the land' -- explicit background establishing both Samuel's death and the illegality of necromancy under Saul's own prior policy (consistent with the Law's prohibition, Deuteronomy 18:10-11, Leviticus 20:6). Yet later in the same chapter, Saul consults a medium at Endor, who appears to summon a figure the text repeatedly calls 'Samuel,' who speaks accurately about Saul's impending death and the outcome of the next day's battle (28:11-19) -- raising the question of whether Scripture presents an actual appearance of a deceased prophet's spirit, in apparent tension with the Law's own prohibition of exactly this kind of practice.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 1 Samuel 28:3-19 plainly and consistently as given, without resolving the interpretive question through their wording; the question addressed here is a live matter of theological and exegetical interpretation, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Interpreters have long been divided on this passage, and this entry does not resolve between the positions, since serious, Scripture-affirming commentators hold several views. Some read the text at face value: God, in an extraordinary and non-repeatable act, permitted Samuel's actual spirit to appear and deliver a final, genuine prophetic word to Saul, despite -- or precisely because of -- the illegitimate means Saul used to seek it. This reading is supported by the accuracy of the message given (28:19's prediction of Saul's and his sons' deaths the next day) and by the text's own consistent use of Samuel's name and description without qualification. Others hold that the medium's actual practice was fraudulent trickery or a demonic impersonation permitted to deceive Saul further as part of his judgment, consistent with mediums and necromancy being condemned elsewhere in Scripture as illegitimate and spiritually dangerous regardless of what does or does not actually occur in the process. A variation of this view holds that the medium herself was as startled as anyone by an unexpected, genuine manifestation she had not actually produced through her usual craft; her own reported terror and loud cry at 28:12 is sometimes read as evidence she did not expect what actually happened. The text does not explicitly settle which of these readings is correct, and this ambiguity has been recognized since antiquity.

## Literary Composition

### 20. David spares Saul's life in the cave at En Gedi and again in Saul's camp -- one event told twice, or two separate occasions?

**References:** 1 Samuel 24:1-22; 1 Samuel 26:1-25

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 24 and 26 both narrate David having the clear opportunity to kill the sleeping or vulnerable Saul, David's companions urging him to do so, David refusing out of respect for 'the LORD's anointed,' David instead taking a token (a piece of Saul's robe in ch. 24, Saul's spear and water jug in ch. 26) as proof of his restraint, David subsequently confronting Saul from a safe distance to demonstrate his innocence, and Saul acknowledging David's righteousness and that he will become king -- a striking degree of structural similarity that raises the question of whether these are two accounts of a single event, or two separate incidents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both chapters plainly and consistently as given, preserving their distinct circumstantial details; the question addressed here is a matter of historical and literary interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The majority of both conservative and critical commentators read these as two genuinely distinct historical episodes rather than a duplicated single event, for several reasons: the specific circumstances differ in concrete, non-interchangeable detail (a chance encounter in a cave during a bathroom stop in chapter 24, versus a deliberate nighttime infiltration of Saul's sleeping military camp in chapter 26); the token taken differs (a piece of robe versus a spear and water jug); the specific dialogue exchanged with Saul differs substantially in content, even though both share the common theme of David's restraint; and the pattern of a determined pursuer twice given, and twice declining, a clear opportunity to kill a vulnerable rival is not inherently implausible as a repeated historical pattern, particularly given that both episodes serve David's consistent, repeated public strategy throughout this period of demonstrating his loyalty and refusal to seize the throne by violence. The structural similarity is generally read as reflecting a deliberate literary pairing by the narrator (presenting two proofs of the same character trait) rather than evidence that only one event actually occurred.

### 21. 'Is Saul also among the prophets?' -- the same proverb explained on two separate occasions

**References:** 1 Samuel 10:10-13; 1 Samuel 19:18-24

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 10:10-13 narrates Saul, freshly anointed, falling into prophetic ecstasy among a band of prophets, prompting bystanders to ask, 'Is Saul also among the prophets?' -- introduced as the origin of a popular saying. 1 Samuel 19:18-24 narrates an entirely separate, later episode in which Saul, pursuing David to Ramah, is likewise overcome by the Spirit and prophesies (even stripping off his clothes and lying naked all that day and night), again prompting the identical question, 'Is Saul also among the prophets?' -- raising the question of whether the text is presenting two accounts of a single origin story for this saying, or two genuinely separate incidents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both episodes plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here is a matter of narrative and historical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as two separate historical episodes rather than a duplicated origin story, occurring years apart under very different circumstances. The first, shortly after Saul's anointing, is presented positively as evidence of his new Spirit-empowered status as king. The second, during Saul's murderous pursuit of David, is presented as an act of God's power overriding and humiliating Saul even in the midst of his open rebellion and hostility. The proverb's repetition on a second, strikingly similar occasion is read as the point of the narrative itself: the narrator explicitly notes in chapter 19 that people asking the question already knew of the earlier incident, implying the second episode is presented precisely because it self-consciously echoes and updates the meaning of an already-established saying. This shows how differently the same phenomenon can be read depending on the state of a person's heart, with Saul's own dramatic moral decline between chapters 10 and 19 recontextualizing an outwardly similar experience.

## Theological Tensions

### 22. Was Israel's request for a king a sin, or a legitimate institution the Law anticipated?

**References:** 1 Samuel 8:4-9; 1 Samuel 8:19-22; 1 Samuel 12:16-19; Deuteronomy 17:14-20

**The claim:** When Israel's elders ask Samuel for a king, God tells Samuel, 'they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them' (8:7), and Samuel later has the people confess, under a dramatic sign of thunder and rain, 'we have added to all our sins this evil, to ask for ourselves a king' (12:19) -- language treating the request itself as sinful. Yet Deuteronomy 17:14-20, given centuries earlier, explicitly anticipates and provides regulating law for 'when you... say, I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me,' without condemning the request itself as inherently wrong, and God goes on to establish, bless, and make an everlasting covenant with David's kingship (2 Samuel 7).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render all the relevant passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns distinguishing the legitimacy of the institution from the sinfulness of the people's specific motive in seeking it, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators locate the sin not in the mere existence or structure of monarchy (which Deuteronomy 17 already anticipates as a legitimate future development, provided the king rules under the Law rather than in the pattern of surrounding pagan kings) but specifically in the elders' stated motive and manner of asking: 1 Samuel 8:5 and 8:20 both explicitly frame the request as wanting a king 'like all the nations,' explicitly to 'go out before us and fight our battles' -- language reflecting a desire to be governed and defended the way pagan nations were, rather than trusting the LORD's own direct kingship and provision as Israel's defender, which is the specific attitude of unbelief and rejection the text condemns (compare 1 Samuel 8:8's framing of the request as continuous with Israel's historical pattern of 'forsaking me and serving other gods'). On this reading, the institution of kingship itself is not the problem (God had already legislated for it and would go on to establish and bless it, particularly in David), but the people's specific heart-motive in seeking it at that moment, and in that manner, is what both 1 Samuel 8 and 12 identify as sinful.

### 23. What is the earth set upon -- pillars, or nothing at all?

**References:** 1 Samuel 2:8; Job 26:7

**The claim:** Hannah's song declares that 'the pillars of the earth are the LORD's, and he hath set the world upon them' (1 Samuel 2:8), language some read as a literal claim that the earth rests upon physical pillars. Job 26:7 instead states that God 'hangeth the earth upon nothing.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both texts are poetic literature (a song of praise and a poetic speech within the book of Job, respectively), and 'pillars of the earth' is widely recognized across the poetic and wisdom books as a stock figure of speech for the earth's foundational stability and security under God's sovereign care and power -- comparable to describing a well-run institution as resting on 'solid pillars' without any claim about literal architecture. Job 26:7, in the same broadly poetic idiom but making a different rhetorical point (the wonder and mystery of God's creative power, in a passage otherwise full of vivid poetic cosmic imagery), emphasizes that the earth's stability rests on nothing visible or structurally obvious at all -- if anything, the two texts' shared poetic register argues against reading either one as a technical claim about the planet's literal physical support structure, since the same genre elsewhere freely uses both 'set upon pillars' and 'hung on nothing' without any indication either was meant as literal cosmological description.

### 24. Does God help his people in times of need, given his refusal to hear their cry in 1 Samuel 8:18?

**References:** 1 Samuel 8:18; Psalm 34:17; Psalm 145:18-19

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 8:18 warns Israel that once they have a king like the nations, 'ye shall cry out in that day because of your king which ye shall have chosen you; and the LORD will not hear you in that day.' Elsewhere, Scripture repeatedly affirms that God hears and answers the cry of his people (Psalm 34:17; Psalm 145:18-19).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Samuel 8:18's warning is explicitly tied to a specific, foretold consequence of the people's own insistent, faithless choice -- having been warned in detail of the burdens a king like the nations would impose (8:11-17) and choosing that path anyway against God's own preference and Samuel's counsel, they are told that when they later cry out specifically about the burdens of the very king they insisted on having, God will not intervene to undo that particular, self-chosen consequence at that particular time (a disciplinary withholding tied to one specific complaint about one specific chosen path, comparable to how Scripture elsewhere describes God, in certain circumstances of persistent covenant unfaithfulness, declining to immediately reverse consequences he had clearly warned would follow). This is a targeted statement about a specific future scenario, not a general claim that God is deaf to his people's cries in general or that Psalm 34:17 and 145:18-19's broader promises are false -- Scripture elsewhere shows God continuing to hear, answer, and act on Israel's behalf throughout the monarchy period, including in response to later kings' own prayers.

## Names and Identity

### 25. Could Haman genuinely be called 'the Agagite' centuries after Samuel killed Agag and Saul's campaign against Amalek?

**References:** 1 Samuel 15:8-33; Esther 3:1, 10

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 15 records Saul's campaign against Amalek, in which 'he utterly destroyed all the people' except King Agag, whom Samuel subsequently kills himself (15:33). Esther, set centuries later in the Persian empire, repeatedly identifies the villain Haman as 'the Agagite' (Esther 3:1, 10) -- a title some read as implying Amalekite royal descent from the very line 1 Samuel 15 describes as having been utterly destroyed.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Samuel 15 itself does not claim the entire Amalekite people or royal line was permanently extinguished for all subsequent history. The same book later records ongoing Amalekite raiders as a live, active threat (1 Samuel 27:8; 30:1-20), within David's own lifetime, only years after Saul's campaign. 1 Chronicles 4:43 records a further Amalekite remnant surviving to be defeated centuries afterward, in the days of King Hezekiah. This confirms that 'all the people' in 1 Samuel 15, like similar totalizing conquest language addressed elsewhere in this project, describes a decisive, comprehensive defeat of the forces engaged in that specific campaign, rather than the literal extinction of every Amalekite or every member of Agag's royal line for all time. 'The Agagite' applied to Haman is most commonly read either as indicating genuine descent from a surviving branch of Agag's line -- which 1 Samuel 15's own text leaves room for, given Agag's death occurring after, not during, the main battle, allowing time for family members to have already fled or been elsewhere -- or, per a minority of commentators, as a somewhat later, non-genealogical title associated with hostility to Israel generally. 'Agag' also appears as a recurring throne-name for Amalekite kings elsewhere in the Old Testament; Numbers 24:7 uses 'Agag' generically for 'the king' of Amalek in Balaam's oracle, well before Saul's Agag was born.
