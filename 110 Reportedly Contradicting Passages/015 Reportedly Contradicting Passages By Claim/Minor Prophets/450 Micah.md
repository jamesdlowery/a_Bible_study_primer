# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Micah

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual and
theological questions involving the book of Micah, either entirely internal to the book or in
comparison with Isaiah, Matthew, and the companion documents addressing the shared
ritual-versus-ethics theme across several prophetic books. For each item, this document states
the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this book's most significant entry:** Entry 2 (Micah 5:2 and Matthew 2:6) documents
a genuine and well-attested difference between the Hebrew original ("too little") and
Matthew's Greek citation ("by no means least") -- an inversion in wording addressed directly by
ancient commentators (Jamieson-Fausset-Brown) and consistent with standard grammatical
analysis of the underlying Greek rhetorical figure Matthew employs.

**Verification status:** Entries 2 and 4 (the Micah 5:2/Matthew 2:6 wording difference, and
the "from of old, from ancient days" translation question) were both checked directly against
multiple sources, including academic commentary specifically analyzing the Greek text of
Matthew's citation. The remaining entries are theological and literary questions where all ten
translations render the same underlying text and the harmonization is exegetical rather than a
matter of wording, several of which cross-reference fuller treatments already published
elsewhere in this project.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine interpretive diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. 'They shall beat their swords into plowshares' -- a shared oracle also found in Isaiah

**References:** Micah 4:1-3; Isaiah 2:2-4

**The claim:** Micah 4:1-3 contains a vision of future universal peace nearly identical to Isaiah 2:2-4, addressed at length in this project's companion Isaiah document as an example of shared or borrowed prophetic material between contemporary prophets.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Micah 4:1-3 and Isaiah 2:2-4 nearly identically, consistent with the underlying Hebrew texts; see the companion Isaiah document for the fuller discussion of this shared oracle's literary history.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Isaiah document, which addresses both the question of which prophet originated the material (Isaiah and Micah were contemporaries, both ministering in Judah under overlapping kings per their respective superscriptions, Isaiah 1:1 and Micah 1:1, making mutual awareness of each other's oracles entirely plausible) and Joel's later, deliberate ironic inversion of the same oracle (Joel 3:9-10, 'beat your plowshares into swords').

## Numbers and Text

### 2. 'Too little to be among the clans of Judah' -- yet Matthew quotes it as 'by no means least'

**References:** Micah 5:2; Matthew 2:6

**The claim:** Micah 5:2 describes Bethlehem as 'too little to be among the clans of Judah' -- emphasizing the town's insignificance as the very point that makes its future significance (as the messianic ruler's birthplace) so striking. Matthew 2:6, quoting this verse (as reported speech from the chief priests and scribes) to identify Jesus' birthplace, renders the same clause in strikingly different terms: 'you are by no means least among the rulers of Judah' -- an apparent direct reversal of Micah's own stated point, from emphasizing Bethlehem's smallness to explicitly denying that it is least at all.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Micah 5:2's 'too little' (or a close synonym) and Matthew 2:6's 'by no means least' (or a close synonym) accurately, each reflecting its own source text's actual wording; this genuine difference occurs between the Hebrew Old Testament and the Greek New Testament themselves, not among English translations of either passage, and is addressed at the level of the two original-language texts rather than resolved by any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuine and well-documented difference in wording between the Hebrew original and Matthew's Greek citation, not a matter of translation choice among English versions of either passage (which consistently and accurately render each text's own actual wording). Most commentators read Matthew's version as a deliberate theological reinterpretation rather than a mistaken or careless quotation: the underlying Greek verb Matthew uses is a rhetorical figure (litotes, an emphatic double-negative construction, 'by no means least') that intensifies rather than merely restates Micah's point -- both texts agree that Bethlehem's significance stands in ironic contrast to its ordinary insignificance among Judah's towns, with Micah emphasizing the ordinary smallness that makes the coming ruler's birth there remarkable, and Matthew (writing after the fact, with the Messiah's birth there now an accomplished reality) emphasizing that this very birth has definitively elevated Bethlehem's true significance -- no longer merely 'small' in an ordinary sense, but now, in light of what has actually occurred there, properly reckoned among the most significant places in Judah. On this reading, the two texts describe Bethlehem's status at two different points in this single narrative arc (its ordinary, pre-messianic insignificance, and its now-realized significance following the Messiah's birth there) rather than making strictly incompatible claims about the same fixed fact.

## Theological Tensions

### 3. 'What does the LORD require of you?' -- the classic ethical summary, and the ritual-vs-ethics theme

**References:** Micah 6:6-8

**The claim:** Micah 6:6-8 delivers one of the most famous ethical summaries in the Bible: 'with what shall I come before the LORD... shall I come before him with burnt offerings...? He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?' -- connecting to the same general tension addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (Isaiah 1:11-17, Hosea 6:6, Amos 5:21-24, 1 Samuel 15:22, Jeremiah 7:22-23) regarding whether such prophetic language is compatible with the Torah's own sacrificial legislation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Micah 6:6-8 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same emphatic-priority reading addressed at greater length in the companion documents on Isaiah, Hosea, Amos, 1 Samuel, and Jeremiah, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Isaiah, Hosea, Amos, 1 Samuel, and Jeremiah documents, all pointing to the same resolution: this is best read as the recognized pattern of emphatic Hebrew relative-priority language addressed throughout this project, prioritizing genuine covenant faithfulness -- justice, mercy, and humble relationship with God -- over ritual observance divorced from those realities, rather than a rejection of sacrificial worship as such. Micah 6:6-8's own escalating rhetorical structure (moving from burnt offerings, to thousands of rams, to even child sacrifice, 6:7, each proposal rejected in turn before arriving at the true requirement) is widely read as a deliberate rhetorical device underscoring that no quantity or intensity of ritual substitutes for genuine ethical and relational covenant faithfulness, the same point this project has addressed as the consistent thread running through each of the parallel passages in other prophetic books.

## Theological Interpretation

### 4. 'Whose coming forth is from of old, from ancient days' -- eternal pre-existence, or an ancient royal lineage?

**References:** Micah 5:2

**The claim:** Micah 5:2, describing the coming ruler from Bethlehem, adds that 'his coming forth is from of old, from ancient days' (or, in some translations, 'from everlasting,' or 'from the days of eternity') -- language some read as a clear statement of the Messiah's own eternal, pre-existent divine nature, while others read it more modestly as describing an ancient, venerable royal lineage (tracing back to David, or further to the patriarchs) rather than literal eternal pre-existence.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** Translations of this phrase genuinely range from stronger, more absolute-sounding renderings ('from everlasting,' KJV, NKJV; 'from the days of eternity,' NASB) to more moderate ones ('from ancient days,' ESV; 'from ancient times,' NIV; 'from days long ago,' CSB) -- reflecting the real flexibility of the underlying Hebrew term (olam), which does not by itself require the strongest possible reading; this translation variance affects how directly a given English reader encounters the stronger pre-existence interpretation versus the more modest ancient-lineage interpretation.
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'from days long ago,' among the more moderate renderings.
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'from ancient days,' a more moderate rendering than 'everlasting' (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting,' using 'everlasting,' the strongest available English term (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders 'from everlasting,' matching the KJV (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'from the days of eternity,' a strong rendering close to the KJV tradition (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'His goings forth are from everlasting, from the ancient days,' joining KJV, NKJV, and NASB in the stronger, more absolute-sounding camp rather than the more moderate renderings found in ESV, NIV, and CSB (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'from ancient times,' a more moderate rendering.
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'whose origins are from the distant past, from ancient times,' a moderate rendering.
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders 'whose origin is from of old, from ancient days,' a moderate rendering (verified against the published RSV text) -- and, per a documented account of the ESV's own translation history, the ESV's similar wording at this verse was itself patterned directly on the RSV's here; RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuine question of interpretation with real translation-sensitive stakes, and this entry does not force a single resolution. The underlying Hebrew phrase (mimei olam, roughly 'from the days of antiquity/eternity') uses the same term (olam) addressed at several other points in this project regarding its flexible range between 'a long, indefinite duration' and 'eternity' in the fullest sense (compare the discussion of 'perpetual statutes' in the companion Leviticus document) -- the term does not by itself require the strongest, most absolute reading, and could plausibly describe an ancient royal line stretching back many centuries without necessarily asserting a claim of the ruler's own personal eternal pre-existence before creation. Those favoring the stronger, pre-existence reading note the passage's broader messianic context (identifying a divinely appointed, everlasting ruler whose reign will extend 'to the ends of the earth,' 5:4) as consistent with, and reinforcing, a fuller divine-messianic reading, and some connect this passage to other texts more explicitly addressing the Messiah's eternal nature (John 1:1-2, addressed in this project's discussion of related passages). Those favoring the more modest reading note that the same phrase and its underlying term are used elsewhere in the Old Testament for periods that, while lengthy and venerable, are not strictly eternal in the philosophical sense, and hold that Micah's immediate point is most directly about the Messiah's Davidic ancestry stretching back to ancient times, a point fully compatible with, but not necessarily requiring, the fuller claim of personal eternal pre-existence that later, fuller New Testament revelation makes more explicit.

### 5. 'A man's enemies are the men of his own house' -- quoted by Jesus regarding the division his own coming brings

**References:** Micah 7:5-6; Matthew 10:34-36

**The claim:** Micah 7:5-6 laments the social and familial breakdown of Micah's own corrupt generation: 'put no trust in a neighbor... for the son treats the father with contempt, the daughter rises up against her mother... a man's enemies are the men of his own house.' Jesus quotes this same language directly in Matthew 10:35-36, applying it not to describe social corruption to be lamented, but to describe the division his own coming and message will provoke: 'I have come to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother... and a person's enemies will be those of his own household.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Micah 7:5-6 and Matthew 10:34-36 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing a typological application of established prophetic language to a new situation, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this as a further example of the typological, pattern-based application addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (regarding Hosea 11:1's use in Matthew 2:15, and Joel 2:28-32's use in Acts 2) -- Jesus draws on Micah's vivid description of family division, originally describing the corrupt social breakdown of Micah's own eighth-century audience, as fitting, memorable language for describing a related but distinct phenomenon: the division that allegiance to Jesus himself would provoke within families, some members embracing him and others rejecting him. This is not a claim that Micah originally predicted this specific New Testament phenomenon, but a recognized use of vivid, established prophetic language to describe a new but structurally similar situation -- family division along lines of ultimate loyalty -- addressed further in this project's discussion of Matthew 10:34's similar 'sword, not peace' language in the general document set.

### 6. A book of judgment closing with an extended hymn to mercy

**References:** Micah 7:18-20; Micah 1:2-7; Micah 3:1-12

**The claim:** Micah's opening and central chapters deliver extensive, often severe oracles of judgment against both Israel and Judah for social injustice, corrupt leadership, and idolatry (1:2-7, 3:1-12). The book's closing verses, however, shift to an extended, joyful hymn of confidence in God's mercy: 'Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity... he will again have compassion on us; he will tread our iniquities underfoot' (7:18-20) -- a pattern of judgment giving way to a concluding note of hope addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (regarding Lamentations 3:22-24's similarly placed affirmation amid surrounding lament, and Hosea's and Ecclesiastes' comparable structural patterns).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the book's judgment oracles and its closing hymn plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the book's own deliberate structural movement from judgment to a climactic affirmation of mercy, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the parallel cases addressed in the companion Lamentations and Hosea documents, this is not read as a contradiction but as the book's own deliberate structural and theological arc -- moving from an unflinching, extended indictment of persistent covenant unfaithfulness toward a climactic affirmation that God's own character (his willingness to forgive and restore a repentant people) remains the more fundamental and ultimately more decisive reality than the judgment his people's sin has provoked. Some commentators additionally note a deliberate wordplay bookending the whole book: the prophet's own name, Micah, is itself a shortened form of a Hebrew phrase meaning 'who is like Yahweh?' -- precisely the rhetorical question the book's closing hymn poses directly ('Who is a God like you?', 7:18), suggesting the entire book has been building, across its alternating cycles of judgment and hope, toward this closing, name-echoing affirmation of God's unique, mercy-defined character as its intended final word.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. Universal peace among the nations (4:1-5) alongside continued military conflict with Assyria (5:5-9) -- within the same book

**References:** Micah 4:1-5; Micah 5:5-9

**The claim:** Micah 4:1-5 describes a future era of universal peace, in which the nations stream to Zion, learn God's ways, and beat their swords into plowshares (addressed in entry 1 above). Only one chapter later, the same book describes continued, explicitly military conflict: 'he shall be our peace when the Assyrian comes into our land... they shall shepherd the land of Assyria with the sword' (5:5-6), and a 'remnant of Jacob' compared to a lion 'among the flocks of sheep, which, when it goes through, treads down and tears in pieces' (5:8) -- language of ongoing, active military engagement sitting in some tension with the immediately preceding vision of universal, disarmed peace.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the book's ultimate eschatological vision from its treatment of a nearer-term historical crisis, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this juxtaposition, similar to a comparable pattern addressed in this project's companion Ezekiel and Isaiah documents (regarding judgment oracles followed by promises of restoration and blessing for the same or related nations), as reflecting different stages or aspects of a single unfolding prophetic timeline rather than a genuine contradiction between two competing final visions: Micah 4's vision describes the ultimate, final state of God's kingdom in its complete, eschatological fulfillment (widely read as the same 'latter days' vision shared with Isaiah 2, addressed in entry 1), while Micah 5's material addresses a nearer-term, historical stage -- the immediate threat from Assyria that Micah's own contemporary audience faced, and God's promise of deliverance from that specific, near-term crisis through decisive military action against a hostile invading empire, which does not describe or compete with the final, universal peace of chapter 4's ultimate vision. This is a recognized pattern in Hebrew prophecy, where near-term historical deliverance and ultimate eschatological fulfillment are addressed within the same prophetic collection without requiring either to cancel out the other.
