# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 1 Maccabees

**A note on this document's place in the series:** As with the five prior deuterocanonical
books addressed in this series, 1 Maccabees is included in only one of the ten translations
surveyed: RSV2CE, a Catholic edition. The other nine (AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB,
NIV, NLT) follow the Protestant and Jewish canon, which excludes it. This document notes, for
each entry, that nine of the ten translations do not contain the book at all.

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical
and theological questions involving 1 Maccabees, either entirely internal to the book or in
comparison with Esther and Hebrews. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation.

**A note on this book's distinctive character:** Unlike Tobit and Judith (addressed in their
own companion documents, both widely read as edifying religious fiction), 1 Maccabees is
widely regarded, including by secular historians, as one of the most historically reliable
narratives in the entire deuterocanonical corpus, closely following the Old Testament's own
annalistic historical style and corroborated extensively by Josephus's independent later
work. This document highlights that contrast directly (entry 1) while still addressing the
specific historical difficulties the book does contain (entries 2 and 5).

**Verification status:** Entries 4 and 2 were both checked in detail against multiple sources,
including close attention to the Greek verb tense used in 1 Maccabees 9:27's "prophets
ceased" language, and Catholic Answers'
own published treatment of the same three verses. The remaining entries are historical and
theological questions addressed from standard scholarly positions.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by relevant scholarship, or, where a genuine unresolved historical inaccuracy exists, an
honest account of it. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary text directly.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 1. A book widely regarded, unlike Tobit and Judith, as one of the most historically reliable sources in the ancient world

**References:** 1 Maccabees (entire book)

**The claim:** Unlike Tobit and Judith (addressed in their own companion documents, both widely read as edifying religious fiction due to substantial, acknowledged historical anomalies), 1 Maccabees is widely regarded by historians -- including secular historians with no particular investment in its canonical status -- as one of the most detailed, careful, and generally reliable historical narratives to survive from the ancient Hellenistic period, closely following the annalistic style of the Old Testament's own historical books (Kings and Chronicles). It supplies specific, checkable dates using the Seleucid calendar throughout, and its account of the Maccabean revolt is extensively corroborated by, and served as a primary source for, the first-century Jewish historian Josephus's own later, independent historical work.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 1 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 1 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 1 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the full text of 1 Maccabees as canonical Scripture. The book follows the annalistic style of Kings and Chronicles, using specific Seleucid-calendar dates throughout, and its narrative is independently corroborated by and served as a source for Josephus's later history (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not a point of contradiction but a notable point of contrast this project highlights explicitly, consistent with how this series has treated variation in genre and historical reliability across different deuterocanonical books (Sirach's unusually well-documented authorship being a comparable case addressed in its own companion document). 1 Maccabees' comparative reliability does not mean it is entirely free of specific difficulties (addressed in entries 2 and 5 below), but its overall character -- careful attention to specific dates, named historical figures independently attested in Greek and Roman sources, and a general absence of the miraculous, legendary, or symbolic elements that pervade Tobit and Judith -- has led even scholars skeptical of the deuterocanonical books' canonical status to treat 1 Maccabees as valuable, credible history in its own right, distinct from the more overtly parabolic character of some other deuterocanonical narratives.

### 2. Alexander the Great 'divided his kingdom among them while he was still alive' -- a specific, checkable historical inaccuracy

**References:** 1 Maccabees 1:5-9

**The claim:** 1 Maccabees 1:6 states that as Alexander the Great lay dying, 'he summoned his most honored officers, who had been brought up with him from youth, and divided his kingdom among them while he was still alive.' The historically well-attested account, however, holds that Alexander died in 323 BC without formally or deliberately dividing his empire among his generals -- the division of his territories among the Diadochi ('successors') occurred only after his death, through a prolonged and violent series of conflicts (the Wars of the Diadochi) rather than through any direct, deliberate act of Alexander's own while still living.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 1 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 1 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 1 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (1:6): 'So he summoned his most honored officers, who had been brought up with him from youth, and divided his kingdom among them while he was still alive' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuine, specific historical inaccuracy in an otherwise unusually reliable historical narrative (addressed in entry 1 above), and this entry does not attempt to explain it away. Most historians and commentators read this detail as a simplified, retrospective compression of a considerably messier and more prolonged historical process -- the author, writing more than a century after Alexander's death and primarily concerned with establishing the narrative background for the Seleucid dynasty's own later, more immediately relevant history (Antiochus IV Epiphanes, whose persecution the rest of the book addresses), telescopes the actual, drawn-out Wars of the Diadochi into a brief, tidy summary presenting the outcome (Alexander's territories eventually held by his former generals and their successor dynasties) as though it had been Alexander's own orderly, deliberate arrangement, rather than the product of years of conflict following his death. This kind of historical compression and simplification for narrative economy is a recognized and unremarkable feature of ancient historical writing generally, and does not undermine the book's overall value as a source for the specific, more immediately relevant events of the Maccabean revolt itself, which the book treats with considerably greater precision and detail.

## Theological Interpretation

### 3. God's name almost entirely avoided in favor of 'Heaven' -- a further parallel to Esther's own reticence

**References:** 1 Maccabees 3:18-19; 1 Maccabees 3:50; 1 Maccabees 4:24; 1 Maccabees 4:55

**The claim:** 1 Maccabees almost entirely avoids direct theological language describing God acting visibly in history, consistently substituting indirect terms such as 'Heaven' where a direct reference to God's own action might be expected (compare 3:18-19, 'it is easy for many to be hemmed in by few, for in the sight of Heaven there is no difference between saving by many or by few'; similarly 3:50, 4:24, 4:55) -- a stylistic and theological reticence closely paralleling this project's earlier discussion, in the companion Esther document, of that book's own complete avoidance of any explicit reference to God.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 1 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 1 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 1 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (3:18-19): 'It is easy for many to be hemmed in by few, for in the sight of Heaven there is no difference between saving by many or by few. It is not on the size of the army that victory in battle depends, but strength comes from heaven' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a further instance of the same literary and theological pattern already addressed at length in the companion Esther document. Both books narrate God's people's deliverance through primarily political, military, and human means, without the frequent direct divine speech, miracle, or explicit theological commentary characteristic of many other biblical narratives -- instead trusting the reader to perceive God's providential hand at work through the pattern of events themselves. 1 Maccabees' specific substitution of 'Heaven' for direct reference to God is generally read as reflecting a documented broader tendency in later Second Temple Jewish literature toward increasing reverential circumlocution when referring to God directly, a tendency with some parallel in the rabbinic tradition's own growing reluctance to pronounce the divine name directly. It is not read as evidence of theological indifference or doubt about God's active involvement, which the book's overall narrative arc -- Israel's deliverance from overwhelming odds -- continues to presuppose throughout.

### 4. 'Since the time that prophets ceased to appear' -- a temporary absence, or a permanent, doctrinal cessation of prophecy?

**References:** 1 Maccabees 4:46; 1 Maccabees 9:27; 1 Maccabees 14:41

**The claim:** 1 Maccabees makes three related statements about the absence of prophets in its own historical period: the altar stones defiled by pagan sacrifice are set aside 'until there should come a prophet to tell what to do with them' (4:46); a period of national distress is described as unlike anything 'since the time that prophets ceased to appear among them' (9:27); and Simon Maccabeus is made 'leader and high priest forever, until a trustworthy prophet should arise' (14:41). These verses have been cited, particularly in some Protestant apologetic arguments against the deuterocanonical books' own canonicity, as evidence that all prophecy and divine revelation had definitively and permanently ceased in Israel by this period, which would place the composition of any deuterocanonical book claiming scriptural authority during or after this period in a difficult position.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 1 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 1 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 1 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (4:46): the defiled altar stones were stored 'until there should come a prophet to tell what to do with them'; (9:27): 'there was great distress in Israel, such as had not been since the time that prophets ceased to appear among them'; and (14:41): Simon was made leader and high priest 'for ever, until a trustworthy prophet should arise' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated point, and this entry presents the leading considerations on more than one side rather than resolving the question definitively. Notably, careful readers on multiple sides of the broader canonical debate agree that at least two of the three passages (4:46 and 14:41) actually cut against, rather than support, a claim of permanent cessation -- both explicitly anticipate and await a future prophet's arrival ('until' a prophet comes or arises), which would be a strange thing to say if prophecy had ceased permanently and irreversibly, rather than describing a temporary absence the author expected to end. The remaining verse (9:27) is more ambiguous in isolation, but a detailed linguistic study (noting the same Greek verb used elsewhere in 1 Maccabees for the 'appearing' of Judas Maccabeus himself and of a military detachment, in contexts clearly describing temporary absence rather than permanent nonexistence) argues the verse most naturally describes the temporary non-appearance of a prophet at that specific low point in Israel's history, not a doctrinal claim about the permanent end of prophecy for all future time. Separately, and regardless of how 9:27 is read, 1 Maccabees' own narrative continues to describe events some contemporary readers understood in prophetic or revelatory terms (its companion book, 2 Maccabees, explicitly records Judas Maccabeus receiving a prophetic dream, addressed in the companion 2 Maccabees document), suggesting the author did not understand all forms of divine revelation to have definitively ended even within his own narrative's own timeframe.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 5. Antiochus IV's death -- a comparatively restrained account, compared with 2 Maccabees' more elaborate parallel version

**References:** 1 Maccabees 6:1-16

**The claim:** 1 Maccabees 6:1-16 narrates the death of the persecutor Antiochus IV Epiphanes in relatively restrained terms: he falls ill from grief and shock upon receiving news of his armies' defeats in Judea and the failure of an attempted temple robbery in Persia, and dies of this wasting illness, delivering a deathbed speech acknowledging his own troubles resulted from his mistreatment of Jerusalem (6:8-13). This account differs in several details and in overall tone from 2 Maccabees 9's own parallel account of the same death (addressed at greater length in the companion 2 Maccabees document), which is considerably more elaborate and includes graphic details of physical decay absent from this book's own version.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 1 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 1 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 1 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (6:12-13): 'now I remember the evils I did at Jerusalem... I know that it is because of this that these evils have come upon me; and behold, I am perishing of deep grief in a strange land' (verified against the published text), a comparatively restrained account of Antiochus's death, without 2 Maccabees 9's more elaborate details of physical decay.

**Reconciliation:** This entry is included here primarily to note the existence of a parallel account addressed more fully in the companion 2 Maccabees document, which discusses the relationship between the two versions directly. In brief, most commentators read the two accounts as complementary and compatible in their essential facts (Antiochus's illness and death occurring specifically in connection with his military reversals and his sacrilegious ambitions toward Jerusalem and other temples), while differing considerably in literary elaboration and specific narrative detail -- a pattern of two independent (or semi-independent) historical works treating the same significant event with different levels of detail and rhetorical emphasis, comparable to other cases addressed at various points in this project regarding parallel biblical accounts of the same event narrated with differing emphasis (2 Kings and 2 Chronicles' treatment of several of the same kings' reigns, addressed in their own companion documents).

## Literary Composition

### 6. Mattathias's dying roll call of faithful heroes (2:51-60) -- a pattern with a notable New Testament parallel

**References:** 1 Maccabees 2:49-70; Hebrews 11:1-40

**The claim:** 1 Maccabees 2:51-60 records the dying Mattathias delivering an extended speech to his sons, citing a roll call of earlier biblical figures commended for their faithfulness under trial -- Abraham, Joseph, Phinehas, Joshua, Caleb, David, Elijah, Hananiah/Mishael/Azariah (the three men in the fiery furnace), and Daniel -- as models for his own sons to emulate as they take up armed resistance against Antiochus's persecution.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 1 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 1 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 1 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (2:57-60), part of Mattathias's dying speech: 'David, because he was merciful, inherited the throne of the kingdom for ever. Elijah because of great zeal for the law was taken up into heaven. Hananiah, Azariah, and Mishael believed and were saved from the flame. Daniel because of his innocence was delivered from the mouth of the lions' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable literary and rhetorical pattern with a well-known parallel elsewhere in Scripture -- Hebrews 11's own extended 'roll call of faith,' citing many of the same figures (Abraham, Joseph, and others) as examples of persevering faith under difficult circumstances, follows a very similar literary convention of invoking a catalog of revered ancestral examples to encourage a present audience facing its own trial. Both passages draw on a well-established convention in ancient Jewish exhortatory literature of using a compressed historical review of faithful predecessors as a rhetorical and pedagogical device, rather than representing any tension between the two texts -- Mattathias's speech in 1 Maccabees, composed considerably earlier than Hebrews, may itself represent an example of the same broader genre convention Hebrews' own author later draws on independently, both texts converging on a common tradition of moral exhortation through historical example rather than one directly influencing the other.
