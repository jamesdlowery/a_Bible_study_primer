# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Tobit

**A note on this document's place in the series:** Tobit is the first deuterocanonical
("apocryphal") book addressed in this series. Unlike every prior document, which compared how
ten translations of a book in the shared Protestant/Jewish/Catholic/Orthodox canon render the
same underlying text, Tobit itself is included in only one of the ten translations surveyed in
this project: RSV2CE, a Catholic edition. The other nine (AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB,
NIV, NLT) follow the Protestant and Jewish canon, which excludes Tobit as non-canonical. This
document therefore notes, for each entry, that nine of the ten translations do not contain the
book at all, rather than comparing differing wording of a shared text.

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical
and theological questions involving the book of Tobit, either entirely internal to the book or
in comparison with Deuteronomy, Numbers, Leviticus, Daniel, Matthew, and Luke. For each item,
this document states the claim as it is typically raised and gives the standard scholarly
reconciliation or harmonization, drawing on both Catholic and broader scholarly commentary.

**A note on candor:** Entry 2 addresses genuinely acknowledged historical and chronological
difficulties in the book, including by scholars who affirm its canonical and inspired status;
this entry does not attempt to explain these away as strictly accurate history, instead
presenting the standard scholarly account of the book's likely literary genre (an edifying
religious narrative built around a historical setting, rather than a strict historical
chronicle).

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by relevant scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary text directly.

## Canonical Status

### 1. A book included in one of these ten translations, and absent from the other nine

**References:** Tobit (entire book)

**The claim:** Tobit is not merely rendered differently across the ten translations surveyed in this project -- it is entirely absent from nine of them. Only RSV2CE, a Catholic edition, includes the book at all, since it belongs to the collection of deuterocanonical books the Catholic Church (and, in a related but not always identical form, Eastern Orthodox tradition) recognizes as canonical Scripture, while the Protestant and Jewish canons both exclude it as non-canonical.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Tobit. Tobit is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Tobit in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Tobit is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the full text of Tobit as canonical Scripture, positioned among the historical books following Nehemiah.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a translation variance in the sense addressed elsewhere in this project (where the same underlying text is rendered with different wording), but a genuine difference in which books different Christian and Jewish traditions recognize as belonging to the canon of Scripture at all. Tobit was likely composed in the third or second century BC, probably originally in a Semitic language (Aramaic or Hebrew, fragments of both having been found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran, confirming the book's genuine antiquity and its use within at least some streams of Second Temple Judaism), but it was not included in the Hebrew Masoretic canon that later normative Judaism settled upon, nor in the Protestant Old Testament canon, which historically followed the Hebrew canon's scope rather than the broader collection found in the Greek Septuagint (which does include Tobit, among other books). The Catholic Church affirmed Tobit's canonicity definitively at the Council of Trent (1546), in continuity with its widespread liturgical and devotional use throughout preceding centuries. This is a genuine, longstanding difference in canonical recognition between major Christian traditions, not an error in any single translation's own scope.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 2. A chronology that does not match the known historical sequence of Assyrian and Babylonian kings

**References:** Tobit 1:1-2, 15; Tobit 1:3-5; Tobit 14:11; Tobit 14:15

**The claim:** Tobit's opening verses state he was exiled from the northern kingdom of Israel under the Assyrian king Shalmaneser (1:1-2), and 1:15 describes Sennacherib as Shalmaneser's son and successor -- yet the historically attested sequence has Sargon II, not Shalmaneser, as Sennacherib's actual father, and it was Tiglath-pileser III, not Shalmaneser V, who is credited elsewhere in Scripture (2 Kings 15:29) with the specific deportation of Naphtali that Tobit describes. The book further states Tobit lived to the age of 158 (1:3-5, 14:11), spanning from this exile through the reigns of Sennacherib and Esarhaddon and ultimately to the report of Nineveh's destruction (612 BC) -- and its closing chapter attributes that destruction specifically to 'Nebuchadnezzar and Ahasuerus' (14:15), while the historically attested conquerors of Nineveh were the Babylonian king Nabopolassar (Nebuchadnezzar's father, not Nebuchadnezzar himself) allied with the Median king Cyaxares.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Tobit. Tobit is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Tobit in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Tobit is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders 14:15's conquerors of Nineveh as 'Nebuchadnezzar and Ahasuerus,' following its Greek textual basis; other ancient versions and some modern Catholic translations (such as the New American Bible) render this verse differently, some substituting 'Cyaxares' directly for 'Ahasuerus' to reflect the more probable historical referent.

**Reconciliation:** These historical and chronological difficulties are widely acknowledged, including by many Catholic scholars who affirm the book's canonical and inspired status, and this entry does not attempt to explain them all away as accurate history. The most common scholarly explanation holds that Tobit was composed as an edifying religious narrative -- structurally similar to a novella or wisdom tale built around a historical setting, in the manner of Judith or Esther's own narrative genre -- rather than as a strict historical chronicle. Precise regnal sequences and exact figures were not the author's primary concern, in the same way this project has addressed similar genre questions regarding other biblical narratives elsewhere. Some scholars additionally note that the book otherwise displays genuine, substantial historical knowledge of the actual Assyrian royal succession, correctly sequencing Shalmaneser (omitting Sargon), Sennacherib, and Esarhaddon, and drawing on authentic Assyrian court details associated with the historical figure Ahiqar, mentioned within the narrative itself (1:21-22). This is consistent with a Semitic-language original composed reasonably close to the historical events it describes, with specific errors -- the father-son relationship between Shalmaneser and Sennacherib, and the identity of Nineveh's conquerors -- most likely reflecting either simplification for narrative purposes or the author's own limited access to precise court records generations after the events. 'Ahasuerus' at 14:15 is generally read by scholars as a Hebrew rendering of the Median king's name, most likely Cyaxares, whose own son bore a name the book of Daniel separately renders 'Ahasuerus' as well (Daniel 9:1), suggesting some consistent naming tradition rather than pure invention. 'Nebuchadnezzar' is understood as attributing the conquest to the coming Babylonian power broadly, rather than distinguishing precisely between Nabopolassar and his more famous son.

## Theological Interpretation

### 3. 'What you hate, do not do to anyone' -- the negative Golden Rule, compared with Jesus' positive formulation

**References:** Tobit 4:15; Matthew 7:12; Luke 6:31

**The claim:** Tobit 4:15, part of Tobit's extended deathbed ethical instruction to his son, states the principle later known as the Golden Rule in negative form: 'what you hate, do not do to anyone' (a formulation with parallels elsewhere in ancient Near Eastern and rabbinic ethical teaching, including the saying later attributed to the first-century rabbi Hillel). Jesus states the same general principle in explicitly positive form: 'whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them' (Matthew 7:12; similarly Luke 6:31) -- raising a question of whether this represents a meaningful ethical difference or simply two equivalent statements of the same underlying principle.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Tobit. Tobit is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Tobit in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Tobit is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (4:15): 'And what you hate, do not do to any one' (verified against the published text), the negative formulation of the Golden Rule.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators and ethicists who have studied both formulations conclude there is a genuine, meaningful difference in scope and demand between the negative and positive versions, even though both plainly share the same underlying concern for treating others as one would wish to be treated. The negative formulation ('do not do what you hate') is generally read as primarily prohibitive -- establishing a floor of restraint from harm, satisfied by simply refraining from injuring others -- while Jesus' positive formulation ('do what you would wish done to you') is generally read as considerably more demanding, requiring proactive, positive good done toward others, not merely the avoidance of harm. This distinction is widely discussed in both Jewish and Christian ethical scholarship (including in comparisons of Hillel's own negative formulation, from a similar era, with Jesus' positive one) as reflecting two different, though related and compatible, ethical standards -- a minimal, restraint-based ethic capable of being satisfied through mere passivity, versus an expansive, action-based ethic requiring active benevolence -- rather than as competing or contradictory statements of the same single principle.

### 4. Raphael, 'one of the seven holy angels' -- an expanded angelology beyond Michael and Gabriel

**References:** Tobit 12:15; Daniel 8:16; Daniel 10:13, 21; Daniel 12:1

**The claim:** Near the story's conclusion, the angel who has accompanied Tobias throughout the narrative under the disguised name 'Azarias' reveals his true identity: 'I am Raphael, one of the seven holy angels who present the prayers of the saints and enter into the presence of the glory of the Holy One' (12:15) -- introducing both a named angel (Raphael) beyond the two named angels found in the protocanonical Old Testament (Gabriel and Michael, both named only in Daniel, addressed in the companion Daniel document) and an explicit doctrine of seven specific, especially exalted angels who serve directly before God.

**How the ten translations render it:**

Tobit is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Tobit in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Tobit is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (12:15): 'I am Raphael, one of the seven holy angels, which present the prayers of the saints, and which go in and out before the glory of the Holy One' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not read as a contradiction, but as a further development within the same broader pattern of increasingly specific angelic revelation addressed at several points elsewhere in this project (regarding Daniel's naming of Michael and Gabriel, itself a development beyond earlier books' more general references to 'the angel of the LORD' or an unnamed heavenly host). Whatever view a reader takes of Tobit's own canonical status, this project addresses the angelological content itself as continuous with, and building upon, the same trajectory of angelic specificity found in Daniel -- the concept of seven especially exalted angels serving in God's most immediate presence has some further echo in later Jewish apocalyptic literature and finds a possible parallel in Revelation 8:2's mention of 'the seven angels who stand before God,' a passage some commentators read as reflecting a similar tradition, whether or not directly dependent on Tobit itself.

## Ethical Tensions

### 5. Burning a fish's heart and liver to repel a demon, and its gall to cure blindness -- folk remedy, magic, or divinely sanctioned means?

**References:** Tobit 6:1-8; Tobit 8:1-3; Tobit 11:7-13; Deuteronomy 18:10-11

**The claim:** At the angel Raphael's specific instruction, Tobias catches a large fish and preserves its heart, liver, and gall for later use (6:1-8) -- the heart and liver are later burned to produce a smoke that drives away the demon Asmodeus on Tobias's wedding night (8:1-3), and the gall is applied to cure Tobit's blindness (11:7-13). This raises a question about whether these specific remedies -- involving burning animal organs to affect a supernatural being, and applying a substance to cure an ailment understood as demonically caused -- constitute the kind of occult or magical practice the Torah explicitly prohibits (Deuteronomy 18:10-11, condemning divination, sorcery, and various occult practices).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Tobit. Tobit is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Tobit in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Tobit is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (6:4): 'Cut open the fish and take the heart and liver and gall and put them away safely,' with the heart and liver later burned to repel the demon (8:2-3) and the gall used to cure Tobit's blindness (11:11-13) (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, including those who affirm the book's canonical and inspired status, distinguish this narrative's specific remedies from the prohibited occult practices of Deuteronomy 18 on the basis of their source and mechanism. The remedies are explicitly given by, and their effectiveness explicitly attributed to, a good angel acting under God's own authority and instruction; Raphael's own later self-disclosure (12:6-15) makes clear that his entire mission and its methods were divinely commissioned, rather than being independently discovered or practiced occult techniques drawing on forbidden spiritual power sources. The distinction is one of ultimate source and authorization -- divinely sanctioned angelic instruction, versus autonomous human recourse to condemned occult practices -- not a claim that the physical remedies themselves possessed some neutral, purely natural pharmacological property. Ancient and some modern commentators have proposed various natural properties fish gall or smoke might possess that could contribute to a naturalistic explanation of the healing and the repelling of the demon, though the narrative's own framing presents the outcomes as directly attributable to Raphael's angelic power and God's own providence, not merely natural medicine.

### 6. Burying the dead as a central act of righteousness -- despite the purity concerns elsewhere attached to corpse contact

**References:** Tobit 1:17-20; Tobit 2:3-8; Numbers 19:11-13; Leviticus 21:1-4

**The claim:** Tobit's own righteousness is established early in the narrative largely through his repeated, defiant practice of burying fellow Israelites executed by the Assyrian king, in direct violation of a royal decree forbidding it (1:17-20, 2:3-8) -- a practice the book treats as an unambiguous act of piety, even at serious personal risk. Yet the Torah elsewhere treats contact with a human corpse as a significant source of ceremonial impurity requiring purification (Numbers 19:11-13), and imposes specific, heightened restrictions on priests' contact with the dead precisely to preserve their ritual purity (Leviticus 21:1-4) -- raising a question about how burying the dead can be presented as an unqualified good given these purity concerns.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Tobit. Tobit is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Tobit in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Tobit is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (1:17-20): the narrative establishes Tobit's righteousness partly through his practice of burying the dead in defiance of the king's decree, continuing even after King Sennacherib's edict forbidding it (2:3-8) (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, since the Torah's own purity regulations regarding corpse contact never prohibit burying the dead as such, or treat it as morally wrong -- they establish a system of ritual purification required afterward (Numbers 19's specific seven-day purification process), addressing cultic and ceremonial status rather than moral prohibition. Burying the dead, including caring for the bodies of the poor, the abandoned, and even one's enemies, is affirmed as a positive obligation and act of charity at multiple points elsewhere in the Old Testament (compare Deuteronomy 21:22-23's requirement that even an executed criminal's body not be left exposed overnight), consistent with Tobit's own emphasis rather than contradicting it -- the tension some readers perceive rests on conflating temporary ceremonial impurity (which required purification but was not itself sinful) with moral wrongdoing (which the text nowhere attributes to corpse contact undertaken for the purpose of honorable burial).

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. An angel's sustained disguise and false name throughout most of the narrative

**References:** Tobit 5:4-5, 12; Tobit 12:6-15; Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2

**The claim:** The angel Raphael accompanies Tobias for the great majority of the narrative under an assumed human identity and false name -- 'Azarias, the son of the great Hananiah' (5:12) -- allowing Tobit and Tobias to believe they have hired a trustworthy human kinsman as a traveling companion, and only revealing his true angelic identity and mission at the story's conclusion (12:6-15). This raises the same general question addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding whether deception by God's messengers is compatible with God's own truthfulness (Numbers 23:19, Titus 1:2).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Tobit. Tobit is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Tobit in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Tobit is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (5:12): the angel identifies himself as 'Azarias the son of the great Ananias, one of your relatives,' maintaining this disguise until revealing himself in 12:15 (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish this kind of temporary, protective concealment of one's full identity -- disguise, rather than the assertion of specific false statements intended to actively mislead toward harm -- from active deception of the kind Scripture elsewhere condemns, a distinction with some parallel to other biblical episodes involving angelic or divine figures appearing in ordinary human form without immediately disclosing their full nature (compare the visitors to Abraham in Genesis 18, whose full identity is likewise not immediately announced). Raphael's own assumed name and human cover story serve the narrative's protective and instructive purpose (guiding and testing Tobias without overwhelming him with a premature angelic disclosure) and are revealed truthfully at the appropriate narrative conclusion, rather than being maintained as a permanent deception or used to cause harm -- a pattern read as consistent with, rather than contradicting, the requirement that God's messengers ultimately deal truthfully, since the concealment is temporary, protective, and followed by full and voluntary disclosure of the truth.
