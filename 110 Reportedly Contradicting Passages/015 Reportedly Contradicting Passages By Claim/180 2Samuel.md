# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 Samuel

This document catalogs 23 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of 2
Samuel, either entirely internal to 2 Samuel or between 2 Samuel and a directly related
passage elsewhere (1 Samuel, 1 Chronicles, Deuteronomy, Ezekiel, Numbers, Acts). For each
item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV**
specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording
materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the
standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this book's character:** Unlike some earlier books in this series, a substantial
share of 2 Samuel's classic "contradictions" (entries 1-8) are genuine textual variants
between 2 Samuel and its parallel in 1 Chronicles -- the same category of Masoretic-vs-
alternate-witness numeral and name disputes already documented for 1-2 Kings elsewhere in this
project. Because the specific disputed verse in each of these cases sits in 2 Samuel itself
(not in Chronicles), they are given full, properly-sourced treatment here, reusing wording
that was independently verified against the published texts of all ten translations when
first researched for this project's general document set. The remaining entries (9-12)
address narrative, theological, and ethical questions internal to 2 Samuel's own story.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Who incited David to take the census -- the LORD, or Satan?

**References:** 2 Samuel 24:1; 1 Chronicles 21:1

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 24:1 states the LORD incited David to number Israel; 1 Chronicles 21:1, describing the same event, states Satan incited David to do it.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses as given, without softening either statement; the harmonization is theological/interpretive rather than a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard reading, consistent with a Hebrew theology of divine sovereignty operating through secondary agents (compare Job 1-2, where Satan acts but only within God's permission), is that both are true at different levels of causation: God, in judgment for Israel's sin (2 Samuel 24:1 explicitly ties the census to God's anger 'against Israel' for an unstated prior offense), permitted or directed Satan to tempt David into the census, exactly as God later permits Satan to test Job. Chronicles names the proximate tempter; Samuel names the ultimate, permitting cause -- a pattern, not a contradiction, that appears elsewhere in Scripture.

### 2. Who killed Goliath -- David, or Elhanan?

**References:** 2 Samuel 21:19; 1 Samuel 17:49-51; 1 Chronicles 20:5

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 17 famously credits David with killing Goliath the Gittite. 2 Samuel 21:19, in this book, states (read literally in the Masoretic Hebrew) that Elhanan killed Goliath the Gittite -- the actual verse where this apparent conflict originates.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders literally, without supplying 'brother of' or adding explanatory brackets, leaving the tension with 1 Samuel 17 unresolved in the main text.
- **CSB:** CSB renders literally, without supplying 'brother of,' typically flagging the Chronicles parallel in a footnote.
- **ESV:** ESV renders the verse literally as 'Elhanan...struck down Goliath the Gittite,' leaving the tension with 1 Samuel 17 unresolved in the main text.
- **KJV:** KJV supplies 'the brother of' in italics at this verse, per KJV convention flagging words not literally in the Hebrew but harmonized from Chronicles.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also supplies 'the brother of,' following the same harmonizing convention as the KJV.
- **NASB:** NASB renders literally, 'Elhanan...killed Goliath the Gittite,' without supplying 'brother of.'
- **LSB:** LSB renders literally, tracking NASB's approach.
- **NIV:** Current NIV (2011 revision) reads 'Elhanan son of Jair the Bethlehemite killed the brother of Goliath the Gittite,' supplying 'the brother of' like the KJV/NKJV; the earlier 1984 NIV printing had instead rendered this literally as 'killed Goliath the Gittite,' without 'the brother of' (verified against the published texts of both editions).
- **NLT:** NLT translates 'the brother of Goliath' directly in the main text, effectively adopting the harmonized reading rather than leaving it to a footnote.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders literally, 'Elhanan...slew Goliath the Gittite,' without supplying 'brother of' in the main text.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Chronicles 20:5, describing the same battle, states Elhanan killed 'Lahmi, the brother of Goliath.' Comparing the Hebrew consonants involved, most textual scholars conclude 2 Samuel 21:19 suffered a scribal mix-up in transmission: the word for 'the brother of' (achi) and the direct-object marker before a name (et) resemble each other, and a nearby word for 'Bethlehemite' appears to have absorbed what should have been the name 'Lahmi.' Chronicles most likely preserves the original sense (Elhanan killed Goliath's brother), and this verse's wording became garbled in transmission.

### 3. How did Saul actually die -- and does the Amalekite's story to David change the account?

**References:** 2 Samuel 1:1-16; 1 Samuel 31:1-6; 1 Chronicles 10:1-6

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 31 narrates Saul falling on his own sword after his armor-bearer refuses to kill him. In this book, an Amalekite comes to David claiming he personally finished off the wounded Saul at Saul's own request (2 Samuel 1:6-10) -- an account that, if accepted at face value, would conflict with 1 Samuel's own narration.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the Amalekite's claim here as reported speech (not narrator assertion), consistent with 1 Samuel 31's own account; none of the ten harmonizes the two accounts in the text itself, since the resolution is a matter of narrative interpretation.

**Reconciliation:** The standard and near-universal harmonization treats the Amalekite's account as a fabricated story invented for personal advantage -- hoping for a reward from David for claiming to have killed his rival -- rather than as Scripture's own competing narration of events. The narrator of 1 Samuel 31 never retracts or qualifies his own account, and David's own reaction here in 2 Samuel 1 (having the man executed for claiming to kill 'the LORD's anointed,' 1:14-16) reads as judgment on a self-incriminating false claim, not as David accepting a second, contradictory version of how Saul actually died. The Amalekite's speech is reported speech within the narrative, not narratorial assertion.

### 4. Did Saul's entire family die with him at Gilboa, given that a son of his is made king only verses later?

**References:** 2 Samuel 2:8-10

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 31 narrates Saul and three of his sons dying together at the battle of Gilboa. Only a chapter later, 2 Samuel 2:8-10 has Abner taking 'Ish-bosheth the son of Saul' and making him king over Israel -- raising a question of whether Saul's family line was wiped out with him or not.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is addressed at length in this project's 1 Samuel companion document (see its entry on the identity of Saul's sons), which shows that Ish-bosheth is best identified with 'Ishvi'/'Eshbaal,' a fourth son of Saul distinct from the three (Jonathan, Malchishua, and Abinadab) who died alongside their father at Gilboa per 1 Samuel 31:2 -- not present at that battle, and so available to be made king by Abner shortly afterward, exactly as this passage records. No text claims Saul's entire family died at Gilboa; only the three sons present there are said to have died with him.

### 5. Did Absalom have sons, or was he without an heir?

**References:** 2 Samuel 14:27; 2 Samuel 18:18

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 14:27 states plainly that 'unto Absalom there were born three sons, and one daughter.' Yet 2 Samuel 18:18, describing Absalom setting up a memorial pillar for himself, has Absalom explain, 'I have no son to keep my name in remembrance,' and the text notes the pillar 'is called unto this day, Absalom's place.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that Absalom's three sons, mentioned in 14:27 as having been born to him, evidently died in childhood or young adulthood sometime before the events of chapter 18 -- a span of some years separates the two passages, during which Absalom's rebellion, exile, and restoration to Jerusalem all take place -- leaving him without a surviving son by the time of his death, consistent with his own stated motive for the pillar (ensuring his name would be remembered by some means other than a living son to carry it forward) and with the fact that no son of Absalom appears anywhere else in the narrative or in the later royal genealogies. Scripture elsewhere records children's deaths without separately narrating each one (compare the unnamed sons or daughters of other figures who simply disappear from later mention), so the text's silence about exactly when or how Absalom's sons died is not itself evidence against this straightforward reading.

### 6. What were David's actual last words -- the oracle of 2 Samuel 23, or his later deathbed charge to Solomon?

**References:** 2 Samuel 23:1-7; 1 Kings 2:1-9

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 23:1 introduces a poetic oracle with the heading, 'Now these be the last words of David.' Yet 1 Kings 2:1-9, chronologically later (as David is 'nigh unto death'), records a further, extended set of instructions David gives directly to Solomon -- apparently additional words spoken after the ones 2 Samuel 23 labels as his 'last.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'The last words of David' in 2 Samuel 23:1 functions as a specific literary heading identifying a particular inspired composition -- a final poetic oracle or song, in the same genre as similar 'last words' or concluding oracles attributed to other biblical figures. Compare Jacob's blessing in Genesis 49, or Moses' song in Deuteronomy 32-33, neither of which was understood to be literally the last words the person ever spoke in any context. It is not read as a chronological claim that David never spoke another word after uttering it. 1 Kings 2's deathbed charge to Solomon is a separate kind of material entirely: private, practical, and political instruction for the transition of the kingdom, distinct in genre and purpose from the formal, prophetic-poetic oracle 2 Samuel 23 specifically labels. A public farewell address is, in just the same way, not falsified by a person later giving private instructions to a family member.

## Numbers and Text

### 7. How many fighting men were counted in David's census?

**References:** 2 Samuel 24:9; 1 Chronicles 21:5

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 24:9 gives 800,000 for Israel and 500,000 for Judah; 1 Chronicles 21:5 gives 1,100,000 for Israel and 470,000 for Judah.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations preserve both numbers as given; no translation harmonizes the figures in the main text, since this is treated as a real historical/accounting question rather than a wording issue.

**Reconciliation:** The standard explanations involve different counting bases: Chronicles' Israel total may include an additional 300,000 already under arms as a standing garrison force (mentioned in 1 Chronicles 27), which Samuel's total may exclude, and the Judah figures may reflect whether Judah's own 30,000-man garrison (also 1 Chronicles 27) and/or the tribes of Levi and Benjamin (explicitly excluded from the count per 1 Chronicles 21:6) are folded in or held out of each total. No manuscript tradition changes these numbers; the difference is best explained as two different accounting methods applied to the same underlying census, not a transmission error.

### 8. Cost of the threshing floor: 50 shekels of silver, or 600 shekels of gold?

**References:** 2 Samuel 24:24; 1 Chronicles 21:25

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 24:24 has David pay 50 shekels of silver for the threshing floor and oxen; 1 Chronicles 21:25 has him pay 600 shekels of gold for 'the place.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both figures as given, without harmonizing them in the text; the resolution is interpretive (different purchases), not a matter of translation.

**Reconciliation:** The most widely held harmonization is that these are two separate purchases at different scopes: the 50 shekels of silver in 2 Samuel covers only the threshing floor and the oxen used for the immediate sacrifice, while the 600 shekels of gold in Chronicles covers the entire surrounding plot of land on which the Temple would later be built (per 2 Chronicles 3:1) -- a larger transaction described from a broader, Temple-history perspective.

### 9. Years of famine offered to David: seven, or three?

**References:** 2 Samuel 24:13; 1 Chronicles 21:11-12

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 24:13 (Masoretic Hebrew) offers David seven years of famine as one of three punishment options; 1 Chronicles 21:11-12 offers three years.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads 'three years,' with bracketed notes on the Hebrew 'seven' and the Chronicles parallel.
- **CSB:** CSB reads 'three years' with a footnote on the Hebrew 'seven.'
- **ESV:** ESV reads 'three years' directly in the main text, following the Septuagint and Chronicles, with a footnote on the Hebrew 'seven.'
- **KJV:** KJV reads 'seven years' at 2 Samuel 24:13, following the Masoretic Hebrew as received.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also reads 'seven years,' generally with a footnote pointing to the Septuagint's 'three' and the Chronicles parallel.
- **NASB:** NASB reads 'seven years' with a footnote citing the Septuagint and Chronicles reading of 'three.'
- **LSB:** LSB reads 'seven years' with a footnote on the Septuagint/Chronicles 'three,' tracking NASB.
- **NIV:** NIV reads 'three years' in the main text, harmonized with the Septuagint and Chronicles, footnoting the Hebrew 'seven.'
- **NLT:** NLT reads 'three years,' harmonizing directly.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads 'three years' in the main text, following the same harmonized textual choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Septuagint text of 2 Samuel 24:13 itself reads 'three years,' matching Chronicles, and many textual scholars judge the Masoretic Hebrew's 'seven' here to be a copying error. Most modern translations adopt or note the three-year figure.

### 10. How many did David's champion kill in one encounter -- 800, or 300?

**References:** 2 Samuel 23:8; 1 Chronicles 11:11

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 23:8 (Masoretic Hebrew) credits Jashobeam/Josheb-basshebeth with killing 800 men in one encounter, in the very verse where the figure originates; the parallel list at 1 Chronicles 11:11 gives 300.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV reads '800' at 2 Samuel 23:8, following the Masoretic Hebrew as received.

**Reconciliation:** Most textual scholars regard this as a scribal transmission difference in the Hebrew numeral notation of this verse, with 300 -- the figure independently preserved in Chronicles and reflected in some Greek manuscript witnesses -- generally judged the more likely original figure. This does not affect any doctrinal or historical claim beyond the specific tally.

### 11. How many horsemen did David capture from Hadadezer -- 1,700, or 7,000?

**References:** 2 Samuel 8:4; 1 Chronicles 18:4

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 8:4, in the Masoretic Hebrew text, states David captured 'a thousand chariots, and seven hundred horsemen, and twenty thousand footmen' from Hadadezer's forces. 1 Chronicles 18:4, describing the same battle, instead gives 'a thousand chariots, and seven thousand horsemen, and twenty thousand footmen' -- a tenfold difference in the horsemen figure specifically.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with the closely related numerical variants already documented in this book regarding the census totals and other figures, most textual scholars regard this as a straightforward scribal transmission error affecting a single numeral in the Masoretic tradition of 2 Samuel 8:4, rather than a substantive historical disagreement -- a number of Hebrew manuscripts and the Lucianic recension of the Septuagint read 'seven thousand' at 2 Samuel 8:4 as well, agreeing with Chronicles, which most scholars judge to preserve the more likely original figure. Several modern translations accordingly render 2 Samuel 8:4 with 'seven thousand' or note the Chronicles parallel, treating this as a transmission difficulty of exactly the kind already addressed at length elsewhere in this document rather than a new category of problem.

### 12. How many Syrian soldiers did David kill in the battle described in this book -- 700, or 7,000?

**References:** 2 Samuel 10:18; 1 Chronicles 19:18

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 10:18 states that David 'slew the men of seven hundred chariots of the Syrians, and forty thousand horsemen' in this battle. 1 Chronicles 19:18, describing the same engagement, instead states David killed 'seven thousand men which fought in chariots, and forty thousand footmen' -- the chariot-associated figure differs by a factor of ten, and the second figure's category (horsemen versus footmen) also differs between the two accounts.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated as a further instance of the same kind of numeral and category variation already documented repeatedly in this book's parallel passages in Chronicles -- most textual scholars regard the chariot-associated figure as a scribal numeral discrepancy of the same type addressed elsewhere (whether 700 or 7,000 chariot-linked casualties is the more original figure, with Chronicles' larger number generally judged at least as likely to be original given the scale of forces described earlier in the same battle narrative), while the horsemen/footmen distinction may reflect the two books categorizing the same total casualties by a different descriptive term for the same men, or by two overlapping but not identical groups within Hadadezer's combined force. Neither figure affects the passage's substantive point -- a decisive Israelite victory over a large coalition force -- and no manuscript tradition suggests the underlying historical event itself is in question.

### 13. Did Absalom's rebellion begin forty years into David's reign, or four?

**References:** 2 Samuel 15:7

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 15:7, in the Masoretic Hebrew text as literally rendered, states that Absalom made his request to go to Hebron (where he launched his rebellion) 'after forty years' -- a figure impossible on its face, since David's entire reign lasted only forty years total (2 Samuel 5:4), and Absalom's rebellion is narrated well before David's death.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a widely recognized textual difficulty of the same kind as several others already documented in this project (compare 1 Samuel 13:1's similarly corrupted regnal formula), and is treated by virtually all textual scholars as a numeral that has been altered or corrupted somewhere in the Hebrew text's transmission, not as a claim that Absalom waited an impossible forty years. The main Greek Septuagint tradition, the Syriac Peshitta, and other ancient versions all read 'four years' at this verse rather than forty, a figure fully consistent with the surrounding narrative chronology, and most modern English translations accordingly render this verse as 'four years' or note the textual difficulty explicitly -- with the Hebrew numeral for 'forty' (arba'im) and 'four' (arba) differing by a single element easily lost or duplicated in scribal transmission.

## Names and Identity

### 14. Whose sons were handed over to the Gibeonites -- Michal's or Merab's?

**References:** 2 Samuel 21:8; 1 Samuel 18:19; 1 Samuel 25:44

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 21:8 (Masoretic Hebrew) says Michal, daughter of Saul, bore five sons later handed over to the Gibeonites -- yet 1 Samuel 18:19 says Merab, not Michal, was the one married to Adriel, whose sons these were, and other texts describe Michal as childless.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP reads 'Michal,' but adds bracketed/amplified commentary noting the Merab identification question.
- **CSB:** CSB reads 'Merab' in the main text at 2 Samuel 21:8, following the Merab identification (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV reads 'Merab' in the main text, following the Hebrew manuscripts and the Merab identification, with a footnote on the majority Hebrew reading 'Michal.'
- **KJV:** KJV reads 'Michal' at 2 Samuel 21:8, following the Masoretic Text as received, preserving the tension with 1 Samuel 18:19.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also reads 'Michal,' typically with a footnote referencing the Merab question.
- **NASB:** NASB reads 'Merab' in the main text at 2 Samuel 21:8 (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB reads 'Merab' in the main text at 2 Samuel 21:8, tracking NASB's text-critical choice here (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV reads 'Merab' in the main text with a footnote on the Hebrew 'Michal.'
- **NLT:** NLT reads 'Merab,' harmonizing directly in the main text.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads 'Michal,' following its base text without adopting the Merab emendation in the main text.

**Reconciliation:** Nearly all textual scholars, and most modern translations, judge that a scribal slip substituted 'Michal' for 'Merab' somewhere in the Masoretic transmission of 2 Samuel 21:8 -- an easy error given the two sisters' similar roles in the narrative. Several Hebrew manuscripts, along with some ancient versions, read 'Merab' at this verse, and most modern translations adopt or note that reading.

### 15. Was Ahimelech the son of Abiathar, or Abiathar the son of Ahimelech?

**References:** 2 Samuel 8:17; 1 Samuel 22:20

**The claim:** 1 Samuel 22:20 clearly identifies Abiathar as the son of Ahimelech. 2 Samuel 8:17 (in the Masoretic Hebrew, reflected in some translations) names 'Ahimelech the son of Abiathar' among David's officials -- reversing the relationship, in the very verse where the reversal originates.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV reads 'Ahimelech the son of Abiathar,' following the Masoretic order as received.

**Reconciliation:** Most textual scholars regard 2 Samuel 8:17 as a scribal transposition of the two names (an easy error, since the same two names recur together across this narrative), and note that this Ahimelech was likely Abiathar's son, named after his grandfather per a common ancient naming pattern -- consistent with a parallel list at 1 Chronicles 24:6, which names 'Ahimelech' as a priest in the next generation after Abiathar. Most modern translations either retain the Hebrew order with a footnote, or adjust for clarity.

### 16. Was David's second son named Chileab, or Daniel?

**References:** 2 Samuel 3:2-5; 1 Chronicles 3:1-4

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 3:3 names David's second son, born in Hebron to his wife Abigail, as 'Chileab.' 1 Chronicles 3:1, listing the same sons in the same birth order, instead names the second son 'Daniel.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of several instances in the royal genealogies where an individual is recorded under two different personal names -- a documented and unremarkable practice for the period (compare Solomon/Jedidiah, 2 Samuel 12:24-25, and other double-named figures addressed elsewhere in this project) -- with 'Chileab' and 'Daniel' most likely representing the same son of David and Abigail under two names by which he was known, one perhaps a family or throne name and the other a name emphasizing God's favor ('Daniel' meaning 'God is my judge'). Both lists agree exactly on the son's birth position (second) and mother (Abigail), the two most historically significant details, differing only in which of the son's two personal names each book happens to record.

### 17. Was Amasa's father Ithra the Israelite, or Jether the Ishmaelite?

**References:** 2 Samuel 17:25; 1 Chronicles 2:17

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 17:25, in the Masoretic Hebrew text, names Amasa's father as 'Ithra an Israelite.' 1 Chronicles 2:17, giving the same genealogical information, instead names him 'Jether the Ishmeelite.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both discrepancies here -- the name (Ithra/Jether) and the ethnic designation (Israelite/Ishmeelite) -- are widely treated by textual scholars as reflecting the same underlying individual: 'Ithra' and 'Jether' are recognized variant spellings of the same Hebrew name, and 'Israelite' (Yisre'eli) and 'Ishmeelite' (Yishme'eli) differ in the Hebrew consonantal text by a single letter, making a scribal confusion between the two entirely plausible and, in the judgment of most textual scholars, the more likely explanation than an actual disagreement about Amasa's father's ethnic origin -- with 'Ishmeelite,' preserved in Chronicles and in several ancient versions of 2 Samuel 17:25 itself, generally judged the more likely original reading. Several modern translations render 2 Samuel 17:25 as 'Ishmaelite' directly, or note the Chronicles parallel, treating this the same way as the other name-transmission variants already documented in this book.

### 18. Who did Elhanan kill -- Goliath, or one of Goliath's relatives?

**References:** 2 Samuel 21:19-20; 1 Chronicles 20:5

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 21:19, read literally in the Masoretic Hebrew, credits Elhanan with killing 'Goliath the Gittite,' the same giant 1 Samuel 17 credits David with killing. The following verse, 21:20, describes a separate giant of great stature killed by Jonathan, David's nephew.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question -- concerning verse 19 specifically, which is where the apparent conflict with David's own famous victory actually arises -- is addressed at length elsewhere in this same document (see the entry on who killed Goliath), where 1 Chronicles 20:5's parallel account ('Elhanan... slew Lahmi the brother of Goliath') and the widely recognized scribal transmission error behind 2 Samuel 21:19's wording are discussed in full. Verse 20's account of a different giant, six-fingered and six-toed, killed by Jonathan in a separate engagement in the same chapter's list of Philistine-giant encounters, is not itself in dispute and does not bear on the Goliath question addressed in the preceding verse.

## Theological Tensions

### 19. 'A man after God's own heart' -- alongside adultery, deception, and murder

**References:** 2 Samuel 11:1-27; 2 Samuel 12:1-15; 1 Samuel 13:14; Acts 13:22

**The claim:** David is described, before he even becomes king, as 'a man after [God's] own heart' (1 Samuel 13:14, echoed in Acts 13:22). Yet 2 Samuel 11 narrates David committing adultery with Bathsheba, then arranging the death of her husband Uriah in battle to conceal it -- a sustained, deliberate course of sin involving deception and what amounts to murder, seemingly in tension with the earlier commendation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the commendation and the Bathsheba narrative plainly and without euphemism; the harmonization is a matter of understanding what 'a man after God's own heart' does and does not claim, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that 'a man after God's own heart' (in its original context, 1 Samuel 13:14, contrasting David with Saul specifically regarding obedience to a particular command) has never been read by careful interpreters as a claim of David's moral perfection or sinlessness, but as a description of the fundamental orientation of David's heart toward God -- above all, evidenced not by an absence of serious sin (which 2 Samuel 11-12 makes undeniable and which the text never minimizes or excuses) but by David's response when confronted with his sin: unlike Saul, who characteristically minimized, rationalized, or blamed others for his failures (1 Samuel 13:11-12, 15:13-21), David responds to Nathan's confrontation with immediate, full acknowledgment and repentance ('I have sinned against the LORD,' 2 Samuel 12:13), later expressed at length and without self-justification in Psalm 51. The commendation is understood to describe David's characteristic posture of repentance and covenant loyalty over the course of his life, not a claim that he never sinned seriously -- a reading the text of 2 Samuel itself supports by narrating his sin in unflinching, uncensored detail rather than concealing or downplaying it.

### 20. Did David sin, given his own song's claim to righteousness and blamelessness?

**References:** 2 Samuel 22:21-25

**The claim:** David's song of deliverance declares, 'The LORD rewarded me according to my righteousness... For I have kept the ways of the LORD, and have not wickedly departed from my God... I was also upright before him, and have kept myself from mine iniquity' (22:21-24) -- language some read as an unqualified claim of personal sinlessness, in apparent tension with the Bathsheba and Uriah episode this same book narrates elsewhere (2 Samuel 11-12).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is closely related to, and resolved along the same lines as, this document's entry on David being called 'a man after God's own heart' despite his sin with Bathsheba. In addition to that entry's reasoning, most commentators also note that this specific song (substantially identical to Psalm 18, whose superscription places it in the context of deliverance from Saul and other enemies) is most naturally read as David's declaration of relative innocence and covenant faithfulness specifically in the matter at hand -- his conduct toward Saul and his other pursuing enemies, whom David repeatedly refused to harm despite ample opportunity and provocation (chapters 24 and 26 of 1 Samuel) -- rather than a sweeping, all-encompassing claim to have never sinned in any matter across his entire life, a claim nothing else in Scripture's consistently frank portrayal of David would support. Ancient Near Eastern royal and personal thanksgiving psalms of this genre regularly employ this kind of situational, relative claim to righteousness (innocence of the specific charges or dangers just escaped) without functioning as a general theological statement of sinlessness.

## Law and Ethics

### 21. The death of David and Bathsheba's child (2 Samuel 12) -- a child punished for the father's sin?

**References:** 2 Samuel 12:13-18; Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20

**The claim:** Nathan tells David that although the LORD has 'put away' his sin and he will not die for it, 'the child who is born to you shall die' (12:14) -- and the child does die seven days later (12:18), despite David's fasting and pleading. This appears to place the direct consequence of David's own sin onto an entirely innocent infant, in apparent tension with Deuteronomy 24:16's principle that children shall not be put to death for a parent's sin, and Ezekiel 18:20's explicit statement that 'the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this passage plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing personal moral guilt (which Deuteronomy 24:16 and Ezekiel 18 address) from the temporal consequences of sin working out within a family and community, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators distinguish this passage from the judicial/legal principle Deuteronomy 24:16 and Ezekiel 18 directly address (restraining human courts and affirming that God does not assign personal moral guilt to a child for a parent's crime) from a specific act of divine judgment within a particular historical narrative, in which death itself -- the universal human condition since Genesis 3, affecting infants as much as adults throughout Scripture apart from any question of personal guilt -- is the means by which a specific consequence of David's sin (rather than a personal sentence of guilt pronounced against the child) is worked out. The text itself never states or implies the infant was guilty of anything; the death is presented as part of the severe temporal consequences following from David's own sin (compare 12:10-12's wider description of ongoing family strife to follow, none of which requires or asserts personal guilt on the part of those who suffer from it), a pattern of consequence-bearing within families and communities that the broader Old Testament (including Ezekiel 18 itself, in its wider context) treats as compatible with, rather than a denial of, each individual's ultimate personal accountability before God.

### 22. Uzzah struck dead for touching the Ark -- disproportionate judgment, or a specific, known violation?

**References:** 2 Samuel 6:1-7; Numbers 4:15; Numbers 7:9; 1 Chronicles 15:13-15

**The claim:** As the Ark of the Covenant is being transported to Jerusalem on a new cart, the oxen stumble and Uzzah reaches out to steady the Ark -- an act that, on its face, looks like a reasonable, even pious, attempt to prevent the Ark from falling. God's anger 'broke out against Uzzah,' and he dies on the spot (6:6-7) -- a judgment some read as excessively severe for what appears to be a well-intentioned act.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this episode plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading the episode alongside the Law's specific transport instructions and David's own later acknowledgment of the error, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Law had given explicit, specific instructions for transporting the Ark that this episode violates on two counts, which 1 Chronicles 15:13 later has David himself explicitly acknowledge as the reason for the disaster ('because we did not seek him according to the rule'): first, the Ark was to be carried only by Levites, using poles inserted through its rings, and was never to be touched directly by anyone -- 'the sons of Kohath... shall not touch the holy things, lest they die' (Numbers 4:15) -- yet David's transport uses a new cart (a Philistine-style method, echoing how the Philistines themselves had returned the Ark on a cart in 1 Samuel 6, rather than the divinely prescribed Levitical method) with Uzzah and his brother, not carrying Levites, in physical proximity able to touch it at all. Uzzah's death is generally read by commentators not as a disproportionate punishment for a single spontaneous, well-meaning gesture in isolation, but as the outcome of a whole process of casual, uninstructed handling of an object the Law treated with the utmost seriousness -- a failure of the entire preceding arrangement (for which David himself, not only Uzzah, bears responsibility, and which David is shown to recognize once he investigates the proper procedure before his second, successful attempt to move the Ark in chapter 6's second half and in 1 Chronicles 15).

### 23. How many fighting men were counted in David's census -- 800,000/500,000, or 1,100,000/470,000?

**References:** 2 Samuel 24:9; 1 Chronicles 21:5-6; 1 Chronicles 27:1-15; 2 Chronicles 1:14

**The claim:** 2 Samuel 24:9 reports the results of David's census: 'in Israel there were 800,000 valiant men who drew the sword, and the men of Judah were 500,000.' 1 Chronicles 21:5, describing the same census under the same commander (Joab), gives different totals: 'all Israel was 1,100,000 men who drew the sword, and Judah was 470,000 men.' Both the Israel figure (800,000 vs. 1,100,000) and the Judah figure (500,000 vs. 470,000) differ between the two accounts of the same event.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders 2 Samuel 24:9, 'And there were in Israel eight hundred thousand valiant men that drew the sword; and the men of Judah were five hundred thousand men,' against 1 Chronicles 21:5, 'all they of Israel were a thousand thousand and an hundred thousand men that drew sword: and Judah was four hundred threescore and ten thousand men that drew sword' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This numeric discrepancy is widely addressed by conservative scholarship as a difference in what each figure includes, not a factual error, and 1 Chronicles 21:5-6 itself supplies part of the explanation: it explicitly states that Joab, who found the census abhorrent, 'did not count the Levites and Benjamin among them' -- meaning the two books' totals were compiled on different bases from the start. The standard harmonization further notes that 1 Chronicles 27:1-15 separately describes a standing royal army organized into twelve divisions of 24,000 men each (288,000 total), plus 12,000 men specifically stationed in Jerusalem and nearby cities (2 Chronicles 1:14) -- together accounting for almost exactly the 300,000-man gap between Israel's two figures (800,000 + 300,000 = 1,100,000), on the reasoning that these standing, already-organized troops would not have needed to be separately counted in Joab's field census reported in 2 Samuel, but were added into Chronicles's more comprehensive total. A similar accounting is proposed for Judah's smaller 30,000-man discrepancy, pointing to the standing force of 30,000 mentioned in 2 Samuel 6:1. This project does not treat this specific reconstruction as certainly correct in every detail, since the exact administrative categories involved cannot be verified independently, but notes that the general principle -- that ancient census reports commonly distinguished active, reserve, and standing forces, and that two summaries of the same census could reasonably differ by which categories each one totals -- is a well-established feature of how such counts worked in the ancient Near East, and is explicitly supported by 1 Chronicles's own text rather than merely inferred from outside the passage.
