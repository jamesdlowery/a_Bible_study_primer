# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Deuteronomy

This document catalogs 25 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Deuteronomy, either entirely internal to Deuteronomy, between Deuteronomy and its own earlier
parallel in Exodus, or between Deuteronomy and later narrative books (Judges, 1 Samuel, 1
Kings, Ruth, Ezekiel, Matthew). For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Law and Text

### 1. Two different reasons given for the Sabbath commandment

**References:** Exodus 20:8-11; Deuteronomy 5:12-15

**The claim:** Exodus 20:11 grounds the Sabbath commandment in creation: 'in six days the LORD made heaven and earth... and rested the seventh day; therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day.' Deuteronomy 5:15, restating the same commandment, instead grounds it in the Exodus: 'you shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out from there... therefore the LORD your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both rationales plainly and consistently as given in their respective passages; the harmonization concerns recognizing two complementary grounds for a single command, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is generally read not as competing, mutually exclusive justifications but as two complementary rationales for the same command, each suited to its literary context: Exodus 20, closely following the creation-ordered structure of Genesis 1-2, appeals to the creation pattern God himself established; Deuteronomy 5, part of Moses' second-generation sermon recalling Israel's own history on the plains of Moab, appeals to Israel's specific redemption from slavery as the reason they above all people should grant their own household and servants a day of rest. A single command can rest on more than one true, complementary ground -- rest patterned after God's own creation-rest, and rest granted in gratitude for God's liberating action in history -- without either explanation excluding the other.

### 2. Does the tenth commandment list the neighbor's house or wife first?

**References:** Exodus 20:17; Deuteronomy 5:21

**The claim:** Exodus 20:17 lists the objects of the coveting prohibition in this order: house, wife, servants, ox, donkey. Deuteronomy 5:21, restating the same commandment, lists wife first, then house: 'you shall not covet your neighbor's wife. And you shall not desire your neighbor's house...'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations preserve each passage's own original order (house-first in Exodus 20:17, wife-first in Deuteronomy 5:21) without harmonizing the sequence in the text; the difference is one of list order, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a minor stylistic variation between the two restatements of the Decalogue rather than a substantive conflict -- both lists prohibit coveting the same complete set of a neighbor's household and property, and Hebrew lists of this kind elsewhere in Scripture are not always given in a fixed, invariable order across parallel restatements (compare minor list-order variations in other repeated Old Testament genealogies and catalogs). No item is added to or removed from the prohibition in either version; only the sequence of the same items differs.

### 3. Was the Passover lamb to be roasted, or boiled?

**References:** Exodus 12:9; Deuteronomy 16:7

**The claim:** Exodus 12:9 explicitly instructs that the Passover lamb must not be eaten raw or boiled in water, but roasted over fire. Deuteronomy 16:7 instructs, in some translations, that the offering should be 'boiled' (or 'cooked') and eaten.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'you shall cook and eat it,' the general term (verified against the published text).
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'you are to cook and eat it,' the general term (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'you shall cook it,' using the general term (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders Deuteronomy 16:7 as 'thou shalt roast and eat it,' actually agreeing with Exodus 12:9 rather than conflicting (verified against the published text; carried over from the companion Exodus document's verification pass).
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'you shall roast and eat it,' matching the KJV (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB (1977, 1995, and 2020 editions) renders 'you shall cook and eat it,' the general term (verified against the published text).
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'And you shall cook and eat it in the place which Yahweh your God chooses,' tracking NASB's wording (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'roast it,' matching the specific reading, and does not create a tension with Exodus 12:9 (verified against the published text).
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'roast the lamb,' matching the specific reading (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders this verse 'And you shall boil it and eat it at the place which the LORD your God will choose' (verified against the published RSV text); since the RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves the RSV text with minimal alteration, RSV2CE almost certainly retains 'boil' here as well, making it the one translation among the ten surveyed whose specific wording most directly raises the appearance of tension with Exodus 12:9's explicit prohibition on boiling the Passover lamb.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew verb in Deuteronomy 16:7 (bashal) is a general term for cooking that can include roasting, not exclusively boiling in water -- most modern translations render it with a general term like 'cook' precisely to avoid the false impression of a conflict with Exodus 12:9's specific prohibition on boiling. Additionally, Deuteronomy 16 addresses the broader festival offerings across the week (which included other sacrifices that could be boiled), not only the Passover lamb itself specifically.

## Law and Practice

### 4. A single centralized place of worship, yet legitimate altars used elsewhere in Israel's later history

**References:** Deuteronomy 12:5-14; Judges 6:24-26; 1 Samuel 7:9-10; 1 Kings 18:30-39

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 12 repeatedly and emphatically restricts sacrificial worship to a single site, 'the place that the LORD your God will choose,' explicitly forbidding sacrifice 'at any place that you see' (12:13). Yet later narratives approvingly describe worship and sacrifice at other locations: Gideon builds an altar at Ophrah under direct divine instruction (Judges 6:24-26); Samuel offers sacrifice at Mizpah (1 Samuel 7:9-10); and Elijah, in one of the Old Testament's most celebrated scenes, rebuilds an altar and offers sacrifice on Mount Carmel, with fire from heaven confirming God's approval (1 Kings 18:30-39) -- all prior to, or entirely apart from, the single central sanctuary Deuteronomy 12 describes.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Deuteronomy 12's centralization language and the later narrative accounts plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing the law's anticipated timeframe of full application, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators generally read Deuteronomy 12's centralization law as intended to take full effect once Israel was settled in the land with rest from its enemies and a divinely designated central sanctuary established (a condition the text itself anticipates, 12:9-11, 'when he gives you rest from all your enemies... then to the place that the LORD your God will choose'), which was not fully realized until Solomon's Temple. During the intervening period -- the conquest, the era of the judges, and the early monarchy, when the Ark and tabernacle were themselves unsettled and moved between locations (Shiloh, Nob, Gibeon) -- the biblical text itself treats worship at various altars, particularly under direct divine sanction or prophetic authority (as with Gideon, Samuel, and Elijah, each explicitly commissioned or vindicated by God in the narrative), as compatible with a law whose full, settled-land application had not yet come into force. This does not extend to unauthorized high-place worship the text elsewhere criticizes even kings otherwise called righteous for tolerating (e.g., 1 Kings 15:14, 22:43).

### 5. The law for Israel's king (Deuteronomy 17:14-17) vs. Solomon's own practice

**References:** Deuteronomy 17:14-17; 1 Kings 10:14-11:8

**The claim:** Deuteronomy's law governing a future Israelite king explicitly prohibits the king from acquiring many horses, many wives ('lest his heart turn away'), or excessive silver and gold. 1 Kings 10-11 describes Solomon doing all three at an extraordinary scale -- vast horse-trading operations, 700 wives and 300 concubines explicitly said to have turned his heart away after his foreign wives led him into idolatry (11:1-8), and enormous quantities of gold -- without an immediate prophetic rebuke recorded at the moment each accumulation is first described, though the text does record God's displeasure and its consequences later, in 11:9-13.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the connection between them is a matter of recognizing 1 Kings' own narrative structure and echoing vocabulary, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not generally treated as Scripture contradicting itself but as Scripture directly illustrating, in narrative form, the very failure Deuteronomy's law was written to prevent -- the author of Kings narrates Solomon's accumulations largely without comment through chapter 10, then explicitly ties the consequence (God's anger, the announced division of the kingdom) directly back to the specific violation of exactly this pattern in 11:1-13, using language ('his wives turned away his heart,' 11:3) that directly echoes Deuteronomy 17:17's warning. Far from an oversight, most commentators read 1 Kings 10-11 as a deliberate, structured demonstration of Deuteronomy's king-law being violated in each of its particulars, with consequences following exactly as that law implied they would.

### 6. Ammonites and Moabites excluded from the assembly 'forever' -- yet Ruth the Moabite is welcomed in

**References:** Deuteronomy 23:3-6; Ruth 1:16-17; Ruth 4:13-17; Matthew 1:5

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 23:3 states that 'no Ammonite or Moabite may enter the assembly of the LORD... even to the tenth generation, forever,' citing their historical hostility toward Israel. Yet the book of Ruth presents a Moabite woman, Ruth, not only welcomed into the Israelite community after declaring loyalty to Naomi and to Naomi's God (1:16-17), but married to Boaz, an ancestor of King David (4:13-17), and later listed in Jesus' own genealogy (Matthew 1:5).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Deuteronomy 23:3-6 and the Ruth narrative plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the scope and grammatical/historical particulars of the original exclusion, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Several harmonizations are commonly offered. First, the Deuteronomy 23 exclusion is widely understood by Jewish and Christian interpreters alike to concern formal, corporate covenant status and full participation in Israel's public assembly and worship structures specifically as a nation -- a restriction with an explicitly stated historical rationale tied to the Ammonites' and Moabites' refusal to aid Israel and their hiring of Balaam (23:4). It is not read as an absolute, individual bar against any convert or sojourner from those peoples who personally forsook their native gods and allegiance and joined Israel's covenant, as Ruth explicitly and famously does ('your people shall be my people, and your God my God,' Ruth 1:16). Second, a considerable body of Jewish legal tradition, reflected for example in later rabbinic discussion, held that the exclusion specifically named male Ammonites and Moabites (the Hebrew grammatical forms in Deuteronomy 23:3 are masculine). The reasoning was that the stated historical offense -- failing to meet Israel with bread and water, and hiring Balaam -- was a corporate, male and national failure of hospitality and aggression, not equally attributable to Moabite women. On this reading, Ruth falls outside the restriction's scope entirely on textual and grammatical grounds, rather than requiring an exception to it.

### 7. Were fruit trees in besieged cities to be spared, given that Elisha later instructed Israel's own army to fell every tree in Moab?

**References:** Deuteronomy 20:19-20; 2 Kings 3:19, 25

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 20:19-20 commands that fruit-bearing trees be spared during a siege ('thou shalt not destroy the trees thereof... for the tree of the field is man's life'), permitting only non-fruit trees to be cut for siege works. 2 Kings 3:19, 25, however, records the prophet Elisha instructing the combined Israelite, Judean, and Edomite forces to 'fell every good tree' in their campaign against Moab, which the army then carries out.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Several harmonizations are commonly offered. First, Deuteronomy 20's protection is most directly addressed to Israel's own conduct within the land of its inheritance, governing warfare against the Canaanite nations and cities it was to permanently possess and cultivate -- 20:19 grounds the command in the practical concern that Israel would need those very trees for its own future food supply, 'for thou mayest eat of them.' That rationale does not straightforwardly extend to punitive campaigns against a foreign nation's territory Israel had no intention of inheriting or cultivating, such as Moab. Second, the 2 Kings 3 campaign is explicitly undertaken at a specific prophetic word from Elisha (3:16-19), and some commentators read this as a case where an inspired prophetic instruction for a particular, extraordinary judgment overrides the general peacetime provision -- in the same way other prophetically-directed actions elsewhere in the historical books are treated as authorized exceptions to a general rule rather than violations of it.

### 8. May a eunuch enter the congregation of the LORD?

**References:** Deuteronomy 23:1

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 23:1 excludes anyone 'wounded in the stones, or hath his privy member cut off' from entering 'the congregation of the LORD.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question, and the eunuch's later, explicitly-announced inclusion (Isaiah 56:3-5; Acts 8:26-39), is addressed at length in this project's Leviticus document (see its entry on eunuchs and physical blemishes, which treats Deuteronomy 23:1 together with the related priestly-service restriction in Leviticus 21:17-20), rather than being repeated in full here; in brief, the exclusion is read as describing a specific status under the Mosaic covenant's administration that Isaiah's prophecy explicitly anticipates being widened, a widening the New Testament presents as now realized in Christ.

### 9. For how many generations does the exclusion of an illegitimate child's descendants from the congregation of the LORD last?

**References:** Deuteronomy 23:2

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 23:2 states, 'A bastard shall not enter into the congregation of the LORD; even to his tenth generation shall he not enter into the congregation of the LORD' -- raising a question, for some readers, of whether this describes a literal ten-generation countdown or something else, and how long such an exclusion was intended to last.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This provision uses the same 'tenth generation' language addressed elsewhere in this document regarding the Ammonite and Moabite exclusion in the same chapter (Deuteronomy 23:3), where 'tenth generation' is widely read, consistent with a documented ancient Near Eastern idiom, as a round expression for a very long or effectively indefinite period rather than a literal number of generations to be individually counted and then automatically lifted -- the same chapter's structure (grouping several classes excluded from 'the congregation' together, vv. 1-8) supports reading each exclusion's durational language consistently rather than requiring one class to count out a literal ten generations while a neighboring one does not. As with the related entries on Ammonite/Moabite exclusion and Ruth's inclusion, and on eunuchs' exclusion and later prophetic inclusion, most interpreters read the phrase as marking the seriousness and formality of the restriction under the Mosaic covenant's administration rather than establishing a literal, self-executing expiration clock.

### 10. How should Edomites be treated -- as brothers not to be abhorred, or as a nation marked for judgment?

**References:** Deuteronomy 23:7; Obadiah 1:10-18; Psalm 137:7

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 23:7 instructs Israel, 'Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite; for he is thy brother,' explicitly permitting Edomite descendants to enter Israel's assembly after three generations. Elsewhere, the prophets pronounce severe, sweeping judgment specifically against Edom for its hostility toward Israel (Obadiah 1:10-18's extended oracle against Edom; Psalm 137:7's imprecation, 'Remember, O LORD, the children of Edom... Rase it, rase it, even to the foundation thereof').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two kinds of text address different subjects and different moments: Deuteronomy 23:7 establishes Israel's own peacetime, covenantal posture toward individual Edomites as kin (Edom being descended from Esau, Jacob's brother), instructing Israel specifically not to harbor personal or national contempt toward them as a people -- a standing ethical instruction governing Israel's own attitude. The prophetic oracles against Edom (Obadiah, echoed in Psalm 137) are pronouncements of specific divine judgment provoked by Edom's own later, particular acts of hostility and violence against Judah -- most immediately, per Obadiah's own text, Edom's gloating over and participation in Jerusalem's destruction by Babylon (Obadiah 1:10-14) -- a covenant curse invoked against a specific historical betrayal, in the same pattern Scripture applies to any nation (including Israel itself) that acts unjustly, rather than a reversal of the baseline instruction not to hold Edomites in contempt as a people simply for who their ancestor was.

## Law and Ethics

### 11. Individual responsibility for sin (Deuteronomy 24:16) vs. generational punishment (Exodus 20:5)

**References:** Deuteronomy 24:16; Exodus 20:5; Exodus 34:7; Ezekiel 18:1-4, 20

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 24:16 states plainly, 'Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor shall children be put to death because of their fathers. Each one shall be put to death for his own sin.' Exodus 20:5 and 34:7, however, describe God as 'visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and the fourth generation.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Deuteronomy 24:16 and Exodus 20:5/34:7 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing judicial guilt from relational/generational consequence, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization distinguishes two different spheres. Deuteronomy 24:16 directly addresses judicial and legal practice: Israelite courts are forbidden from executing a child for a parent's crime or vice versa, a real and historically significant restraint on collective punishment compared to surrounding ancient legal codes. Exodus 20:5 and 34:7 instead describe the natural, relational consequences of sin working themselves out across generations within families and communities, in the specific context of a warning against idolatry's corrupting, generation-spanning influence on a household's habits and character. Ezekiel 18 makes this distinction fully explicit centuries later, directly refuting a popular proverb that unfairly blamed the current generation for the sins of a prior one ('The soul who sins shall die... the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father,' 18:20) -- while still affirming that consequences of sin, though not personal guilt, can and do carry forward through families and nations.

### 12. Total destruction commanded for nearby nations, yet differentiated and sometimes peaceful treatment prescribed for others

**References:** Deuteronomy 20:10-18; Deuteronomy 2:4-9; Deuteronomy 2:19

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 20:16-18 commands total destruction ('you shall save alive nothing that breathes') of the nations within the land Israel is to inherit. Yet the same book's warfare instructions (20:10-15) require Israel to first offer terms of peace to cities 'very far' from the land, sparing the population if they surrender, and Deuteronomy 2:4-9 and 2:19 explicitly forbid Israel from provoking war with, or taking any of the land of, Edom, Moab, or Ammon -- nations closely related to Israel by ancestry (through Esau and Lot).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each set of instructions plainly and consistently as given, preserving the text's own explicit distinctions between the named Canaanite nations, distant cities, and Israel's kin-nations; no translation differences are involved in this entry.

**Reconciliation:** Deuteronomy's own text draws these distinctions explicitly rather than leaving them for the reader to reconcile: the total-destruction command in 20:16-18 is expressly limited to 'the cities of these peoples that the LORD your God is giving you as an inheritance' -- the specific Canaanite nations named in the immediately preceding verse (20:17) -- and is explicitly justified within the text by concern over their corrupting religious influence ('that they may not teach you to do according to all their abominable practices,' 20:18), not applied as a universal policy toward every nation Israel might encounter. Cities 'very far' (20:15) and the specifically named kin-nations of Edom, Moab, and Ammon (2:4-9, 19) are treated under entirely different, explicitly stated rules within the same section of the same book -- the text itself, not a later harmonizer, draws the relevant distinctions.

### 13. Interest may be charged to foreigners, but not to fellow Israelites -- a double standard, or a covenant-community ethic?

**References:** Deuteronomy 23:19-20; Exodus 22:25; Leviticus 25:35-37

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 23:19-20 (like Exodus 22:25 and Leviticus 25:35-37) prohibits Israelites from charging interest on loans to a fellow Israelite, while explicitly permitting interest to be charged to a foreigner -- raising the question of whether the law reflects an ethical double standard toward outsiders rather than a single, consistent principle.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render this provision plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization is a matter of historical-economic and covenantal interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this provision not as a general statement about the relative worth of Israelites versus foreigners, but as one specific application of a broader biblical pattern in which the covenant community was bound to a distinct, internally generous economic ethic toward its own members -- functioning less like commercial banking (the presumed context of a loan to a foreign trader or business counterpart, where interest was a standard and mutually expected feature of the arrangement in the ancient Near East) and more like mutual aid within an extended family obligated to care for its own poor and vulnerable (the explicit context Exodus 22:25 and Leviticus 25:35 give for the prohibition, addressed specifically to loans made to 'the poor among you'). The distinction tracks a difference in the nature and purpose of the transaction (charitable aid to a struggling covenant member versus ordinary commercial lending to an outside party) more than a claim that foreigners as such deserved harsher treatment across the board -- the same law elsewhere commands generous, non-exploitative treatment of the foreigner residing among Israel in numerous other respects (e.g., Deuteronomy 24:17-22, Leviticus 19:33-34).

### 14. Divorce permitted by Moses, restricted by Jesus -- and differently, in each Gospel

**References:** Deuteronomy 24:1-4; Matthew 5:31-32; Matthew 19:3-9; Mark 10:2-12; Luke 16:18

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 24:1-4 permits a man to divorce his wife by giving her a certificate of divorce, under a specific set of circumstances; Jesus restricts divorce, but Matthew's version of Jesus' teaching includes an exception 'except for sexual immorality,' while Mark and Luke record Jesus prohibiting divorce and remarriage without stating any exception.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each passage plainly and consistently as given, including Matthew's exception clause and Mark/Luke's unqualified statement; the harmonization is exegetical, not translation-based.

**Reconciliation:** Regarding Moses and Jesus: Jesus himself explains the tension directly in the text (Matthew 19:8/Mark 10:5) -- Moses permitted divorce 'because of your hardness of heart,' a concession to a fallen situation and a regulation of an existing practice (Deuteronomy 24:1-4 itself is framed as addressing what happens *if* a man divorces, rather than commanding that he do so), not God's creational ideal, which Jesus restores by pointing back to Genesis 2. Regarding the exception clause appearing only in Matthew: most conservative harmonists hold that Mark and Luke simply give the general rule (the normal, overwhelming case) while Matthew, writing to a more Jewish-Christian audience concerned with the applicable grounds under Jewish law, records the same teaching with its implicit exception made explicit -- an example of one Gospel supplying detail the others summarize.

## Authorship and Composition

### 15. Moses' own death and burial, narrated in the book traditionally attributed to him

**References:** Deuteronomy 34:1-8; Deuteronomy 34:10-12

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 34 narrates Moses' death, God's own burial of him 'in the land of Moab... but no man knows the place of his burial to this day,' the thirty days of mourning that followed, and a concluding summary evaluation of Moses' unique prophetic standing -- all written in the third person, about events after Moses' own death, in a book traditionally held (and internally claiming, at several points, e.g., Deuteronomy 31:9, 31:24) to have been substantially written by Moses himself.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Deuteronomy 34 plainly and consistently as given, without adding any editorial comment on authorship in the biblical text itself; the question is one of composition history, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The longstanding and still widely held explanation, found as early as certain rabbinic traditions and affirmed by many conservative scholars today, is that Moses wrote the great bulk of Deuteronomy himself, with the final chapter's account of his death and burial appended after the fact by a later hand -- most commonly identified with Joshua, his immediate successor and the one closest to these events, a role some later Jewish tradition explicitly assigns to him. This is treated as analogous to inspired appendices or later editorial notices elsewhere in the Old Testament (compare the notice of Joshua's own death appended at the end of the book of Joshua, or the concluding notice of Job's later life and death at the end of that book) rather than as a claim undermining Mosaic authorship of the substantial legal and narrative content that precedes it. A minority of scholars, working from source-critical models of the Pentateuch's composition, instead attribute Deuteronomy's final form to a later compilation process spanning centuries; this remains a genuine matter of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than a settled point all conservative or all critical scholars agree on.

### 16. Do this book's own parenthetical asides about the conquest's outcome fit Mosaic authorship before that conquest occurred?

**References:** Deuteronomy 2:10-12, 20-23; Deuteronomy 2:37

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 2, recounting Israel's approach to the promised land, includes several parenthetical, past-tense editorial notes about earlier peoples (the Emim, the Horites, the Zamzummims) and states that the descendants of Esau 'destroyed the Horites... and dwelt in their stead; as Israel did unto the land of his possession, which the LORD gave unto them' (2:12) -- comparing Edom's earlier conquest to Israel's own, in a way some read as presupposing that Israel's conquest of Canaan was already a completed, past event at the time of writing, which would be impossible for Moses himself to have written, since he died before it occurred (Deuteronomy 34).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry raises the same underlying authorship question addressed at greater length elsewhere in this document (see the entry on Deuteronomy 34's account of Moses' own death), and the same range of positions applies: many conservative scholars read 2:12's comparison as a later editorial gloss -- of exactly the same kind widely recognized in 34:1-8's account of Moses' death and burial -- inserted by Joshua or another inspired hand shortly after the conquest to clarify the comparison for its original audience, without this affecting Mosaic authorship of the substantial legal and narrative material that makes up the great bulk of the book. Others note that the verse need not refer to the full Canaan conquest at all: 'as Israel did unto the land of his possession' could describe Israel's already-completed conquest of Transjordan territories (Sihon and Og, narrated in this same chapter and the next, which did occur within Moses' lifetime and under his own leadership) rather than the yet-future conquest west of the Jordan under Joshua, in which case no post-Mosaic vantage point is required at all.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 17. Moses 'known face to face' (Deuteronomy 34:10) -- yet 'no one may see my face and live' (Exodus 33:20)

**References:** Deuteronomy 34:10; Exodus 33:11; Exodus 33:20

**The claim:** Deuteronomy's closing summary states, 'there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face' -- a striking claim of intimacy, given Exodus 33:20's statement that no one can see God's face and live.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Deuteronomy 34:10's 'face to face' and Exodus 33:20's 'cannot see my face' plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same distinction addressed for Exodus 33 in the companion document, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As addressed more fully in the companion Exodus document (which treats Exodus 33:11 and 33:20 together), the standard harmonization distinguishes an intimate, direct, and uniquely privileged mode of communication and relationship ('face to face,' describing the unusual closeness and directness of God's speech to Moses compared to every other prophet, exactly as Numbers 12:6-8 explicitly states in so many words: 'with him I speak mouth to mouth, clearly, and not in riddles') from a full, unmediated visual apprehension of God's unveiled essential glory, which Exodus 33:18-23 describes as fatal for any mortal and which even Moses was not permitted to see (being shown God's 'back' but not his 'face' at that specific moment, Exodus 33:23). Deuteronomy 34:10's 'face to face' language draws on and echoes the same idiom of unparalleled directness and intimacy used already within Exodus itself, not a claim that Moses was granted the very sight of God's essence that Exodus 33:20 rules out.

### 18. Did Israel see God directly at Horeb, or hear only a voice with no visible form?

**References:** Deuteronomy 4:12, 15; Deuteronomy 5:4

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 4:12 states that at Horeb, 'ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude; only ye heard a voice,' and 4:15 repeats, 'ye saw no manner of similitude.' Only one chapter later, Deuteronomy 5:4 states, 'The LORD talked with you face to face in the mount out of the midst of the fire' -- an apparently more direct, visual claim within the very same book.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization, consistent with how 'face to face' functions elsewhere in this document (see the entry on Deuteronomy 34:10's use of the same idiom regarding Moses), reads 5:4's 'face to face' as describing the directness and unmediated nature of God's spoken communication with the assembled people at Sinai -- an audible voice coming from the fire, addressed to the whole nation without a human intermediary in that specific moment -- rather than a visual sighting of God's form, which 4:12 and 4:15 explicitly and repeatedly deny occurred ('ye saw no similitude'). Read together, the same chapter sequence is describing one consistent event from two angles: an unmediated, direct verbal encounter (hence 'face to face,' an idiom of directness) without any accompanying visible form (hence 'no similitude'), not two competing claims about whether God was seen.

## Theological Tensions

### 19. Will the earth last forever, or will it pass away?

**References:** Deuteronomy 4:40; 2 Peter 3:10; Revelation 21:1

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 4:40 promises that obedience will bring blessing 'upon the earth, which the LORD thy God giveth thee, for ever.' 2 Peter 3:10 states that 'the heavens shall pass away with a great noise... the earth also and the works that are therein shall be burned up,' and Revelation 21:1 describes 'a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** As with other uses of 'forever' (Hebrew olam) addressed elsewhere in this project (see the Leviticus document's treatment of 'perpetual'/'everlasting' language), the term regularly describes a long, enduring, or era-bounded duration determined by context, rather than strict, unending eternity in an absolute metaphysical sense. Deuteronomy 4:40's promise concerns Israel's secure possession and enjoyment of the land 'forever' within the framework of its own covenant relationship with God -- a promise about the land's role in Israel's ongoing national life, not a separate metaphysical claim about the planet's ultimate, unending physical duration in its present form. 2 Peter 3 and Revelation 21 describe a later, climactic renewal or transformation of the created order, widely understood by conservative theologians as continuity through transformation and purification rather than the annihilation of the earth as such (compare Romans 8:19-22's picture of creation being 'delivered from the bondage of corruption,' not erased). Even the New Testament's language of the 'first earth' passing away is therefore compatible with a renewed, continuous earth persisting 'forever' in the fuller sense Deuteronomy 4:40 anticipates.

### 20. Does God keep covenant with his people 'to a thousand generations,' or has that covenant been set aside in Christ?

**References:** Deuteronomy 7:9; Hebrews 8:13; Galatians 3:23-25

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 7:9 describes God as one 'which keepeth covenant and mercy with them that love him and keep his commandments to a thousand generations' -- language suggesting a covenant of essentially permanent, ongoing force. The New Testament, however, describes the Mosaic covenant specifically as having 'decayed and waxeth old' and being 'ready to vanish away' (Hebrews 8:13), with the law functioning as a temporary 'schoolmaster' until the coming of Christ (Galatians 3:23-25).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry raises the same 'perpetual'/'everlasting' language question addressed at length in this project's Leviticus document (see its entry on the priesthood and sacrifices' perpetual language against Hebrews), and the same resolution applies: Deuteronomy 7:9's 'thousand generations' describes God's own faithfulness and steadfast covenant love as a durable, dependable character trait -- a description of God's own reliability that remains true regardless of which specific covenant administration is presently in force -- rather than a claim that the particular legal and ceremonial provisions of the Mosaic covenant given at Sinai could never be fulfilled, completed, or succeeded by a further covenant. Hebrews' argument is that God's own faithfulness (exactly what Deuteronomy 7:9 celebrates) is precisely what is being kept and fulfilled in the new covenant's arrival, not contradicted by it -- the New Testament writers consistently present the new covenant as God keeping his ancient promises even more fully, not abandoning his faithfulness.

### 21. Did God need to test Israel in the wilderness in order to find out what was in their hearts?

**References:** Deuteronomy 8:2; Psalm 139:1-4; Jeremiah 17:10

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 8:2 states that God led Israel through the wilderness 'to humble thee, and to prove thee, to know what was in thine heart, whether thou wouldest keep his commandments, or no' -- language read by some as implying God lacked prior knowledge of Israel's heart and needed the wilderness experience to discover it. Elsewhere, Scripture describes God's knowledge of the human heart as already total and complete, prior to any test (Psalm 139:1-4; Jeremiah 17:10, 'I the LORD search the heart... to give every man according to his ways').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew verb translated 'to know' here (yada) is regularly used elsewhere in Scripture, as throughout ancient Near Eastern legal and covenantal language generally, for a demonstrated, experiential, or publicly-manifested knowledge rather than the acquisition of previously unavailable information -- the wilderness testing served to make manifest, to Israel itself and to any observer, what was already fully known to God, functioning didactically for Israel's own self-understanding and covenant formation (compare 8:3's stated purpose, 'that he might make thee know that man doth not live by bread only') rather than functioning as an information-gathering exercise for God. This pattern -- God 'testing' someone in a way that reveals rather than informs -- appears elsewhere in Scripture in comparable language (e.g., Genesis 22:12's 'now I know that thou fearest God' regarding Abraham, a text most conservative commentators read the same way), consistent with God's comprehensive prior knowledge affirmed throughout the rest of Scripture.

### 22. Are all people God's children, or only some?

**References:** Deuteronomy 14:1; John 8:44; Ephesians 2:2-3; 1 John 3:10

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 14:1 declares to Israel, 'Ye are the children of the LORD your God.' Elsewhere, Scripture distinguishes between those who are and are not God's children: Jesus tells certain religious opponents, 'ye are of your father the devil' (John 8:44), Paul describes unbelievers generally as 'by nature the children of wrath' (Ephesians 2:2-3), and John states plainly that 'the children of God' and 'the children of the devil' are distinguishable by their conduct (1 John 3:10).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Scripture is widely recognized to use 'children of God' in at least two distinct senses, and the immediate context of each passage indicates which is meant: Deuteronomy 14:1 addresses Israel's unique national and covenantal relationship to God as his specially chosen and set-apart people (paralleling similar language elsewhere describing Israel corporately as God's 'son,' e.g. Exodus 4:22, Hosea 11:1) -- a relationship of election and calling rather than a claim about every individual Israelite's personal spiritual regeneration or standing. John, Paul, and Jesus in the passages cited are instead addressing a narrower, more personal and spiritual sense of divine sonship -- becoming God's child through faith and spiritual rebirth (John 1:12-13) -- a status the New Testament describes as available to, but not automatically possessed by, every person regardless of national identity. The two senses (corporate/covenantal calling, and personal/spiritual regeneration) operate at different levels and are not in tension once distinguished.

### 23. Does Paul's citation of this verse mean obedience to the law brings a curse?

**References:** Deuteronomy 27:26; Galatians 3:10-13

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 27:26 pronounces, 'Cursed be he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them.' Paul cites this very verse in Galatians 3:10 to argue that 'as many as are of the works of the law are under the curse' -- an argument some read as claiming that obedience to God's own law somehow results in a curse, an apparently strange, self-undermining outcome for a law given by God himself.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Paul's argument, read in its full context (Galatians 3:10-13), does not claim the law itself is bad or that obedience as such is cursed; rather, he argues from Deuteronomy 27:26's own explicit standard -- confirming 'all' the words of the law, without exception -- that no one, in practice, actually achieves the complete, perfect obedience the verse itself requires (a premise consistent with Paul's broader teaching elsewhere that 'all have sinned,' Romans 3:23), so that anyone seeking to be justified on the basis of law-keeping alone inevitably falls under the curse the law itself pronounces on incomplete obedience, not because the law is flawed but because human performance of it is. Paul's solution, given in the same passage (Galatians 3:13), is that 'Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us' -- the argument's entire point is that the curse Deuteronomy 27:26 describes is real and is addressed, not evaded, through Christ's substitutionary fulfillment, rather than Paul disputing that Deuteronomy 27:26 means what it plainly says.

### 24. Does God take pleasure in the death of the wicked, or does their destruction bring him no pleasure at all?

**References:** Deuteronomy 28:63; Ezekiel 33:11

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 28:63 warns that, in response to persistent covenant unfaithfulness, 'the LORD will rejoice over you to destroy you, and to bring you to nought.' Ezekiel 33:11 states directly, 'I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live... turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization distinguishes God's settled disposition and ultimate desire toward sinners in general (which Ezekiel 33:11 explicitly and directly addresses -- God's preferred outcome is always repentance and life, and this text is best read as God's own stated general disposition toward the wicked) from the specific, judicial satisfaction of justice being rightly carried out when persistent, willful covenant-breaking finally receives its warned consequence, described in the vivid, anthropomorphic language of Deuteronomy 28:63 (comparable to how a judge can take a righteous satisfaction in justice being done even while taking no pleasure in the wrongdoer's suffering as such, or preferring that the crime had never been committed at all). Deuteronomy 28's broader context is a long, escalating series of covenant warnings whose entire purpose -- like the appeal in Ezekiel 33:11 itself -- is to move Israel toward repentance and away from the very outcome being described, suggesting the 'rejoicing' describes the certainty and completeness of the threatened judgment's execution should Israel persist in unfaithfulness, not God's ultimate preference between that outcome and Israel's repentance.

### 25. Do humans have genuine free will to choose their own destiny, or does God sovereignly determine it?

**References:** Deuteronomy 30:19; John 6:44; Ephesians 1:4-5; Romans 9:16

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 30:19 has Moses telling Israel, 'I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life,' presenting a genuine, morally significant choice. Elsewhere, Scripture describes salvation and belief as originating in God's own sovereign initiative rather than unaided human choice: 'No man can come to me, except the Father... draw him' (John 6:44); believers are described as chosen 'before the foundation of the world' (Ephesians 1:4-5); and Paul states plainly that election 'is not of him that willeth... but of God that sheweth mercy' (Romans 9:16).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is among the most extensively debated theological questions in the history of the Christian church, and different Christian traditions resolve the relationship between divine sovereignty and human choice in different ways -- broadly, Reformed/Calvinist traditions emphasize God's sovereign initiative in salvation as primary while still affirming that people make real, morally responsible choices for which they are accountable (a position often called compatibilism, holding that genuine human choice and comprehensive divine sovereignty are not mutually exclusive); Arminian and other traditions place greater emphasis on a libertarian human freedom that God has sovereignly chosen to grant and respect, understanding texts like Deuteronomy 30:19 as addressed to that genuine freedom, with God's foreknowledge (rather than unilateral determination) underlying texts about election. All major traditions agree that Deuteronomy 30:19 issues Moses' genuine appeal for Israel to choose covenant faithfulness, and that Scripture equally affirms God's sovereign purposes in salvation; how exactly these two truths relate at the deepest philosophical level remains a matter on which thoughtful, Scripture-affirming Christians continue to differ, rather than a question this document resolves in favor of one school of theology over another.
