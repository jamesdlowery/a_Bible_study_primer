# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Ephesians

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Ephesians,
either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Romans, Galatians,
and Amos documents, and Psalm 68. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses the primary New
Testament text for the household-submission question already introduced in the companion
Galatians document, presented with the same even-handedness between egalitarian and
complementarian readings. Entry 6 addresses a genuine, documented citation variance between
Ephesians 4:8 and its Old Testament source, Psalm 68:18, resolved with reference to the ancient
Aramaic Targum's own independent "giving" rendering of the same verse.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1, 6, and 4 are presented with genuine scholarly and theological
diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entry 6 was checked in detail against multiple sources, including
citation to the specific wording of the Targum on Psalm 68:18. The remaining entries are
addressed from standard harmonization approaches, several of which build on treatments already
established in the companion Romans, Galatians, and Amos documents.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly or
theological diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'Submit to one another' -- and then, 'wives, submit to your own husbands'

**References:** Ephesians 5:21-33

**The claim:** Ephesians 5:21 instructs believers generally to be 'submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ' -- language of mutual, reciprocal submission. The very next verse specifies this in gender-differentiated terms: 'Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord... as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands' (5:22, 24) -- language addressed specifically and only to wives, with husbands instead instructed to love their wives 'as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her' (5:25), rather than being given a parallel command to submit. This is one of the most actively and sincerely debated passages in contemporary Christian theology and ethics regarding marriage and gender roles.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ephesians 5:21-33 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most actively and sincerely debated questions in contemporary Christian theology and ethics regarding marriage, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is directly bound up with the same egalitarian/complementarian debate addressed in the companion Galatians document regarding Galatians 3:28, and this project does not adjudicate the underlying question. Those favoring a mutual-submission reading (broadly, an egalitarian position) hold that 5:21 establishes the controlling principle for the entire household code that follows, with 5:22's instruction to wives specifically representing one particular application of a broader mutual submission both spouses owe each other, not a unique or asymmetric obligation belonging to wives alone -- on this reading, husbands are in fact called to their own form of submission, expressed through the specific, sacrificial, self-giving love 5:25 describes (a demanding form of submission in its own right, even though the word 'submit' itself is not repeated for husbands). Those favoring an asymmetric, gender-differentiated reading (broadly, a complementarian position) hold that 5:21's general call to mutual deference within the body of Christ is then specified into distinct, non-identical roles for husbands and wives specifically within marriage -- wives called to submission, husbands called to sacrificial headship and love -- understanding these as two different, complementary expressions of Christian relationship rather than two applications of one single, undifferentiated principle, and noting that the passage's own repeated, extended instruction is addressed to wives regarding submission specifically (5:22, 24) in a way not mirrored by a parallel command to husbands to submit to their wives. This project takes no position on which reading is correct, consistent with its treatment of the comparable question in the companion Galatians document.

### 2. 'By grace you have been saved through faith... not a result of works' -- addressed at length in the companion Romans document

**References:** Ephesians 2:8-9; Romans 3:28; James 2:14-26

**The claim:** Ephesians 2:8-9 states, 'For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast' -- one of the clearest and most frequently quoted statements of salvation by grace through faith in the New Testament, connecting to the faith/works question addressed at length in the companion Romans document regarding Romans 3:28 and James 2:24.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ephesians 2:8-10 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Romans document for the fuller discussion of the faith/works question.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Romans document, which addresses the standard resolution: Paul's consistent target throughout his letters, including here, is the claim that human merit or law-observance can earn righteous standing before God, which he denies absolutely, while James addresses the separate question of how genuine, saving faith is recognized as authentic through its observable fruit -- both authors agree that genuine faith and good works are inseparably connected, addressing the relationship from two different angles. Ephesians itself reflects this same connection immediately afterward: 'For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them' (2:10), affirming that good works remain the intended and expected outcome of the grace-given faith 2:8-9 describes, even while denying they are its basis.

### 3. 'He chose us in him before the foundation of the world' -- addressed at length in the companion Romans document

**References:** Ephesians 1:4-5; Romans 9:10-24

**The claim:** Ephesians 1:4-5 states, 'he chose us in him before the foundation of the world... he predestined us for adoption as sons through Jesus Christ' -- language connecting to the same election and predestination question addressed at length in the companion Romans document regarding Romans 9.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ephesians 1:4-5 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Romans document for the fuller discussion of this question.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Romans document, which addresses the genuine, long-running theological division between Reformed/Calvinist and Arminian and other traditions regarding the nature of divine election, without adjudicating between them. This project takes the same approach to Ephesians 1:4-5 as to Romans 9, noting that this remains one of the most substantively and sincerely debated theological questions across major Christian traditions.

### 4. 'He had also descended into the lower regions, the earth' -- the incarnation, or a descent to the underworld?

**References:** Ephesians 4:9-10

**The claim:** Ephesians 4:9, in the midst of the discussion addressed in entry 6 above, adds a further detail: 'In saying, "He ascended," what does it mean but that he had also descended into the lower regions, the earth?' -- a phrase whose precise meaning has been debated, since some later Christian tradition (reflected in some historic forms of the Apostles' Creed, which include the phrase 'he descended into hell') has connected this verse to a supposed descent by Christ to the underworld or realm of the dead between his crucifixion and resurrection, while other readings understand the passage more simply as referring to the incarnation itself.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ephesians 4:9-10 plainly and consistently as given; this remains a genuinely debated interpretive question with connections to broader questions of historic Christian doctrine, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated interpretive question, and this entry presents the two leading readings rather than a single certain answer. Many modern commentators read the phrase 'the lower regions, the earth' as a grammatical construction (technically, a genitive of apposition, in which 'the earth' further explains or identifies 'the lower regions,' rather than describing a place lower than or beneath the earth) describing simply Christ's own incarnation -- his descent from heaven to ordinary earthly human existence -- making the passage's overall point a straightforward contrast between Christ's humble descent to earth in the incarnation and his subsequent triumphant ascension back to heaven, without reference to any separate descent to the underworld or place of the dead. Other commentators, including some connected to the historic tradition reflected in versions of the Apostles' Creed, read the phrase as referring to a specific, distinct descent to the realm of the dead or to Hades/Sheol between Christ's death and resurrection (a reading with some connection to 1 Peter 3:18-20's own separate, much-debated reference to Christ 'proclaiming to the spirits in prison'), understanding Ephesians 4:9 as a further scriptural basis for this element of traditional Christian doctrine. This project does not adjudicate between these two readings.

### 5. 'The mystery... which was not made known to the sons of men in other generations' -- vs. earlier Old Testament anticipations of Gentile inclusion

**References:** Ephesians 3:1-13; Isaiah 19:23-25; Amos 9:11-12; Ruth 1:1-4:22; Jonah 1:1-4:11

**The claim:** Ephesians 3:5-6 describes the inclusion of Gentiles as fellow heirs with Israel as a 'mystery... which was not made known to the sons of men in other generations as it has now been revealed to his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit' -- language suggesting this truth was entirely hidden prior to the apostolic era. Yet this project has addressed several Old Testament texts that appear to anticipate, in some form, God's blessing extending to Gentile nations alongside Israel: Isaiah 19:23-25's blessing of Egypt and Assyria alongside Israel, Amos 9:11-12 (whose Septuagint reading, cited at the Jerusalem Council in Acts 15 and addressed at length in the companion Amos document, speaks of 'the remnant of mankind' seeking the Lord), and the book of Ruth's own inclusion of a Moabite woman within the Davidic and messianic line, as well as the book of Jonah's account of a Gentile city's repentance and divine mercy.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ephesians 3:1-13 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the general anticipation of Gentile blessing (present in various forms in the Old Testament) from the specific, fuller mode of Jew-Gentile unity Paul describes as newly revealed, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Paul's language of a previously hidden 'mystery' as referring not to the bare fact that God's blessing would somehow, someday extend to the Gentiles. The Old Testament passages cited above do indeed anticipate that in general terms, and Paul himself elsewhere freely acknowledges and draws on them as scriptural support, e.g. Romans 15:9-12's own extended catena of Old Testament citations anticipating Gentile inclusion. Instead, the 'mystery' refers to the specific, previously unrevealed manner and full extent of that inclusion Paul is specifically describing in this letter -- namely, Jews and Gentiles united as genuinely equal 'fellow heirs, members of the same body, and partakers of the promise in Christ Jesus' (3:6), without any distinction or hierarchy between them, united in a single new covenant community. This is addressed at length earlier in the same letter (2:11-22), describing Christ breaking down 'the dividing wall of hostility' between Jew and Gentile. It is not Gentiles being incorporated as, in effect, secondary beneficiaries, or through conversion into Jewish covenant identity specifically, which is closer to how the Old Testament anticipations, including Ruth's and the proselyte tradition more broadly, generally functioned. On this reading, the 'mystery' concerns the specific, previously unrevealed mode and completeness of Jew-Gentile unity in Christ, not a claim that no Old Testament text had ever hinted at Gentile blessing in any form.

## Numbers and Text

### 6. 'He gave gifts to men' -- vs. Psalm 68:18's 'received gifts among men'

**References:** Ephesians 4:8; Psalm 68:18

**The claim:** Ephesians 4:8 quotes what is evidently Psalm 68:18: 'When he ascended on high he led a host of captives, and he gave gifts to men.' The Hebrew source text, however, reads in the opposite direction: 'You ascended on high, leading a host of captives... you received gifts among men' -- a striking, direct reversal (giving versus receiving) between Paul's citation and its apparent Old Testament source, beyond the expected shift from second-person ('you') to third-person ('he') address.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ephesians 4:8 and Psalm 68:18 exactly as given in each respective text, without harmonizing the specific verb in either passage to match the other; this is a matter of ancient citation practice and textual tradition (the Targum's alternate rendering), not a matter of translation choice among the ten translations of either passage.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely documented difference, and this entry presents the leading explanations rather than a single certain answer. The most widely offered solution notes that Paul's specific wording matches, more closely than the Hebrew or the Greek Septuagint (which itself follows the Hebrew's 'received'), the ancient Aramaic Targum's own paraphrase of this Psalm, which independently renders the verb as 'gave' rather than 'received' -- the Targum on Psalm 68:18 reads, in relevant part, 'you gave gifts to the sons of men,' indicating this 'giving' reading was already an established, traditional Jewish interpretive rendering of the verse well before Paul's own letter, rather than an invention unique to him. Other commentators note that both ideas -- receiving spoils as a conquering king, and then distributing them among one's own people -- are conceptually present within the underlying image itself even in the Hebrew's own 'received' wording (an ancient victorious king's reception of tribute or plunder from conquered peoples was always understood as being for the ultimate purpose of distributing it among his own followers and people), meaning Paul's citation draws out and makes explicit an implication already inherent in the image, applying it directly to Christ's own triumphant ascension and his subsequent giving of spiritual gifts to the church (the passage's own immediate point, 4:11-13) -- both the receiving and the giving being true and complementary aspects of the same triumphant, victorious act, with Paul's own citation emphasizing the specific aspect (giving) most relevant to his argument.
