# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Philemon

This document catalogs 3 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Philemon,
Paul's shortest surviving letter, either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison
with the companion Colossians document and 2 Corinthians. For each item, this document states
the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the
passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or
resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or
harmonization.

**A note on this letter's central and most historically significant entry:** Entry 1 addresses
this letter's own stance toward slavery -- one of the most historically consequential and
actively contested ethical questions addressed anywhere in this project. This letter was
directly and explicitly cited by both pro-slavery advocates and abolitionists in the
nineteenth-century American slavery debate, often in direct dialogue with each other's
opposing readings of the same text. This entry presents that genuine historical and scholarly
division candidly rather than minimizing it.

**A note on candor:** Entry 1 is presented with the full range of serious scholarly and
historical positions, including a minority reading that questions the letter's traditional
premise altogether, rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entry 1 was checked in detail against multiple sources documenting
both the letter's actual historical use in the American slavery debate and the range of
contemporary scholarly interpretation of Paul's own intent. The remaining entries are
addressed from standard interpretive approaches.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine historical and
scholarly diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged
to consult the primary text and the cross-references directly.

## Ethical Tensions

### 1. Paul sends a slave back to his master -- the letter's historically consequential and actively contested stance on slavery

**References:** Philemon 1:1-25

**The claim:** Paul's letter to Philemon concerns Onesimus, a slave belonging to Philemon (a wealthy member of the Colossian church, addressed in the companion Colossians document) who had apparently fled and come into contact with Paul, who was then imprisoned. Rather than helping Onesimus escape permanently, hiding him, or declaring the institution of slavery itself unjust, Paul sends Onesimus back to Philemon, accompanied by this letter -- a fact this letter itself has been cited, at direct cross purposes, on both sides of one of history's most consequential moral debates: this letter was extensively and explicitly invoked by pro-slavery advocates in the nineteenth-century American South (one slaveholding writer calling its supposed endorsement of slavery 'clear and conclusive'), while abolitionists pointed to the same letter's own specific language -- Paul's appeal that Philemon receive Onesimus 'no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother' (v.16), and Paul's explicit preference to act 'not by compulsion but of your own accord' (v.14) -- as evidence the letter's actual thrust undermines rather than endorses slavery as an institution.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the entirety of Philemon plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most historically significant and actively contested ethical questions addressed anywhere in this project, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is among the most historically consequential and directly, actively contested ethical questions addressed anywhere in this project, and this entry presents the considerations on each side candidly rather than minimizing the letter's genuinely difficult, real-world historical use on both sides of the American slavery debate. Those reading the letter as more straightforwardly accommodating of slavery as an institution note that Paul does, as a plain historical fact, return an enslaved person to his enslaver rather than declaring the institution itself sinful or unlawful. Neither this letter nor the rest of the New Testament ever issues an explicit, direct command to abolish slavery as such. Some connect this to the New Testament's broader pattern, addressed at other points in this project, of transforming social relationships and their underlying spirit from within existing structures rather than immediately overturning them by direct command -- a strategy some scholars argue reflects a considered judgment that an explicit early call for a general abolition would have been received, and likely violently suppressed, as a dangerous political revolt in the Greco-Roman world, rather than advancing the gospel's own primary aims. Those reading the letter as substantively undermining slavery's own moral legitimacy note Paul's own specific, repeated, and remarkably pointed language. He describes Onesimus, in relation to himself, as 'my very heart' (v.12) and, to Philemon, as 'a beloved brother' (v.16, specifically 'both in the flesh and in the Lord'), explicitly reframing the relationship between master and former slave as one of Christian kinship that fundamentally alters, even if it does not formally abolish, the relationship's own legal and social character. He also deliberately refuses to simply command Philemon by apostolic authority, despite claiming the right to do so (v.8-9), specifically so that any change in Onesimus's status would be voluntary rather than compelled (v.14). Some scholars, including a detailed Themelios journal article specifically analyzing Paul's social positioning and theological presuppositions, argue this rhetorical strategy was deliberately calculated to make Onesimus's manumission the socially and theologically expected, though not formally commanded, outcome, given the relational categories Paul has just established. Paul also personally and financially guarantees to cover any debt or loss Onesimus's flight caused (v.18-19), removing Philemon's strongest remaining practical grievance against releasing him. A minority, more radical scholarly reading, associated with the biblical scholar Allen Callahan, proposes Onesimus was not Philemon's slave at all, but his own estranged blood brother, making the letter fundamentally about family reconciliation rather than slavery in any form. Most scholars do not accept this reading, but it is worth noting as part of the genuine range of scholarly positions this brief letter has generated. This project does not adjudicate between these readings of Paul's own intent. The letter's own historical reception -- cited by slaveholders defending their practice and by abolitionists condemning it, often in direct and explicit dialogue with each other -- itself illustrates how genuinely contested this question has been and remains.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. 'Charge that to my account' -- a personal financial guarantee, often read as a picture of substitutionary atonement

**References:** Philemon 1:17-19

**The claim:** Philemon 17-19 has Paul personally guarantee any debt Onesimus owed Philemon: 'If you consider me your partner, receive him as you would receive me. If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, charge that to my account. I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it.' This specific, personal offer to absorb another's debt has been widely used by later theologians and preachers as an illustrative picture of Christ's own substitutionary atonement -- taking on a debt that was not his own so that the one who owed it could be received without penalty.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Philemon 17-19 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a notable theological illustration, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable theological illustration this project highlights explicitly, since Paul's own specific, personal, and financially concrete action here (rather than a merely abstract theological statement) has long been read by commentators as a vivid, real-world enactment of the same substitutionary principle Paul describes more abstractly and directly elsewhere regarding Christ's own atoning work (compare 2 Corinthians 5:21's 'for our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God'). The parallel is not exact in every detail (Paul's own guarantee addresses a specific financial debt between three named individuals in a single historical circumstance, while Christ's atonement addresses the far more comprehensive problem of sin's guilt before God), but the underlying structure -- an innocent party voluntarily offering to bear a debt or liability that was not properly his own, so that the actual debtor might be received without further penalty or grievance -- is widely regarded as a fitting, illustrative parallel rather than a strict theological equivalence, and is commonly cited in Christian teaching for exactly this illustrative purpose.

## Literary Composition

### 3. Philemon's own place within the Colossians correspondence

**References:** Philemon 1:1-2; Colossians 4:9, 17

**The claim:** Philemon shares several named individuals and circumstances in common with Colossians (addressed in its own companion document), including Onesimus himself, described in Colossians 4:9 as 'our faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you' being sent along with Tychicus -- suggesting both letters were written and delivered at the same time, by the same messengers, to closely related recipients (Philemon likely being a leading member of the Colossian church addressed more broadly in the other letter).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Philemon 1:1-2 and Colossians 4:9, 17 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a matter of historical and literary connection between two related letters, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a notable and instructive point of connection between two letters in the Pauline corpus, allowing each to inform the historical reading of the other -- Colossians 4:9's brief, passing mention of Onesimus being sent to Colossae 'with Tychicus' is widely read as confirming the historical circumstances Philemon addresses at much greater length and with far more personal, relational detail, with the two letters functioning as companion pieces delivered together: one a broader, more general letter to the whole congregation (Colossians), the other a short, personal, and much more delicately persuasive note to a specific individual within that congregation regarding a specific, sensitive personal and social situation (Philemon).
