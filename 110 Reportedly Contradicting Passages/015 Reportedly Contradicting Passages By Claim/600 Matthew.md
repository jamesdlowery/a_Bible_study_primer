# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Matthew

**A note on this document's place in the series:** This document begins this project's
coverage of the New Testament, following the completion of the Old Testament and the standard
Catholic deuterocanonical corpus. All ten translations surveyed in this project include the
Gospel of Matthew.

This document catalogs 69 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the Gospel of
Matthew, most concerning how this Gospel's own account of a given event compares with the
parallel accounts in Mark, Luke, John, and Acts. For each item, this document states the claim
as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved
(only where the translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim
appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this document's relationship to future documents in this series:** Several entries
here (6, 7, 8, 9, and 12) address questions of Gospel harmonization that will be revisited,
from each remaining Gospel's own specific vantage point, in this project's companion Mark,
Luke, and John documents -- each Gospel's own specific wording and detail merits its own direct
treatment rather than being treated as fully resolved by any single combined account presented
here.

**A note on candor:** Entry 7 (the "fourteen generations" count) presents multiple seriously
proposed solutions, including a reading favored by the more critical scholar Bart Ehrman,
without asserting a single certain answer. Entries 1, 5, and 12 are presented with genuine
scholarly diversity of opinion, including on questions (entry 12 especially) that remain bound
up with sincerely contested denominational differences this project does not adjudicate.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Matthew's genealogy of Jesus vs. Luke's -- the most famous harmonization case in the New Testament

**References:** Matthew 1:1-17; Luke 3:23-38

**The claim:** Matthew and Luke both trace Jesus' genealogy back through David, but the two lists diverge substantially: Matthew traces the line forward from Abraham through David's son Solomon, while Luke traces it from Jesus backward through David's son Nathan -- an entirely different son of David, and a largely different set of names for the many generations between David and Joseph. Even Joseph's own father is named differently: Matthew names him 'Jacob' (1:16), while Luke names him 'Heli' (3:23).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 1:1-17 and Luke 3:23-38 plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the two genealogies in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and genealogical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most extensively discussed harmonization questions in the entire New Testament. The standard, long-held resolution, traceable at least to the early church historian Eusebius and reflected in most conservative commentary since, holds that the two genealogies trace two different lines because they serve two different purposes and, in the majority reading, follow two different family lines entirely. Matthew traces Joseph's own legal, royal descent through Solomon's kingly line, establishing Jesus' legal right to David's throne through his adoptive father Joseph -- appropriate to Matthew's overall interest in Jesus as the promised Davidic king. Luke, on the majority interpretive reading, actually traces Mary's own biological descent through Nathan's non-royal line, with 'Heli' understood as Mary's father, and Joseph's own listing in Luke 3:23 ('being the son, as was supposed, of Joseph') read as Joseph being Heli's son-in-law rather than blood son -- a common ancient convention when a genealogy passed through a daughter with no surviving sons. This reading is also the standard explanation offered in this project's own earlier discussion of Jeremiah's curse on Jehoiachin, addressed at length in the companion Jeremiah and Haggai documents. If Matthew's line, which includes the cursed Jehoiachin, traces Joseph's legal claim, while Luke's line, which does not pass through Jehoiachin at all, traces Mary's own biological descent, then Jesus inherits David's legal throne-right through his adoptive father while avoiding Jehoiachin's specific curse through his actual, virgin-born biological descent via Mary. A minority of scholars read both genealogies as tracing Joseph's own line by two different, both fully biological, reckonings, with adoptive or levirate marriage customs proposed to explain the divergence. This project does not adjudicate this position as superior to the majority reading, noting only that multiple serious harmonizations have been proposed and defended at length.

### 2. Two distinct, largely non-overlapping birth narratives

**References:** Matthew 1:18-2:23; Luke 1:5-2:40

**The claim:** Matthew's account of Jesus' birth features the angel's announcement to Joseph (not Mary), the star, magi from the East bringing gifts, Herod's plot and the slaughter of the innocents, and the family's flight to and return from Egypt. Luke's account instead features the angel Gabriel's announcement to Mary, the census requiring the family's journey to Bethlehem, shepherds receiving the angelic announcement, and the family's presentation of the infant Jesus at the Jerusalem Temple, followed by a direct return to Nazareth -- with almost no narrative material shared between the two accounts, and some points of apparent tension in constructing a single combined timeline (Luke's account reads as though the family returns to Nazareth directly after the Temple presentation, roughly forty days after the birth per the purification law of Leviticus 12, leaving no obvious place for Matthew's flight to Egypt to occur within Luke's own sequence).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both birth narratives plainly and consistently as given, without merging or cross-referencing them in the text; the harmonization concerns combining complementary selective accounts, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read the two accounts as complementary rather than contradictory -- each author selecting different episodes from a presumably larger body of tradition and family memory available to them, consistent with each Gospel's own particular theological and narrative interests (Matthew's consistent emphasis throughout his Gospel on typological fulfillment of Old Testament patterns, appropriate to the star, the magi, and the flight to Egypt, which explicitly echoes Israel's own earlier sojourn there; Luke's own particular interest in the poor and marginalized, appropriate to the shepherds, and his consistent attention to Temple piety, appropriate to the presentation). On the specific timeline tension, most harmonizations place the magi's visit and the family's flight to Egypt sometime after the Temple presentation described in Luke -- Matthew's own account describes the magi visiting a 'house' rather than the stable/manger setting of Luke's nativity account, and Herod's murderous decree targets children up to two years old (Matthew 2:16), both details generally read as indicating the magi arrived considerably later than the original birth, allowing the family to have already completed the Temple presentation, returned briefly to Bethlehem or nearby, and only then received the magi and fled to Egypt before eventually settling in Nazareth (which both Gospels agree was the family's ultimate home, Matthew 2:23, Luke 2:39). This reconstruction requires combining details from both accounts rather than reading either Gospel's own narrative in isolation, but does not require treating the two accounts as strictly incompatible.

### 3. Judas's death: hanged himself -- or fell headlong and burst open?

**References:** Matthew 27:3-10; Acts 1:15-19

**The claim:** Matthew 27:5 states that Judas, overcome with remorse after betraying Jesus, 'went and hanged himself.' Acts 1:18, in Peter's own later speech to the assembled disciples, describes Judas's death differently: 'falling headlong, he burst open in the middle and all his bowels gushed out' -- a classic and frequently cited apparent contradiction regarding the specific manner of Judas's death.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 27:3-10 and Acts 1:15-19 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading the two accounts as describing sequential stages of the same event, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as two compatible descriptions of the same event from different vantage points and levels of detail, rather than as competing claims: the standard harmonization holds that Judas hanged himself (Matthew's account, describing the immediate cause of death), and that the rope subsequently broke, or the branch he used gave way, causing his body to fall from a height onto rocky ground below, resulting in the additional, gruesome physical detail Acts describes (a location consistent with the 'Field of Blood' both accounts separately associate with Judas's death, Matthew 27:8, Acts 1:19, itself sometimes identified with a site above a rocky ravine outside Jerusalem, lending some circumstantial support to this reconstruction). This harmonization has been offered since at least the early centuries of Christian commentary and does not require treating either account as false, only as each supplying a different, non-exhaustive part of the same overall sequence of events -- a pattern of complementary rather than competing detail addressed at several other points in this project regarding parallel accounts of the same event narrated with differing emphasis.

### 4. The resurrection morning: differing details across all four Gospels

**References:** Matthew 28:1-10; Mark 16:1-8; Luke 24:1-12; John 20:1-18

**The claim:** Matthew's resurrection account describes Mary Magdalene and 'the other Mary' visiting the tomb, an earthquake, a single angel descending to roll away the stone in their presence, and the two women encountering the risen Jesus directly on their way to tell the disciples (28:1-10). This differs in several specific details from the parallel accounts in Mark (a larger group of named women, a young man already seated inside the tomb, no earthquake described, and in the earliest manuscripts, an abrupt ending without a resurrection appearance), Luke (an unspecified larger group of women, two men in dazzling apparel, no direct encounter with Jesus at the tomb itself), and John (Mary Magdalene alone at first, two angels, and a personal encounter with Jesus that unfolds differently than Matthew's).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each Gospel's resurrection account plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the specific details across the four accounts in the text itself; this is a matter of combining complementary eyewitness-based accounts, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is among the most extensively discussed harmonization questions addressed anywhere in the New Testament, and this entry presents the standard approach applied throughout this project to comparable cases of parallel accounts differing in specific, non-essential detail while agreeing on the central, most theologically significant facts (all four Gospels agree: the tomb was empty, angelic messengers were present, and Jesus was seen alive afterward). Most harmonizations propose that the differing details reflect each author's own selective emphasis and access to different eyewitness testimony, rather than any author's carelessness or fabrication -- a plausible combined sequence holds that several women (including, at minimum, Mary Magdalene, present in all four accounts) visited the tomb together or in overlapping groups over a short span of time, encountering one or more angelic figures at different points, with Matthew's singular focus on Mary Magdalene and 'the other Mary' representing a narrower narrative focus rather than a claim that no other women were present (a claim none of the four Gospels makes explicitly). This project addresses the same underlying resurrection-narrative harmonization question again, from each remaining Gospel's own specific vantage point, in the companion Mark, Luke, and John documents, since each Gospel's own specific details merit their own direct treatment rather than being fully resolved by any single combined account.

### 5. The Sermon on the Mount -- and Luke's differently located, differently worded 'Sermon on the Plain'

**References:** Matthew 5:1-7:29; Luke 6:17-49

**The claim:** Matthew presents an extended block of Jesus' ethical teaching, delivered from a mountainside (5:1), including the Beatitudes in a form beginning 'Blessed are the poor in spirit' (5:3). Luke records a substantially similar, but not identical, block of teaching delivered 'on a level place' (6:17) rather than a mountain, with a shorter and differently worded set of Beatitudes ('Blessed are you who are poor,' 6:20, without 'in spirit,' and including several 'woes' addressed to the rich and comfortable, 6:24-26, absent from Matthew's version) -- raising a question of whether these are two reports of a single occasion, or two distinct sermons delivered on different occasions.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both accounts plainly and consistently as given, preserving each Gospel's own specific location description and wording; the harmonization question addressed here is a matter of historical and literary interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Commentators are divided, and this entry presents both positions rather than adjudicating between them. Some hold these are two independent reports of the same single sermon, with the differing content explained by each Gospel's own selective compression, paraphrase, and thematic emphasis (a documented feature of ancient historical writing more generally, addressed at several points elsewhere in this project), and the differing location description reconciled by proposing Jesus delivered the teaching on a level area partway up or atop a mountain (both descriptions being accurate depending on which specific vantage point or stage of the location is emphasized). Others hold these represent two genuinely distinct occasions -- itinerant teachers in Jesus' own historical setting would naturally have repeated and adapted core teaching material on different occasions to different audiences, meaning Matthew and Luke may each record an authentic, separate delivery of substantially similar core content Jesus taught more than once during his ministry, a reading some find better accounts for the real differences in specific wording and setting than positing extensive compression of a single event. This project does not adjudicate between these two positions.

### 6. The centurion's servant: direct approach, or through intermediaries?

**References:** Matthew 8:5-13; Luke 7:1-10

**The claim:** Matthew's account of Jesus healing a centurion's servant has the centurion himself approach Jesus directly and speak with him in person (8:5-9). Luke's account of what is evidently the same episode instead has the centurion send Jewish elders to intercede on his behalf, and later a second group of friends, with the centurion himself never described as directly meeting or speaking with Jesus in person at any point (7:3-6).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both accounts plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing narrative compression of an action performed through an authorized intermediary, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this as a straightforward case of narrative compression -- a well-documented and unremarkable feature of ancient historical and biographical writing, in which an action performed through an authorized intermediary is often summarized as though performed by the principal party himself, since the intermediary acts with and communicates the principal's own words and authority (a pattern with some rough modern parallel in describing a message delivered by a spokesperson as something 'the president said,' without this being considered inaccurate). Matthew's account telescopes the exchange to its essential content and outcome (the centurion's own remarkable statement of faith, quoted directly in both Gospels in substantially the same words, 8:8-9/7:6-8, being the passage's real theological point), while Luke preserves the fuller detail of how the message was actually physically relayed through the two waves of intermediaries -- both accounts agree entirely on the substance of what was communicated and the outcome (the servant's healing), differing only in how much of the delivery mechanism each author chose to include.

### 7. Where did John the Baptist baptize?

**References:** Matthew 3:1-6; John 1:28; John 3:23

**The claim:** Matthew 3:1-6 places John's baptizing ministry in 'the wilderness of Judaea' and the Jordan region. John 1:28 locates it at 'Bethabara beyond Jordan,' and John 3:23 later locates it at 'Aenon near to Salim,' 'because there was much water there' -- three different place-names associated with John's baptizing activity.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** John's baptizing ministry spanned an extended period and, like any itinerant preacher's, moved between several locations along the Jordan valley over its course -- the Gospels record him at different points and times at different specific sites suited to his purpose (Aenon explicitly noted for its abundant water supply), not competing claims about a single fixed location at a single moment. Matthew's general reference to 'the wilderness of Judaea' comfortably encompasses the broader region within which these more specific sites are found.

### 8. Were the Pharisees baptized by John?

**References:** Matthew 3:7; Luke 7:29-30

**The claim:** Matthew 3:7 has 'many of the Pharisees and Sadducees' coming to John's baptism, with John addressing them directly. Luke 7:29-30, by contrast, states that while the tax collectors 'justified God, being baptized,' 'the Pharisees and lawyers rejected the counsel of God against themselves, being not baptized of him.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Matthew 3:7 does not actually state that the Pharisees and Sadducees who came were baptized -- it records only that they 'came to his baptism' (i.e., came to the site, likely to observe or investigate John's popular movement, consistent with the Pharisees' recorded pattern elsewhere of scrutinizing new religious movements) before John's sharp rebuke of their insincerity ('O generation of vipers, who hath warned you to flee from the wrath to come?'). Luke 7:30's statement that the Pharisees as a group rejected John's baptism is fully consistent with Matthew's account of some Pharisees showing up to observe and being confronted, without actually submitting to the baptism themselves.

### 9. Did Jesus personally baptize anyone?

**References:** Matthew 3:11; John 4:1-2

**The claim:** Matthew 3:11 has John describe the coming Jesus as one who 'shall baptize you with the Holy Ghost, and with fire,' language some read alongside other Gospel references to Jesus baptizing. John 4:1-2 states directly that 'Jesus himself baptized not, but his disciples.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** John 4:2 itself is the New Testament's own explicit clarification on this exact point, stating plainly that Jesus did not personally perform water baptism, leaving that specific act to his disciples acting under his authority -- Matthew 3:11 describes a different, future baptism entirely (a baptism 'with the Holy Ghost, and with fire,' fulfilled at Pentecost, Acts 2), not a claim that Jesus personally administered water baptism to individuals during his earthly ministry.

### 10. How did God address Jesus at his baptism -- in the third person, or the second?

**References:** Matthew 3:17; Mark 1:11; Luke 3:22

**The claim:** Matthew 3:17 records the voice from heaven declaring, in the third person, 'This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.' Mark 1:11 and Luke 3:22 both instead render the voice addressing Jesus directly, in the second person: 'Thou art my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a minor and unremarkable difference in how each Gospel writer chose to report the same spoken declaration -- ancient historical writing routinely permitted reporting a speech either as direct address ('you are...') or as a third-person announcement to onlookers ('this is...') without any implication that two different statements were made; Matthew's version may reflect a greater interest in presenting the moment as a public announcement to the crowd and to John, while Mark and Luke preserve the more intimate, direct address to Jesus himself -- both capturing the same substantive divine affirmation of Jesus' identity and sonship.

### 11. How exactly did Peter, Andrew, James, and John become Jesus's disciples, and when?

**References:** Matthew 4:12-22; Mark 1:16-20; John 1:35-42

**The claim:** Matthew 4:18-22 (paralleled in Mark 1:16-20) describes Jesus calling Peter and Andrew, then James and John, abruptly while they are casting or mending fishing nets, with the brothers immediately leaving everything to follow him -- narrated as an apparently first encounter. John 1:35-42, however, describes Andrew (and likely another disciple) first meeting Jesus much earlier, as a follower of John the Baptist who is directed to Jesus by the Baptist's own testimony ('Behold the Lamb of God!'), with Andrew then bringing his brother Simon to Jesus -- an earlier, calmer, Judean introduction predating the Galilean fishing-net episode. Separately, Matthew places this calling (4:12-22) after John the Baptist's imprisonment, while also, in the same general narrative span, describing Jesus healing Peter's mother-in-law (8:14-15) at a point that could be read as either before or after the formal 'leaving their nets' call.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard and widely accepted harmonization holds that these describe two distinct stages in the same disciples' developing relationship with Jesus, narrated by different Gospels with different emphases: John 1:35-42 records an earlier, initial introduction and period of accompanying Jesus without yet having left their ordinary occupations (consistent with John's Gospel generally covering an earlier Judean phase of Jesus' ministry the Synoptics do not detail), after which the disciples returned to their fishing trade for a time; Matthew 4:18-22 and its parallels then record a later, decisive, formal call to full-time discipleship -- leaving their nets and livelihood entirely -- issued after John's imprisonment and the start of Jesus' Galilean ministry proper. On this reading, the fishing-net scene is not the disciples' first encounter with Jesus but their formal, final commitment following an already-established relationship, resolving the apparent conflict between an abrupt-sounding call and a prior relationship John's Gospel documents. The relative sequencing of Peter's mother-in-law's healing (occurring, per Mark 1:29-31 and Luke 4:38-39, on the same day as other early Capernaum ministry activity) is best read as compatible with this same understanding of an already-underway relationship rather than requiring a strict, single linear sequence across all three Synoptics' sometimes topically-arranged material.

### 12. Was Jesus homeless?

**References:** Matthew 8:20; Mark 2:1; Mark 1:29

**The claim:** Matthew 8:20 has Jesus say, 'The Son of man hath not where to lay his head,' describing a homeless, itinerant existence. Yet the Gospels elsewhere refer to Jesus being 'in the house' at Capernaum (Mark 2:1) and staying at Peter's house there (Mark 1:29), suggesting he had some settled base.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Matthew 8:20 is best read, in its immediate context (a would-be follower's impulsive offer to follow Jesus 'whithersoever thou goest'), as describing the itinerant, unsettled character of Jesus' active preaching ministry -- constantly traveling from town to town without a permanent home of his own to return to -- rather than a claim that Jesus never had access to any dwelling at any point. Capernaum functioned as a home base and gathering point during the Galilean phase of his ministry (likely at Peter's own house, which Jesus used when in the area), consistent with an itinerant teacher who used a friend's or disciple's home as a base of operations without owning a permanent residence of his own -- the same distinction between having no permanent home and having no access to shelter at all applies to many itinerant ancient teachers.

### 13. Was Jairus's daughter already dead, or merely dying, when Jesus was approached -- and when exactly was the woman with the hemorrhage healed?

**References:** Matthew 9:18-26; Mark 5:21-43; Luke 8:40-56

**The claim:** Matthew 9:18 has Jairus tell Jesus at the outset, 'my daughter is even now dead,' before Jesus ever sets out. Mark 5:23 and Luke 8:42 both instead have Jairus report his daughter as merely dying or at the point of death, with the news of her actual death arriving only later, via messengers, while Jesus is en route (delayed by the intervening healing of the woman with a chronic hemorrhage).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a well-recognized and unremarkable case of Matthew's own characteristic editorial compression -- a pattern documented at several other points in this project regarding Matthew's Gospel specifically (compare the fig tree's withering, addressed elsewhere in this document) -- in which Matthew, writing a shorter, more compact account of the same episode, telescopes the sequence by having Jairus's initial report already state the outcome (death) that Mark and Luke's fuller accounts show unfolding progressively as the story develops. All three accounts agree entirely on the substance: a girl who was gravely ill and near death did die before Jesus arrived, and Jesus raised her; Matthew's shorter version simply front-loads the final outcome into Jairus's initial statement rather than narrating the intervening messengers' report separately, a standard feature of ancient historical compression rather than a conflicting claim about the actual sequence of events.

### 14. Did John the Baptist recognize Jesus as the Son of God?

**References:** Matthew 11:2-3; John 1:29-34

**The claim:** Matthew 11:2-3 has John the Baptist, from prison, send disciples to ask Jesus, 'Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?' -- a question read by some as expressing doubt about Jesus' identity. John 1:29-34 has John the Baptist, earlier, declare unambiguously, 'Behold the Lamb of God,' and testify, 'I saw, and bare record that this is the Son of God.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Matthew 11:2-3 not as a genuine reversal of John's earlier, clear recognition, but as a very human moment of doubt or confusion arising from John's own difficult circumstances -- imprisoned, facing his own death, and perhaps puzzled that Jesus' ministry did not yet match his own expectation of the Messiah's coming judgment and deliverance (a theme John himself had earlier preached, Matthew 3:10-12) -- prompting him to seek reassurance rather than to retract his earlier testimony. Jesus' response (11:4-6, pointing to the specific messianic works being accomplished) is best read as gently reaffirming, not correcting a false claim, John's earlier recognition -- a believer's momentary doubt under severe pressure is not the same as never having genuinely recognized the truth in the first place, a pattern of faithful figures experiencing genuine doubt addressed at several points elsewhere in this project.

### 15. Who is for or against Jesus?

**References:** Matthew 12:30; Mark 9:38-40; Luke 9:49-50

**The claim:** Matthew 12:30 has Jesus state, 'He that is not with me is against me.' Mark 9:40 and Luke 9:50, in a different episode, have Jesus instead say the opposite-sounding formula: 'he that is not against us is on our part' (or 'for us').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two statements address different situations and different questions, resolving what only appears to be a flat verbal contradiction: Matthew 12:30 is spoken in the specific context of the Pharisees' hostile accusation that Jesus casts out demons by the power of Satan (12:22-29) -- addressing active, direct opposition to Jesus' own person and mission, where genuine neutrality toward Christ himself is impossible ('not with me is against me' describes the impossibility of neutrality regarding Jesus' own identity and claims). Mark 9:38-40 and Luke 9:49-50 instead address the disciples' concern about an unaffiliated exorcist casting out demons in Jesus' name without being part of their immediate group -- a question about how to treat someone doing genuinely good, Christ-honoring work without formal affiliation, where Jesus counsels inclusion rather than suspicion of an ally acting in his name. One passage addresses direct hostility toward Christ himself; the other addresses cautious openness toward someone acting favorably, if informally, in his name -- different situations, not the same principle stated in contradictory forms.

### 16. Did Jesus always speak in parables?

**References:** Matthew 13:34; Matthew 5:1-7:29

**The claim:** Matthew 13:34 states, 'without a parable spake he not unto them,' seemingly describing Jesus' universal teaching method. Yet Matthew's own Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5-7) and numerous other passages record Jesus teaching at length in direct, non-parabolic discourse.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Matthew 13:34's statement is explicitly scoped, in its immediate context, to Jesus' teaching to the crowds specifically during this particular occasion and phase of his ministry described in this chapter ('these things spake Jesus unto the multitude in parables; and without a parable spake he not unto them' -- 'them' referring back to the crowds addressed in this specific discourse), not a categorical claim covering every teaching occasion throughout Jesus' entire ministry, including the direct instruction he elsewhere gives privately to disciples or in other contexts like the Sermon on the Mount. Read in its own immediate literary context, the verse describes Matthew 13's own parable discourse specifically, not Jesus' teaching style as a universal, exceptionless rule.

### 17. Did Herod think Jesus was John the Baptist raised from the dead?

**References:** Matthew 14:1-2; Luke 9:7-9

**The claim:** Matthew 14:1-2 has Herod state directly to his servants, 'This is John the Baptist; he is risen from the dead.' Luke 9:7-9 instead describes Herod as perplexed, hearing several competing theories about Jesus' identity (John raised, Elijah, an old prophet risen again), and has Herod himself ask uncertainly, 'John have I beheaded: but who is this, of whom I hear such things?'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both accounts are consistent with a single, superstitious, and genuinely conflicted state of mind -- ancient and modern accounts alike commonly record a person voicing a specific fear or belief at one moment (Matthew's account of Herod's dread-filled conviction) while also expressing genuine uncertainty and searching at another (Luke's account of Herod's perplexity amid competing rumors) without this being incoherent; a guilt-ridden ruler troubled by rumors of a dead man's return could easily oscillate between superstitious conviction and genuine puzzlement within the same general period, and the two Gospels are best read as capturing two different facets or moments of Herod's same troubled reaction rather than two flatly incompatible claims about a single, fixed mental state.

### 18. Did the disciples understand Jesus' predictions of his coming death, given that they reacted with sorrow, yet elsewhere are said not to have understood at all?

**References:** Matthew 16:21-23; Matthew 17:22-23; Luke 9:44-45; Luke 18:31-34

**The claim:** Matthew 16:21 has Jesus begin to show his disciples that he must suffer, die, and rise again, provoking Peter's immediate, active protest (16:22). Matthew 17:22-23 records a second prediction, with the disciples described as 'exceeding sorry' -- a reaction implying at least some grasp of the meaning. Luke's parallel accounts of similar predictions (9:44-45; 18:31-34), however, state plainly that the disciples 'understood none of these things... neither knew they the things which were spoken.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These are best read as describing a real, gradual, and uneven process of partial comprehension rather than a flat contradiction about a single fixed state of understanding: the disciples clearly grasped enough of what Jesus said to react with alarm, protest, and sorrow (Peter's rebuke in 16:22 shows he understood the words well enough to strenuously object to them), while still failing to genuinely grasp or accept the deeper meaning and necessity of what Jesus was describing -- particularly the resurrection component, which Luke's own Gospel elsewhere shows the disciples still not expecting even after Jesus' actual death (Luke 24:11's report of the resurrection being met with disbelief, 'their words seemed to them as idle tales'). Emotional reaction to unwelcome news (sorrow, protest) and genuine, settled comprehension of its full significance are different things, and Scripture's own later narrative (the disciples' continued shock at the empty tomb) confirms that whatever partial understanding they had did not amount to the fuller comprehension they would only reach after the resurrection itself.

### 19. Did James and John themselves ask for the best seats in the kingdom, or did their mother ask on their behalf?

Mark's version simply emphasizes the sons' own personal responsibility and complicity in the request, while Matthew notes the mother's role in formally presenting it -- both details plausible aspects of a single family conversation with Jesus.

### 20. Did the fig tree wither immediately, or was its withering only noticed the next day?

**References:** Matthew 21:18-20; Mark 11:12-14, 20-21

**The claim:** Matthew 21:19-20 has Jesus curse the fig tree, and it 'presently' (immediately) withers, with the disciples marveling right away. Mark 11:12-14 has Jesus curse the tree one day, with the disciples only noticing and remarking on its withered state 'in the morning' of the following day, as they pass by again.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is another instance of Matthew's own characteristic editorial compression, the same pattern addressed elsewhere in this document regarding Jairus's daughter: Matthew narrates the cursing and its ultimate result (the tree's withering) together for narrative economy, while Mark spells out the fuller two-day sequence explicitly. The tree's actual physical withering process, per both accounts, is understood to have begun immediately upon the curse ('presently' describing the onset of the withering process itself, consistent with a tree dying from the roots quickly but not being fully, visibly withered until some hours had passed) -- the disciples' noticing and remarking on the completed, visible result the following day (per Mark's fuller timeline) is not in tension with the process itself having begun at once, as Matthew's more compressed account emphasizes.

### 21. Was Jesus taken to Caiaphas directly, or to Annas first?

### 22. Did the false witnesses at Jesus' trial accuse him of saying something he never actually said?

**References:** Matthew 26:59-61; John 2:19-21

**The claim:** Matthew 26:59-61 describes false witnesses testifying against Jesus at his trial, claiming he said, 'I am able to destroy the temple of God, and to build it in three days' -- testimony Matthew's own narration explicitly labels false witness. Yet John 2:19 records Jesus, years earlier, saying something very close to this: 'Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The false witnesses' testimony was false not in reporting words Jesus never spoke (John 2:19 shows he did, in substance, say something very similar), but in twisting and misrepresenting his actual meaning: John 2:21 explicitly clarifies that Jesus 'spake of the temple of his body' -- a veiled, symbolic reference to his own coming death and resurrection, not a literal threat against the physical Jerusalem Temple building. The false witnesses at the trial (whose testimony Matthew 26:60 additionally notes did not even agree with each other in the details) twisted this genuine but symbolic statement into a literal, seditious threat against the Temple building -- a serious, capital charge Jesus never actually intended -- making their witness false through deliberate or careless decontextualization and distortion of his real meaning, not through inventing words from nothing.

### 23. Did Jesus answer the high priest with a direct 'I am,' or with the more indirect 'Thou hast said'?

**References:** Matthew 26:63-64; Mark 14:61-62

**The claim:** When the high priest demands to know if he is the Christ, the Son of God, Matthew 26:64 has Jesus respond, 'Thou hast said' -- a somewhat indirect-sounding formula. Mark 14:62, recording the same exchange, has Jesus answer simply and directly, 'I am.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'Thou hast said' (su eipas) is a recognized Jewish idiom of affirmation, equivalent in force to 'yes, it is as you say' -- both Gospel accounts record the same substantive affirmative answer to the high priest's question, differing only in which conventional way of expressing assent each writer preserved in translating the original Aramaic or Hebrew exchange into Greek. Matthew's own subsequent narrative treats the response as a clear, direct affirmation (the high priest immediately tears his clothes and declares it blasphemy, 26:65), confirming that 'thou hast said' was understood by everyone present as an unambiguous 'yes,' not a evasive or non-committal answer.

### 24. Was Jesus silent before Pilate, given that he also answered one of Pilate's direct questions?

**References:** Matthew 27:11-14

**The claim:** Matthew 27:11 has Jesus directly answer Pilate's question, 'Art thou the King of the Jews?' with 'Thou sayest.' Yet 27:12-14 immediately states that when further accused by the chief priests and elders, Jesus 'answered nothing... he answered him to never a word.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The text itself distinguishes two different moments within the same proceeding, and there is no actual tension once read in sequence: Jesus directly answered Pilate's own formal, specific question regarding the political charge against him (27:11), but then remained silent specifically in response to the chief priests' and elders' subsequent barrage of additional accusations (27:12-13) -- a deliberate, notable silence in the face of unsubstantiated pile-on accusations, not a claim that Jesus never spoke a single word throughout the entire proceeding. 'Never a word' in 27:14 refers to his response to those specific further accusations, exactly as the immediately preceding verses describe, not a contradiction of his earlier direct answer to Pilate's own formal question.

### 25. Who put the robe on Jesus, and was it a single mocking incident or two separate ones?

These are two different incidents, involving two different sets of soldiers under two different rulers, at two different points in the same extended sequence of trials -- not competing accounts of a single event.

### 26. What color was the robe placed on Jesus -- scarlet, or purple?

**References:** Matthew 27:28; Mark 15:17; John 19:2

**The claim:** Matthew 27:28 describes the robe placed on Jesus by the mocking soldiers as 'scarlet.' Mark 15:17 and John 19:2 both instead describe it as 'purple.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Ancient dye technology and color terminology did not draw as sharp a line between deep red/scarlet and purple as modern English does -- both colors were commonly produced from related or overlapping sources in the ancient Mediterranean world, and the color range the ancient terms covered overlapped considerably, particularly for a worn or faded Roman military garment (the standard Roman soldier's cloak, the sagum, was typically a deep red that could, depending on the dye's exact composition, wear, and lighting, be described by an observer as either scarlet or purple). Most commentators read this as the same single garment described by two equally valid ancient color terms for a similar reddish-purple hue, rather than two different garments -- a common and unremarkable variation in color description for a shade sitting near the boundary between what ancient writers called each color.

### 27. Who carried Jesus' cross -- Simon of Cyrene, or Jesus himself?

**References:** Matthew 27:32; John 19:17

**The claim:** Matthew 27:32 (paralleled in Mark and Luke) has the soldiers compel Simon of Cyrene to carry Jesus' cross. John 19:17 instead states, 'he bearing his cross went forth' -- Jesus carrying it himself.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization, consistent with standard Roman crucifixion practice (which required the condemned to carry the crossbeam to the execution site) and with all four Gospels' broader agreement on the same execution, holds that Jesus initially carried his own cross out of the city, as John's account describes, but that at some point along the way -- very plausibly due to physical exhaustion following the severe scourging Matthew, Mark, and John all separately record (27:26; 15:15; 19:1) -- the soldiers compelled Simon to carry it for the remainder of the journey, as the Synoptic Gospels record. John's account describes the beginning of the journey; the Synoptics describe what happened during it; both are accurate descriptions of different stages of the same walk to Golgotha, not competing claims about who carried the cross for its entire length.

### 28. What did the soldiers offer Jesus to drink -- vinegar mingled with gall, or wine mingled with myrrh?

**References:** Matthew 27:34; Mark 15:23

**The claim:** Matthew 27:34 describes Jesus being offered 'vinegar mingled with gall' before the crucifixion, which he tastes and refuses. Mark 15:23 describes the same moment as an offer of 'wine mingled with myrrh,' also refused.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as two descriptions of the same single offered drink -- a sour wine drugged with myrrh, customarily offered to condemned prisoners to dull their senses before crucifixion -- described from two different angles: Mark records the actual physical ingredients (wine and myrrh), while Matthew's 'gall' most likely describes the drink's bitter taste in the general Old Testament idiom for bitterness (compare Psalm 69:21's 'they gave me also gall for my meat,' a psalm widely understood messianically and likely informing Matthew's specific word choice here) rather than naming a literally different, additional ingredient. Both accounts agree Jesus tasted and refused whatever was offered, consistent with a single event described with different descriptive emphases.

### 29. What did the sign above Jesus' head actually say?

**References:** Matthew 27:37; Mark 15:26; Luke 23:38; John 19:19-20

**The claim:** The four Gospels give four different wordings for the sign placed above Jesus on the cross: 'THIS IS JESUS THE KING OF THE JEWS' (Matthew), 'THE KING OF THE JEWS' (Mark), 'THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS' (Luke), and 'JESUS OF NAZARETH THE KING OF THE JEWS' (John).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** John 19:20 itself states the sign was written in three languages (Hebrew, Greek, and Latin) -- each Gospel writer most likely recorded a faithful summary or translation of the sign's substantive content rather than a claim to reproduce every word in every language verbatim, a natural feature of ancient reporting on a multilingual sign rather than a contradiction. All four versions agree entirely on the sign's essential, and historically significant, content -- the charge 'THE KING OF THE JEWS' -- while differing only in which additional identifying words (Jesus' name, hometown, or the demonstrative 'this is') each writer included; the four versions are easily combined into one fuller original reading (such as 'THIS IS JESUS OF NAZARETH THE KING OF THE JEWS') without any actual conflict in substance.

### 30. Did both criminals crucified with Jesus revile him, or only one?

**References:** Matthew 27:44; Mark 15:32; Luke 23:39-43

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard and widely accepted harmonization holds that both criminals initially joined in the general reviling of Jesus that the surrounding crowd, passersby, and religious leaders were all engaged in (which Matthew and Mark's brief summary captures as the crucifixion's opening general character), with one of the two criminals later, over the course of the extended hours of crucifixion, having a change of heart -- rebuking his companion's continued mockery and turning to Jesus in genuine faith, exactly the fuller, more detailed sequence Luke's account supplies. Matthew and Mark's brief accounts describe the initial, shared reaction; Luke supplies the subsequent development that changed one criminal's response -- complementary stages of the same several-hour event, not two flatly incompatible claims about a single, static moment.

### 31. What did the centurion say when Jesus died?

**References:** Matthew 27:54; Luke 23:47

**The claim:** Matthew 27:54 has the centurion (and those with him) say, 'Truly this was the Son of God.' Luke 23:47, describing the same moment, instead has him say, 'Certainly this was a righteous man.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this as a single utterance or reaction combining both sentiments, with each Gospel writer preserving a different part or paraphrase of the centurion's fuller response -- a Roman centurion, astonished by the extraordinary circumstances of Jesus' death (the earthquake, the darkness, the manner of his dying), quite plausibly expressed both that Jesus was innocent of the charges against him (Luke's emphasis, fitting Luke's own recurring interest throughout his Gospel in Jesus' innocence before Roman authority) and that he was in some sense divine or uniquely favored by God (Matthew's emphasis, using language a Gentile centurion might express in terms available to him without necessarily grasping the full Christian sense of 'Son of God'). Ancient historical writing routinely records different portions or paraphrases of the same speech across different sources without claiming exhaustive verbatim transcription; both statements are compatible facets of a single astonished reaction.

### 34. To whom did Jesus make his first post-resurrection appearance?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The general question of harmonizing the resurrection-morning accounts across all four Gospels is addressed at length elsewhere in this document (see the entry on the differing resurrection-morning details across Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John); this entry addresses the more specific question of appearance sequencing. Most commentators hold that the women (led by Mary Magdalene) were, in strict chronological order, the very first to see the risen Jesus, exactly as the Gospel narratives consistently place their encounter earliest in the timeline of that morning. Luke 24:34's report that 'the Lord... hath appeared to Simon' and Paul's list in 1 Corinthians 15:5-8 are best understood not as chronological claims about who saw Jesus literally first, but as functioning within a different convention -- a formal, quasi-legal listing of significant, named apostolic witnesses whose testimony carried particular weight in the social and legal context of the time (women's testimony was not generally considered legally weighty in first-century Jewish or Roman contexts), beginning with Peter as the most prominent named apostolic witness for that specific purpose, rather than asserting that no one, including the women, saw the risen Christ before Peter did.

## Numbers and Text

### 35. 'Fourteen generations' three times -- yet the third set contains only thirteen names

**References:** Matthew 1:17

**The claim:** Matthew 1:17 explicitly states his genealogy divides into three sets of fourteen generations each: Abraham to David, David to the Babylonian deportation, and the deportation to Christ. Yet counting the actual names listed in the third set (1:12-16) yields only thirteen, not fourteen -- a genuine, checkable internal arithmetic discrepancy within the space of a few verses.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 1:17 exactly as given, without adjusting the count or supplying an additional name; this is a matter of compositional interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a widely discussed puzzle, and this entry presents the range of proposed solutions rather than asserting a single certain answer. The most commonly favored solution among conservative commentators holds that David himself is deliberately counted twice -- once as the fourteenth and final name closing the first set (Abraham to David), and again as the first name opening the second set (David to the deportation) -- a reading Matthew's own wording in 1:17 supports, since 'David' is explicitly named as the hinge point closing one set and opening the next; on this reading, the parallel hinge for the third set is not a named individual but the deportation itself, treated as its own 'generation' opening that final set, meaning the discrepancy is not a matter of a name being dropped but of the third set's own hinge being an event rather than a person, more easily overlooked when simply counting names. A related and complementary explanation, favored by other commentators, notes Matthew explicitly stresses the number fourteen because it is the numerical value of the Hebrew consonants in David's own name via the ancient practice of gematria (each Hebrew letter assigned a numerical value), meaning the number's symbolic, David-emphasizing significance may have mattered more to Matthew's own compositional purpose than an exhaustive, independently verifiable head count of every individual generation -- a reading the more critical scholar Bart Ehrman also accepts as the most likely explanation, while treating it as evidence of deliberate authorial 'manipulation' of the numbers for theological effect rather than a strictly literal genealogical record. A further, distinct proposal holds that a specific name (Jehoiakim, Josiah's son and Jeconiah's father, independently attested in 1 Chronicles 3:15-16) was omitted from Matthew's own list of the exilic-period kings, and that restoring this name to the appropriate set would resolve the count without requiring David to be counted twice. This project does not adjudicate between these proposed solutions, noting that multiple serious explanations exist and that none requires positing a simple, uncorrected scribal error.

### 37. Two demon-possessed men at Gadara -- or one, according to Mark and Luke?

**References:** Matthew 8:28-34; Mark 5:1-20; Luke 8:26-39

**The claim:** Matthew's account of Jesus casting demons into a herd of pigs near Gadara describes two demon-possessed men coming out to meet Jesus (8:28). Mark 5:1-20 and Luke 8:26-39, describing what is evidently the same event (matching setting, demons requesting entry into the pigs, and the resulting stampede), each describe only one demon-possessed man.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew's account of two demon-possessed men, and Mark's and Luke's accounts of one, exactly as given in each Gospel, without harmonizing the number in the text itself; this is a matter of narrative interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of several numerical discrepancies of this specific kind addressed in this Gospel (compare entry 10 below, regarding the blind men at Jericho), and the standard harmonization applied to all such cases holds that where two individuals are present, an account mentioning only one does not thereby deny the second person's presence, but simply focuses its narrative attention on the more prominent, more vocal, or more narratively significant of the two (Mark and Luke's shared focus on a single, particularly severely afflicted individual -- whom Mark's fuller account describes in unusually vivid detail, living among the tombs, cutting himself with stones, and unable to be bound even with chains, Mark 5:2-5 -- suggests this individual's condition was sufficiently dramatic and narratively compelling to warrant Mark and Luke's undivided focus, without either author making an affirmative claim that no second person was present). This 'two vs. one' pattern, where Matthew's accounts specify a pair while Mark and Luke's parallel accounts describe only one member of that pair, recurs at least twice in this Gospel (see entry 9) and is generally treated by commentators as reflecting each author's own selective narrative compression rather than a factual disagreement about how many people were actually present.

On the separate detail of whether the healing occurred as Jesus entered or left Jericho, most commentators note that first-century Jericho actually comprised two distinct adjacent settlements (an older site and a newer, Herodian-era site nearby), meaning it is entirely plausible for Jesus to have been described as simultaneously 'leaving' one Jericho and 'approaching' the other, reconciling this specific detail without requiring either account to be mistaken.

## Theological Interpretation

### 42. 'On this rock I will build my church' -- material unique to Matthew, with major doctrinal significance

**References:** Matthew 16:13-20; Mark 8:27-30; Luke 9:18-21

**The claim:** Matthew's account of Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi includes an extended response from Jesus not found in either Mark's or Luke's parallel accounts of the same episode: 'you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven' (16:18-19) -- language of major and lasting doctrinal significance (forming a central scriptural basis for later Catholic teaching on papal primacy and apostolic succession) that Mark and Luke's parallel accounts of the same conversation simply do not include, raising a question of whether this represents an authentic saying the other two Synoptic authors chose to omit, or a later addition reflecting Matthew's own particular theological interests.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 16:13-20, Mark 8:27-30, and Luke 9:18-21 plainly and consistently as given, without adding or removing the distinctive material in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and theological interpretation bound up with contested denominational questions, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a matter of ongoing scholarly and, in places, denominational discussion, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than adjudicating the question definitively. Most commentators across the theological spectrum agree the specific wording and theological weight of this passage are distinctively Matthean, consistent with this Gospel's demonstrated broader interest, relative to Mark and Luke, in questions of church authority and structure -- Matthew is the only Gospel to use the word 'church' (ekklesia) at all, here and again at 18:17. The genuine disagreement concerns whether this reflects Matthew's own selective inclusion of an authentic saying the other evangelists, for their own reasons, chose not to record. Most conservative and Catholic scholars hold this position, noting that an author's silence about an event or saying does not constitute a denial that it occurred, a principle this project has applied at several other points to differing Gospel content. Others hold that it instead reflects a later theological development within the early church's own tradition being read back into the Gospel narrative by Matthew's specific community, a position more common among critical scholars questioning the saying's authenticity as spoken by the historical Jesus in this specific form. This project does not adjudicate this question, which remains directly bound up with broader, actively and sincerely contested denominational questions regarding church governance and authority.

### 43. Does Jesus bring peace, or a sword?

**References:** Matthew 10:34-36; Luke 2:14; Luke 12:51; John 14:27

**The claim:** At Jesus' birth, the angels proclaim 'on earth peace' (Luke 2:14), and Jesus himself later tells his disciples, 'Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you' (John 14:27). Yet in sending out the Twelve, Jesus states directly, 'Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth. I have not come to bring peace, but a sword' (Matthew 10:34), going on to describe setting family members against each other. Luke's parallel version of the same saying (Luke 12:51) uses 'division' rather than 'sword.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 10:34 and its parallel in Luke 12:51 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of interpreting the saying's meaning and scope, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization distinguishes between the peace Jesus's kingdom ultimately brings (reconciliation with God, and eventually a fully restored creation) and the immediate social consequence of proclaiming that kingdom in a world that does not uniformly receive it: division, even within families, between those who follow Jesus and those who do not. Luke's parallel wording, 'division' rather than 'sword,' is widely read as clarifying Matthew's own intended sense -- the 'sword' is figurative, describing conflict and separation caused by responses to Jesus's message, not a literal weapon or an endorsement of violence by his followers (consistent with Jesus's rebuke of Peter's literal sword use at his arrest, Matthew 26:52). Jesus's peace, then, is offered genuinely and without contradiction, while the temporal, social cost of receiving or rejecting it is realistically acknowledged in the same body of teaching.

### 44. Is the Old Testament Law abolished, or upheld by Jesus?

**References:** Matthew 5:17-18; Romans 10:4; Ephesians 2:15; Hebrews 7:18

**The claim:** Matthew 5:17-18 has Jesus declare, 'Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them... until heaven and earth pass away, not an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law.' Yet Paul writes that 'Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes' (Romans 10:4), that Christ has 'abolish[ed] the law of commandments expressed in ordinances' (Ephesians 2:15), and Hebrews describes 'a former commandment... set aside' (Hebrews 7:18) -- language that, on its face, sounds like abolition rather than fulfillment.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 5:17-18, Romans 10:4, Ephesians 2:15, and Hebrews 7:18 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of theological interpretation regarding the Law's continuing role after Christ, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Mainstream Christian theology generally distinguishes several dimensions of the Mosaic Law -- moral, ceremonial, and civil -- and argues that Jesus's 'fulfillment' operates differently across them: he is understood to perfectly embody and complete the Law's moral demands and prophetic anticipations (its sacrificial system pointing to his own atoning death, its ceremonial distinctions pointing to a holiness now achieved in him), while its ceremonial and civil provisions, tied to Israel's particular covenant administration, are considered to have reached their intended goal and are no longer binding in the same way on believers under the new covenant. On this reading, Paul and Hebrews describe the ending of the Law's role as the basis for righteousness before God and as a distinct covenant administration, not a claim that Jesus's own teaching in Matthew 5:17-18 was mistaken or reversed; Jesus's statement that he 'fulfills' rather than 'abolishes' the Law is itself frequently read as anticipating exactly this outcome, since fulfillment implies completion and transformation rather than either simple continuation or simple cancellation.

### 45. 'Eye for eye' vs. 'turn the other cheek'

**References:** Exodus 21:24; Leviticus 24:20; Matthew 5:38-39

**The claim:** The Mosaic law states the principle of proportional retribution, 'eye for eye, tooth for tooth' (Exodus 21:24; Leviticus 24:20), traditionally called the lex talionis. Jesus, in the Sermon on the Mount, appears to directly reverse this: 'You have heard that it was said, "An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth." But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil. But if anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also' (Matthew 5:38-39).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Exodus 21:24, Leviticus 24:20, and Matthew 5:38-39 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of interpreting the relationship between judicial and personal ethics, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read the two passages as addressing different domains rather than contradicting each other. The lex talionis in Exodus and Leviticus was a principle for courts and public justice, established specifically to limit revenge by capping punishment at proportional retribution rather than escalating retaliation (an advance on ancient Near Eastern norms, not a license for personal vengeance -- it was addressed to judges, not to private individuals settling their own grievances). Jesus's teaching in Matthew 5:38-39 is addressed to individual, personal conduct -- how a disciple should respond to a personal insult or grievance -- not a revision of criminal law or a claim that courts should no longer administer proportional justice. Read this way, Jesus is not overturning the Old Testament's own principle but correcting its misapplication as a license for personal retaliation, a distinction consistent with his broader statement, immediately prior in the same sermon, that he came to fulfill rather than abolish the Law (Matthew 5:17).

### 46. Is every sin forgivable, or is one sin unforgivable?

**References:** Matthew 12:31-32; Mark 3:28-29; Luke 12:10; 1 John 1:9

**The claim:** Jesus states, 'every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven people, but the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven... whoever speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the age to come' (Matthew 12:31-32; paralleled in Mark 3:28-29 and Luke 12:10). Yet 1 John 1:9 states without qualification, 'If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness' -- seemingly without exception.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 12:31-32 and 1 John 1:9 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of theological interpretation regarding the nature of the unforgivable sin, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that these two passages describe different situations rather than contradicting each other. 1 John 1:9 is addressed to believers already walking in fellowship with God, assuring them that genuine confession is met with genuine forgiveness -- it is not making a claim about someone who has permanently and definitively rejected the Spirit's testimony about Christ. The 'blasphemy against the Holy Spirit' in the Synoptic passages, spoken in a context where religious leaders were attributing Jesus's Spirit-empowered works to Satan (Matthew 12:24), is widely understood not as any single sinful utterance but as a settled, final state of willful, informed rejection of the Spirit's witness to Jesus -- a hardness of heart so complete that repentance (and therefore the confession 1 John 1:9 describes) no longer occurs. On this reading, the very fact that someone is troubled by the possibility of having committed this sin is itself commonly taken as evidence they have not, since the sin by definition involves the absence of any remaining capacity or desire for repentance.

### 47. Two versions of the Lord's Prayer

**References:** Matthew 6:9-13; Luke 11:2-4

**The claim:** Matthew's Sermon on the Mount includes the Lord's Prayer in a longer form, with a closing doxology ('For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen') present in many manuscripts. Luke's version, given in response to a direct request from the disciples in a different narrative setting, is notably shorter, omitting several phrases present in Matthew's version (including, in the oldest manuscripts, 'who art in heaven,' 'thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven,' and 'but deliver us from evil') and using 'sins' rather than Matthew's 'debts.'

**How the ten translations render it:** Translations vary in whether they include the closing doxology in Matthew 6:13 in the main text or relegate it to a footnote, reflecting a genuine manuscript variant (the doxology is absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts of Matthew but appears already in the Didache, an early second-century Christian text, and in the Byzantine textual tradition underlying the Textus Receptus); translations otherwise render both passages' core content plainly and consistently as given.

**Reconciliation:** Most scholars treat Matthew's and Luke's versions as records of two distinct occasions rather than two accounts of the identical event: Matthew situates the prayer within the Sermon on the Mount, delivered to a crowd, while Luke explicitly frames his shorter version as being given later, in direct response to a specific request from the disciples ('Lord, teach us to pray,' Luke 11:1) after they observed Jesus praying privately. On this reading, Jesus quite plausibly taught a similar model prayer more than once, in different settings and with some natural variation in wording and length, rather than the Gospels giving conflicting reports of a single fixed liturgical text. The doxology's absence from Luke and from the oldest manuscripts of Matthew is a separate, textual-critical question: most manuscript scholars regard it as a liturgical addition that entered the transmission of Matthew's text early (by the time of the Didache) but after the Gospel's original composition, rather than as an authentic saying of Jesus omitted by Luke.

### 48. Who bought the Field of Blood, and why is it so named?

**References:** Matthew 27:6-10; Acts 1:18-19

**The claim:** Matthew 27:6-8 states that the chief priests, unwilling to put Judas's returned blood-money into the temple treasury, used it themselves to buy 'the potter's field' as a burial ground for foreigners, 'therefore that field has been called the Field of Blood to this day.' Acts 1:18-19, in Peter's speech, instead states that Judas himself, 'with the reward of his wickedness, bought a field,' and that the field was called 'Field of Blood' (Akeldama) because of the manner of his own death there.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 27:6-8 and Acts 1:18-19 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of harmonizing two accounts' differing points of emphasis, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The two accounts are commonly read as complementary rather than contradictory, each reflecting a different angle on the same underlying event and its naming. On the purchase: since the priests considered the money still legally Judas's blood-money even after he threw it back at them, using it to buy the field could accurately be described in Acts, from a broader perspective, as Judas himself having effectively purchased it with his ill-gotten reward, even though the priests carried out the physical transaction he made possible. On the naming: Matthew explains the name from the field's purpose (bought with blood money), while Acts explains it from the manner of Judas's own bloody death there -- both plausibly contributed to the same name becoming attached to the same plot of land, without either account needing to be regarded as mistaken.

### 49. Was the tomb's stone already rolled away, or rolled away in the women's presence?

**References:** Matthew 28:1-6; Mark 16:4; Luke 24:2; John 20:1

**The claim:** Matthew 28:2 describes an angel descending, rolling back the stone, and sitting on it -- narrated in connection with the women's arrival at the tomb. Mark 16:4, Luke 24:2, and John 20:1, by contrast, each describe the stone as already rolled away when the women arrive, without narrating the moment of its removal.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 28:2 and the parallel accounts plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of narrative sequencing across independent, complementary accounts, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that Matthew's account, read carefully, does not actually claim the women witnessed the stone being rolled away as it happened -- his narrative describes the earthquake and the angel's action as having occurred (most naturally, at or before dawn), and the women then arrive to find the situation Matthew has just described, matching Mark, Luke, and John's simple statement that they found the stone already moved. Matthew's Gospel, addressed to an audience wanting to know how and why the stone was moved, includes the angelic action as backstory explaining the empty tomb the women (and all four Gospels) agree they discovered; it need not be read as claiming the women were eyewitnesses to the stone's actual removal.

### 50. The Great Commission: given in Galilee, or in Jerusalem?

**References:** Matthew 28:16-20; Luke 24:44-49; Acts 1:4-9

**The claim:** Matthew 28:16-20 narrates Jesus's commissioning of the disciples ('Go therefore and make disciples of all nations...') on 'the mountain to which Jesus had directed them' in Galilee. Luke 24:44-49 and Acts 1:4-9, by contrast, narrate Jesus's final instructions to the disciples -- including a command to remain in Jerusalem until empowered by the Spirit -- in and around Jerusalem, immediately before the ascension.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Matthew 28:16-20 and the parallel Luke/Acts passages plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of harmonizing the sequence of Jesus's post-resurrection appearances, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that these are not competing accounts of a single event but separate incidents within the forty days between the resurrection and the ascension (Acts 1:3 explicitly states Jesus appeared to the disciples 'during forty days'). On this reading, Jesus commissioned the disciples in Galilee as Matthew records (consistent with the angel's and Jesus's own instructions for the disciples to go there, Matthew 28:7, 10), and the disciples subsequently returned to Jerusalem, where Jesus gave further final instructions -- including the command to wait for the Spirit -- shortly before his ascension from the Mount of Olives near Jerusalem (Acts 1:9-12). Luke's own Gospel compresses this forty-day period considerably (Luke 24 reads, on its surface, as though the resurrection, appearances, and ascension occur within a single day), a literary compression Luke's own second volume, Acts, immediately corrects by supplying the forty-day figure -- meaning Luke-Acts read as a whole does not actually require all these events to have occurred in one location or on one day.

### 51. The twelve apostles: Thaddaeus, or Judas son of James?

**References:** Matthew 10:2-4; Mark 3:16-19; Luke 6:14-16; Acts 1:13

**The claim:** Matthew's and Mark's lists of the twelve apostles both include a 'Thaddaeus' among the twelve. Luke's Gospel and the list repeated in Acts 1:13, in the same position in an otherwise nearly identical list, instead name 'Judas the son of James.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the four apostolic lists plainly and consistently as given; this reflects the same underlying question of whether Thaddaeus and Judas son of James are the same or different individuals, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard and widely accepted harmonization holds that Thaddaeus and 'Judas the son of James' (also called 'Judas, not Iscariot' in John 14:22) are the same individual under two different names, a common practice in first-century Judea where individuals were often known by more than one name (compare Simon/Peter, Levi/Matthew, and Saul/Paul elsewhere in the New Testament). This reading is strengthened by the fact that both names occupy the identical position in each Gospel's otherwise closely parallel list of the twelve, and that no list anywhere names both a 'Thaddaeus' and a separate 'Judas son of James' as two distinct apostles alongside each other -- the two names are always presented as alternatives for the same individual, never as two different people appearing together.

### 52. The order of Jesus' wilderness temptations differs between Matthew and Luke

**References:** Matthew 4:1-11; Luke 4:1-13

**The claim:** Matthew and Luke both record three temptations Jesus faced in the wilderness -- turning stones to bread, a display at the temple's pinnacle, and an offer of 'all the kingdoms of the world' -- but they narrate the second and third temptations in reverse order relative to each other: Matthew has bread-temple-kingdoms, while Luke has bread-kingdoms-temple.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both accounts' sequences plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of harmonizing the two Gospels' differing arrangements, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not regard this as a factual contradiction, since neither Gospel claims to be providing a strict, exhaustive chronological account bound by a fixed narrative order in every detail -- ancient biographical writing commonly arranged material topically or thematically as well as chronologically. A widely offered explanation observes that Matthew's Gospel, written with a strong interest in connecting Jesus to the story of Israel and the Temple, may have arranged the temptations to climax at the Temple itself (a location of particular significance to Matthew's Jewish audience), while Luke's Gospel, which as a whole builds steadily toward Jesus's final journey to and arrival in Jerusalem, may have arranged his account to climax there instead, at 'the pinnacle of the temple' in Jerusalem, foreshadowing that later, central movement of his narrative. On this reading, the differing order reflects each author's distinct literary and theological emphasis rather than a disagreement about what actually happened.

## Ethical Tensions

### 54. How should enemies be treated?

**References:** Matthew 5:43-44; Psalm 137:8-9; Deuteronomy 7:1-2

**The claim:** Matthew 5:43-44 has Jesus command, 'Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you.' This is set by some against Old Testament texts describing hostility toward enemies, including the imprecatory psalms (addressed at length in the companion Psalms document) and the commanded conquest of the Canaanite nations (addressed in the companion Deuteronomy and Joshua documents).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question touches the same ground addressed at length elsewhere in this project regarding the imprecatory psalms (entrusting vengeance to God rather than acting on personal animosity) and the conquest commands (a specific, bounded, divinely-directed judgment on particular nations at a particular point in redemptive history, not a standing personal ethic toward one's own enemies). Jesus' command in Matthew 5:44 addresses the ordinary, personal ethic incumbent on his followers in their individual relationships -- refusing personal retaliation and actively seeking the good of those who mistreat them -- and stands in substantial continuity with Old Testament wisdom and law that likewise commanded kindness toward a personal enemy (Exodus 23:4-5; Proverbs 25:21-22, which Paul quotes approvingly in Romans 12:20). Neither the imprecatory psalms' entrusting of ultimate justice to God nor the conquest narratives' specific, bounded historical judgments function as a general permission for personal vengeance that Jesus' teaching would need to overturn.

### 55. How should publicans (tax collectors) be treated?

**References:** Matthew 9:10-11; Matthew 18:17

**The claim:** Matthew 9:10-11 has Jesus freely eating with 'publicans and sinners,' provoking criticism from the Pharisees that he associates with such people. Matthew 18:17, however, has Jesus instruct that an unrepentant, disciplined church member should be treated 'as an heathen man and a publican' -- language using 'publican' as a byword for someone to be avoided or treated as an outsider.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These describe different situations rather than competing standards: Matthew 9:10-13 describes Jesus' own redemptive mission of seeking out sinners to call them to repentance ('I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance'), a positive, evangelistic engagement consistent with his stated purpose. Matthew 18:15-17 describes a specific church-discipline procedure for a professing believer who, after repeated private and communal appeals, refuses to repent of a specific sin -- 'as an heathen man and a publican' describes the practical result of that formal process (no longer treated as a member in good standing of the covenant community) rather than a claim that tax collectors as such should be shunned or that Jesus' own practice of seeking out sinners for redemptive engagement was mistaken. Seeking out the lost to call them to repentance and formally treating an unrepentant, already-disciplined member as outside the community are compatible parts of the same redemptive framework.

### 56. Does Jesus' citation of the death penalty for cursing one's parents mean the Bible approves of capital punishment today?

**References:** Matthew 15:3-4

**The claim:** Matthew 15:3-4 has Jesus cite the Mosaic law's death penalty for cursing one's father or mother (Exodus 21:17) in the course of rebuking the Pharisees' traditions.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question touches the same ground addressed at length elsewhere in this project regarding Old Testament civil penalties and their relationship to the New Testament era (see the companion Leviticus document's entries on Levitical capital penalties, and the Deuteronomy document's entry on the 'curse of the law'). Jesus' citation here serves a specific rhetorical purpose -- demonstrating that the Pharisees' own human tradition ('Corban,' allowing a technical vow to override the commandment to honor one's parents) actually violated the very Mosaic law they claimed to uphold, a point about their internal inconsistency and hypocrisy rather than a personal endorsement of executing this particular penalty under the new covenant. Citing an Old Testament law to expose a specific instance of hypocrisy is not the same as prescribing that law's civil penalties as a program for New Testament-era believers or societies to implement.

### 57. Should believers do what the Pharisees teach, given Jesus' other direct corrections of Pharisaic teaching?

**References:** Matthew 23:1-3

**The claim:** Matthew 23:2-3 has Jesus instruct the crowds and his disciples, 'The scribes and the Pharisees sit in Moses' seat: all therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works.' Elsewhere, Jesus repeatedly and directly corrects specific Pharisaic teachings and traditions (regarding divorce, Sabbath observance, and ceremonial hand-washing, among others).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jesus' instruction in 23:2-3 is best read as referring specifically to the Pharisees' formal role and legitimate function in publicly teaching and reading the Mosaic law itself from their recognized position of authority ('Moses' seat') -- when they were accurately conveying the actual content of the Law of Moses, their hearers were right to heed that teaching regardless of the teachers' own hypocrisy in living it out ('do not ye after their works'), since the validity of the Law taught does not depend on the moral character of the one reading or explaining it. This is a distinct matter from the Pharisees' own additional, extra-biblical human traditions and specific interpretive errors -- their added, burdensome traditions (Mark 7:1-13) and specific misapplications of the Law (their treatment of divorce, Sabbath rules, and ceremonial purity) -- which Jesus directly and repeatedly corrects elsewhere as departures from, rather than accurate teaching of, Moses' own law. The instruction to heed their formal teaching of Scripture from their recognized seat and the correction of their specific traditions and hypocritical practices address two different categories of their activity.

## Theological Tensions

### 58. Should the gospel be preached to everyone, or only to Israel?

**References:** Matthew 10:5-6; Matthew 28:19-20

**The claim:** Matthew 10:5-6 has Jesus instruct the twelve apostles, on this specific early mission, 'Go not into the way of the Gentiles, and into any city of the Samaritans enter ye not: but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.' Later, the Great Commission instructs the same apostles to 'teach all nations' (28:19-20).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These are sequential, not competing, commissions reflecting the unfolding stages of the church's mission across salvation history, a progression the New Testament describes explicitly elsewhere (Romans 1:16's 'to the Jew first, and also to the Greek'; Acts 1:8's geographic expansion from Jerusalem outward 'unto the uttermost part of the earth'): the initial, limited mission of Matthew 10 was a specific, bounded assignment for a specific, early phase of Jesus' own earthly ministry, focused on Israel as the initial recipients of the kingdom's proclamation consistent with God's covenant priorities; the Great Commission, given after Jesus' resurrection and at the transition to the church's post-ascension, worldwide mission, explicitly and deliberately expands the scope to all nations -- a later, superseding instruction appropriate to a later stage of the same unfolding redemptive plan, not a contradiction of the earlier, temporary restriction.

### 59. Did Jesus have secret teachings, given his own statement that he taught nothing in secret?

**References:** Matthew 13:10-11; John 18:20

**The claim:** Matthew 13:10-11 has Jesus tell his disciples privately, 'it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given,' explaining his use of parables partly to conceal meaning from the crowds. John 18:20, before the high priest, has Jesus state, 'I spake openly to the world... and in secret have I said nothing.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jesus' statement in John 18:20 is best read in its specific context -- responding to an implied accusation of clandestine, conspiratorial teaching (the kind of secret plotting his accusers were attempting to pin on him) -- affirming that his public ministry and core message were conducted openly, in synagogues and the temple, before the general public, without any secret, hidden agenda or seditious plot. This is compatible with Matthew 13's description of Jesus giving fuller, more detailed private explanation of his parables' meaning specifically to his committed disciples, a normal pattern of any public teacher who also mentors a smaller group of committed students in greater depth -- deeper explanation given privately to disciples already following him is not the kind of 'secret' teaching (concealed, illegitimate, or conspiratorial) John 18:20 is denying.

### 60. Is childlikeness a good thing to have, or a sign of immaturity to outgrow?

**References:** Matthew 18:3; 1 Corinthians 13:11; 1 Corinthians 14:20

**The claim:** Matthew 18:3 has Jesus say, 'Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven.' Paul, however, writes, 'when I became a man, I put away childish things' (1 Corinthians 13:11), and instructs, 'be not children in understanding... but in understanding be men' (1 Corinthians 14:20).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jesus and Paul commend and discourage different aspects of childhood, addressing different questions: Jesus' statement specifically commends a child's characteristic humility, trust, and lack of self-important status-seeking (the immediate context, Matthew 18:1-4, is the disciples' dispute over who is greatest in the kingdom, which Jesus corrects with a child's example of humble, unpretentious dependence) -- qualities of the heart worth retaining and even recovering as an adult. Paul's statements specifically address immaturity in reasoning, understanding, and judgment (1 Corinthians 13:11's context is spiritual gifts exercised without love; 14:20's context is exercising sound judgment about spiritual gifts in worship) -- a different, intellectual and moral maturity that should indeed increase with growth. Childlike humility and trust (commended) and childish immaturity in reasoning and judgment (discouraged) are not the same quality, and commending one does not require commending the other.

### 61. Is Jesus good, given his own question, 'Why callest thou me good?'

**References:** Matthew 19:16-17

**The claim:** When a rich young man addresses Jesus as 'Good Master,' Jesus responds, 'Why callest thou me good? there is none good but one, that is, God' -- language some read as Jesus denying his own goodness, or denying his own deity.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as Jesus denying his own goodness or deity, but as a probing, Socratic response designed to press the young man to consider the full implication of the flattering title he had used casually: if Jesus is genuinely 'good' in the absolute sense the young man's address implies, and only God is good in that ultimate sense, the young man is being invited to reckon with who Jesus actually is rather than treating 'good' as a merely polite conversational title for a respected teacher. Far from a denial, this reading treats the question as an implicit invitation toward recognizing Jesus' own divine identity, consistent with the rest of the New Testament's consistent affirmation of Jesus' sinless goodness (Hebrews 4:15; 1 Peter 2:22) and deity -- Jesus redirects a casual compliment into a serious theological question rather than making a flat denial of either his goodness or his divine nature.

### 62. How much power did Jesus have, given his statement that certain rewards were 'not mine to give'?

**References:** Matthew 20:23; Matthew 28:18

**The claim:** Matthew 20:23 has Jesus tell James and John that sitting at his right and left hand 'is not mine to give, but it shall be given to them for whom it is prepared of my Father.' Later, the risen Jesus declares, 'All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth' (28:18).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These address different matters: 20:23 concerns a specific, particular allocation -- the assignment of specific positions of honor in the coming kingdom -- which Jesus indicates belongs to the Father's own sovereign plan and prerogative, a matter of the Father's specific will regarding individual assignments rather than a general statement about the extent of Jesus' own power or authority as such. 28:18's declaration of 'all power' concerns Christ's comprehensive authority over creation generally, granted to him following his resurrection and exaltation, and is not contradicted by his acknowledging, within the ordering of the Trinity's shared but distinctly-ordered will, that the Father retains specific prerogative over particular matters such as the precise assignment of individual honors -- a distinction of role and specific prerogative within the Godhead's unified will, not a limitation on Christ's actual power or authority in general.

### 63. Was Jesus a ransom for many, or for all?

**References:** Matthew 20:28; 1 Timothy 2:6

**The claim:** Matthew 20:28 has Jesus describe his mission as giving 'his life a ransom for many.' 1 Timothy 2:6 describes Christ as having 'gave himself a ransom for all.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This touches one of the most substantively and longest debated questions in the history of Christian theology -- the scope of Christ's atonement -- on which serious, Scripture-affirming Christian traditions continue to differ, and this document does not take a side. Some theological traditions (broadly, particular/limited-atonement views within the Reformed tradition) read 'many' in Matthew 20:28 as the more precise statement of the atonement's actual, effectual scope (those who will actually be saved), understanding 'all' in 1 Timothy 2:6 as addressing the atonement's sufficiency and its offer to all people without distinction of nation or class, the letter's own immediate context being a call to pray 'for all men... for kings' (2:1-2) emphasizing the gospel's availability across all categories of people rather than a claim about universal effectual application. Other traditions (broadly, general/unlimited-atonement views, held across a range of denominations) read both texts as consistent statements that Christ's atonement is genuinely sufficient for and offered to all people, with 'many' in Matthew 20:28 simply emphasizing the vast scope of those actually benefited without implying a numerical limitation excluding others. Both readings treat 'many' and 'all' as compatible rather than contradictory, differing only in which text they take as more precisely defining the atonement's intended scope.

### 64. When will the end come, and within what timeframe did Jesus say he would return?

**References:** Matthew 24:14, 34-36

**The claim:** Matthew 24:14 states the gospel must first be preached 'in all the world, for a witness unto all nations; and then shall the end come' -- seemingly describing an extended future timeline. Yet the same discourse states, 'this generation shall not pass, till all these things be fulfilled' (24:34), seemingly describing a near-term timeline within the lifetime of Jesus' own contemporaries, while also stating that 'of that day and hour knoweth no man' (24:36), affirming the timing is ultimately unknowable.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most extensively debated interpretive questions in the New Testament, addressed by several distinct scholarly positions among conservative, Scripture-affirming interpreters, and this document does not adjudicate fully between them. Some interpreters (broadly, a 'preterist' reading) hold that Matthew 24's discourse, or a substantial portion of it, primarily describes events fulfilled within the generation then living -- most notably the Roman destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple in AD 70 -- with 'this generation' referring straightforwardly to Jesus' own contemporaries and 'all nations' and 'all the world' describing the known Roman world of the first century (Colossians 1:23 uses similarly universal-sounding language of the gospel already having been preached 'to every creature under heaven' by Paul's own time). Others (broadly, a 'futurist' reading) hold that the discourse primarily or entirely describes Jesus' still-future final return and the end of the present age, understanding 'this generation' to refer either to the generation that will witness the specific end-time signs just described (rather than Jesus' own contemporaries) or, on a minority reading, to the Jewish people/nation persisting as a people across history until these events. Still others hold a 'both/and' or 'already/not yet' reading, treating the AD 70 destruction as a partial, historical foreshadowing of a fuller, still-future consummation the same discourse also describes. All major positions affirm 24:36's statement that the precise day and hour of the final consummation remains unknown even to Jesus in his human nature (a distinct point from the broader, general timeframe questions the other verses raise), and this document leaves the broader question open as a matter of ongoing, serious eschatological discussion.

### 65. Will Christ's second coming be visible to everyone, given that its exact timing is also said to be unknown and sudden?

**References:** Matthew 24:27, 30, 36-44

**The claim:** Matthew 24:30 describes 'the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with power and great glory,' an event 24:27 compares to lightning flashing across the whole sky -- a vivid, universally visible occurrence. Yet the same discourse describes the timing as unknown and sudden, coming 'as a thief in the night' (compare 24:36-44).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These describe two different dimensions of the same event rather than competing claims: the timing of Christ's return (unpredictable, sudden, unknown in advance, addressed in 24:36-44) and the visibility and manner of the return once it occurs (public, universal, unmistakable, like lightning across the sky, addressed in 24:27, 30) are not in tension -- an event can be both impossible to predict in advance and, once it happens, immediately and universally obvious to everyone, in the same way lightning itself is both unpredictable in its exact timing and, the instant it occurs, visible across the whole sky at once. Nothing in the discourse suggests the event's suddenness implies a secret or partially-hidden occurrence.

### 66. Did Jesus ask God to save him from crucifixion, or did he willingly embrace it from the start?

**References:** Matthew 26:36-39; Matthew 16:21; John 10:17-18

**The claim:** In Gethsemane, Jesus prays, 'O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me' (26:39), a request that could be read as seeking to avoid the crucifixion. Elsewhere, Jesus repeatedly and clearly affirms his settled purpose and full willingness to die (Matthew 16:21's forewarning that he 'must' suffer and die; John 10:17-18's statement that 'no man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is generally understood by conservative theologians through the classical Christian doctrine of Christ's two complete natures and two wills (human and divine), most explicitly worked out in the early church's Christological debates but rooted directly in this very passage: Jesus' genuine human nature experienced a real, natural human aversion to the extreme physical and spiritual suffering ahead (the 'cup,' widely understood to include not merely physical death but bearing the weight of divine judgment for sin), and this prayer gives voice to that genuine human will's natural recoil from suffering -- without this constituting any actual wavering in his settled, ongoing purpose to accomplish the mission he had already repeatedly affirmed. The prayer's own conclusion makes the resolution explicit within the same breath: 'nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt' -- Jesus' human will, however genuinely reluctant, is entirely and immediately submitted to the Father's will, resulting in full, willing acceptance of the cross rather than any actual attempt to avoid it, consistent with his unwavering march toward Jerusalem and the cross throughout the rest of the narrative.

## Names and Identity

### 67. Was the tax collector Jesus called Matthew, or Levi?

**References:** Matthew 9:9; Mark 2:14; Luke 5:27

**The claim:** Matthew 9:9 names the tax collector called by Jesus 'Matthew.' Mark 2:14 and Luke 5:27, describing what is clearly the same event (the same location, the same subsequent meal with 'publicans and sinners'), instead name him 'Levi.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a straightforward and widely recognized case of an individual known by two names, a well-attested and unremarkable practice throughout the Gospels and the wider biblical historical books (compare Simon/Peter, and the several double-named figures addressed elsewhere in this project) -- 'Levi' was most likely this disciple's given Jewish name, with 'Matthew' (meaning 'gift of God') either an additional name he also carried or a name he adopted upon becoming a disciple, similar to how Simon received the added name Peter. Matthew's own Gospel, traditionally attributed to this same disciple, uses his adopted name 'Matthew' throughout, including at the formal listing of the twelve apostles (Matthew 10:3), while Mark and Luke use his other name Levi for the calling narrative specifically.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 68. When was Jesus rejected by the people of his hometown, Nazareth?

**References:** Matthew 13:53-58; Luke 4:16-30

**The claim:** Matthew 13:53-58 (paralleled in Mark 6:1-6) places Jesus' rejection at Nazareth relatively late, well into his Galilean ministry, after extensive teaching and healing elsewhere. Luke 4:16-30 places an apparently similar rejection scene at Nazareth at the very outset of Jesus' public ministry, immediately following his wilderness temptation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators hold one of two positions: some read this as two separate visits to Nazareth and two distinct occasions of rejection, an itinerant teacher returning to his hometown more than once over the course of an extended ministry being entirely unremarkable, with Luke recording an earlier visit and Matthew/Mark a later one; others read Luke's placement as a deliberate literary and thematic choice rather than a strict chronological claim -- Luke's Gospel is widely recognized as arranging some material topically or programmatically rather than strictly chronologically (Luke's own preface, 1:3, describes writing 'in order' in the sense of an organized, reasoned account rather than necessarily a strict chronicle), placing this scene early as a thematic overture that previews the rejection-by-his-own-people theme that runs throughout the Gospel, describing the same single historical event Matthew and Mark place later, in its actual chronological position. Either reading resolves the apparent tension without requiring either Gospel to be historically mistaken.

### 69. Was Jesus crucified at the third hour, or the sixth?

**References:** Matthew 27:45-46; Mark 15:25; John 19:14

**The claim:** Mark 15:25 states Jesus was crucified at 'the third hour' (approximately 9 a.m.), consistent with Matthew 27:45's description of darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour presupposing an earlier crucifixion. John 19:14, however, describes Pilate still presenting Jesus for judgment 'about the sixth hour' (approximately noon) -- apparently placing the crucifixion itself several hours later than the Synoptic timeline allows.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated chronological question, and this document presents it candidly rather than claiming full certainty, consistent with this project's practice elsewhere when the evidence does not clearly settle a question. Two main explanations are commonly offered: some scholars propose that John, writing later and for a different (likely more Greco-Roman) audience, uses Roman civil time-reckoning (counting from midnight) rather than the Jewish reckoning (counting from sunrise) the Synoptics use elsewhere, which would place John's 'sixth hour' at approximately 6 a.m. -- comfortably before the Synoptics' 9 a.m. crucifixion time, resolving the apparent conflict; other scholars hold that ancient hour-reckoning generally was an approximate, rough division of the day rather than a precise clock-time, making a few hours' variance between independent eyewitness traditions or sources unremarkable and not indicative of a genuine factual conflict. Both explanations remain live possibilities among careful, conservative scholars, and no single account has achieved full consensus.
