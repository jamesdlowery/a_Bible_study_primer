# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Baruch

**A note on this document's place in the series:** As with Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of
Solomon, and Sirach, Baruch is a deuterocanonical book included in only one of the ten
translations surveyed in this project: RSV2CE, a Catholic edition, which includes the "Letter
of Jeremiah" as its sixth chapter. The other nine (AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV,
NLT) follow the Protestant and Jewish canon, which excludes it. This document notes, for each
entry, that nine of the ten translations do not contain the book at all.

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical,
literary, and theological questions involving Baruch, either entirely internal to the book or
in comparison with Daniel, Jeremiah, and John. For each item, this document states the claim as
it is typically raised and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation.

**A note on this book's connections to other documents in this series:** Entry 1 addresses the
same Belshazzar/Nebuchadnezzar genealogical detail already treated at length in the companion
Daniel document, and notes that Baruch's own author most likely drew this detail directly from
Daniel's text. Entry 5 connects to the companion Jeremiah document's discussion of the
seventy-year exile prophecy.

**A note on candor:** Entry 3 addresses a genuine internal tension within the book's own
composition (the narrative frame assumes the Temple is standing; the central prayer assumes
it lies in ruins), acknowledged directly in mainstream critical commentary including the
Jerome Biblical Commentary, and this entry does not claim it is fully resolved.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 4 were both checked against multiple sources, including
official USCCB study notes (which acknowledge the Belshazzar issue directly) and patristic
sources on Baruch 3:37's Incarnational interpretation. The remaining entries are historical and
literary questions addressed from standard scholarly positions.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by relevant scholarship, or, where a genuine unresolved difficulty remains, an honest account
of it. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary text directly.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 1. 'His son Belshazzar' -- the same Nebuchadnezzar/Belshazzar genealogical issue already addressed regarding Daniel

**References:** Baruch 1:11-12; Daniel 5:2, 11, 18, 22

**The claim:** Baruch 1:11-12 records the exiles' instruction to 'pray for the life of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, and for the life of Belshazzar his son... that we may live under the protection of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and of Belshazzar his son.' This is the identical genealogical claim already addressed in this project's companion Daniel document -- historically, Belshazzar was the son of Nabonidus, not Nebuchadnezzar, a detail independently confirmed by cuneiform records including the Nabonidus Cylinder.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Baruch. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Baruch in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Baruch is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders Baruch 1:11-12 as 'Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and for the life of Belshazzar his son,' matching the Greek text underlying all Catholic and Orthodox translations of the book.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Daniel document, which addresses the same claim as it appears in Daniel 5:2, 11, 18, and 22, and offers the same range of proposed explanations there: that 'son' may be used in the loose ancient Near Eastern sense of 'successor' or 'descendant' rather than strict biological parentage, or that Belshazzar's mother may have been a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar (making Nebuchadnezzar his actual maternal grandfather), a connection some historians find plausible though not conclusively proven. The official Catholic USCCB study notes to Baruch acknowledge the issue directly and without evasion, stating plainly that 'Belshazzar was the son of Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, not of Nebuchadnezzar... Belshazzar was co-regent for a few years while his father was away in Arabia' -- treating this as a known feature of the text rather than a point requiring apologetic defense to the contrary. Scholars also note that Baruch's version of this detail, along with several other close correspondences (compare Baruch 1:15-20, 2:1-3, 7-14, 16-19 with Daniel 9:7-11:18), suggests Baruch's author draws directly on Daniel's own text as a source, meaning this specific detail was most likely inherited from Daniel rather than arising as an independent error.

### 2. Baruch's narrative setting among the exiles in Babylon -- yet the historical Baruch is independently attested going to Egypt with Jeremiah

**References:** Baruch 1:1-4; Jeremiah 43:1-7

**The claim:** Baruch's narrative frame consistently places its author among the Jewish exile community in Babylon, reading his scroll there and corresponding with Jerusalem from that location (1:1-14). Jeremiah 43:1-7, however, records that after Jerusalem's destruction, the historical Baruch actually accompanied Jeremiah not to Babylon, but to Egypt, along with a group of Judean refugees who forcibly compelled the reluctant prophet and his scribe to go with them -- the last independently attested location of the historical Baruch anywhere in the canonical text.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Baruch. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Baruch in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Baruch is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the full text of Baruch as canonical Scripture, with its narrative frame consistently placing Baruch among the Babylonian exile community throughout chapter 1 (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction within a single account, but as further evidence supporting the standard scholarly reading (addressed in entry 6 above) that the book of Baruch is a later composition written in Baruch's name and persona rather than a genuine autobiographical record by the historical scribe himself -- the book's own author, writing generations or centuries after the historical Baruch's own lifetime, appears to have placed his literary character among the Babylonian exile community (the setting most relevant and meaningful to the book's own intended audience and message of hope for exiles) without this being intended, or received by its original audience, as a claim carefully reconciled against the canonical book of Jeremiah's own more specific and different final report of Baruch's actual, historically attested whereabouts.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 3. A prayer that assumes the Temple lies in ruins -- embedded in a narrative frame where Temple worship continues normally

**References:** Baruch 1:10, 14; Baruch 2:26

**The claim:** Baruch's opening narrative frame has the exiles in Babylon sending money back to Jerusalem specifically 'to procure burnt offerings, sin offerings, and frankincense' for continued sacrificial worship at the Temple (1:10), and instructs that Baruch's accompanying scroll be read publicly 'in the house of the LORD' on feast days (1:14) -- language assuming the Temple is standing and its normal sacrificial worship is ongoing. Yet the extended prayer of confession that follows explicitly presupposes the Temple's destruction: 'the house that is called by your name you have made as it is today, because of the wickedness of the house of Israel' (2:26), referring to the Temple's ruined state as an already-accomplished fact.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Baruch. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Baruch in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Baruch is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (2:26): 'the house that is called by your name you have made as it is today, because of the wickedness of the house of Israel' (verified against the published text), alongside 1:10's instruction to purchase offerings for ongoing Temple worship.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely noted internal tension, acknowledged directly in standard critical commentary (including the Jerome Biblical Commentary, which states plainly that 'there is a contradiction between the prayer itself, which presumes that the Temple is in ruins... and the introduction, which presumes that the Temple is standing and that the normal worship is carried on there'), and this entry does not attempt to fully resolve it. Some commentators propose the book's opening narrative frame and its central prayer may derive from two originally separate compositions, or separate stages of composition, later combined by an editor without fully harmonizing this specific detail; others note Jeremiah 41:5 itself records that some people continued offering sacrifices at the Temple site even after its destruction by Nebuchadnezzar, which could, on a more charitable reading, allow 1:10's instruction to refer to this kind of continued, albeit diminished, worship activity at the ruined site rather than requiring a fully standing, functioning Temple -- though this explanation does not fully resolve 1:14's own apparent assumption of normal, ongoing Temple governance. This entry is presented candidly as a genuine internal difficulty within the book's own composition rather than one with a single, fully satisfying resolution.

## Theological Interpretation

### 4. 'Afterward she appeared on earth and lived among men' -- language read since antiquity as anticipating the Incarnation

**References:** Baruch 3:36-38; John 1:14

**The claim:** Baruch's hymn to Wisdom concludes with a striking statement: personified Wisdom, having been given by God to Israel, 'afterward... appeared on earth and lived among men' (3:37, in some versifications 3:38) -- language bearing a close resemblance to John 1:14's description of the Word becoming flesh and 'dwelling among us.' This verse has been read since the earliest centuries of Christian interpretation as a prophetic anticipation of the Incarnation, raising the same general question addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding personified Wisdom's relationship to New Testament Christology (Proverbs 8, and the Wisdom of Solomon 7, both addressed in their own companion documents).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Baruch. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Baruch in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Baruch is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (3:37): 'Afterward she appeared upon earth and lived among men' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This verse has an unusually rich and well-documented history of patristic Christological interpretation -- early Church Fathers including Origen, Tertullian, Cyprian, and Lactantius all read this passage as prophetic testimony to Christ's incarnation, and the passage remains liturgically prominent to this day in Eastern Orthodox and Byzantine-rite Catholic worship, appointed for reading at the Royal Hours of Christmas Eve and Nativity vespers specifically because of its perceived Incarnational significance. As with the comparable personified-Wisdom passages in Proverbs 8 and the Wisdom of Solomon, this project does not adjudicate whether the original author intended this specific verse as direct predictive prophecy of Jesus' incarnation specifically, or whether later Christian tradition draws out and applies, to Christ specifically, imagery already developed within the wisdom tradition to describe divine Wisdom's own accessibility and self-disclosure to Israel through the Law (the immediately preceding verses, 3:36-4:1, closely identify Wisdom with 'the book of the commandments of God, and the law that endures forever') -- both readings treat the passage as pointing toward, rather than contradicting, later Christian theological development, differing only on whether that connection was the original author's own conscious intent.

## Chronology

### 5. Jeremiah's seventy years reinterpreted as seven generations

**References:** Baruch 6:2 (Letter of Jeremiah); Jeremiah 25:11-12; Jeremiah 29:10

**The claim:** The 'Letter of Jeremiah' (included as Baruch chapter 6 in RSV2CE and most Catholic editions) opens by stating the exiles would remain in Babylon 'for seven generations,' after which God would bring them out in peace -- reinterpreting Jeremiah's own well-known prophecy of a seventy-year exile (Jeremiah 25:11-12, 29:10, addressed at greater length in the companion Jeremiah document) into a different unit of measurement.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Baruch. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Baruch in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Baruch is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the Letter of Jeremiah as Baruch chapter 6, which opens by stating the exiles would remain in Babylon 'even to seven generations' (verified against the published text; the RSV-Catholic Edition's specific wording here is also directly discussed by Catholic Answers in addressing this same reconciliation question).

**Reconciliation:** Most scholars read this not as a contradiction of Jeremiah's own prophecy, but as a later, alternative way of expressing a roughly similar span of time, using a different but related reckoning convention. If a generation is figured at approximately forty years, a figure with some precedent elsewhere in Scripture (compare Numbers 32:13's forty years for a wilderness generation to pass), seven generations would suggest a considerably longer span than seventy years read as a strict, literal count. Some scholars read this as evidence that 'the Letter of Jeremiah' was composed at a later date than the original prophecy, addressed to a Jewish audience still living under foreign domination well past the seventy-year mark and in need of a reinterpreted framework for continued hope and patience. This is similar to comparable reinterpretations and applications of Jeremiah's seventy-year prophecy found in other Second Temple Jewish literature, most notably Daniel 9:24's own extension of the seventy years into 'seventy weeks of years.' This is generally treated as a documented pattern of later Jewish reflection creatively reapplying and reworking an earlier prophetic timeframe for new circumstances, rather than as a claim that directly competes with or contradicts Jeremiah's own original statement.

## Literary Composition

### 6. Attributed to Jeremiah's own scribe -- yet likely composed centuries after his lifetime

**References:** Baruch 1:1-4

**The claim:** Baruch opens by identifying its author as 'Baruch the son of Neriah' (1:1) -- the same historical figure who served as the prophet Jeremiah's scribe and secretary, prominently featured throughout the book of Jeremiah itself (Jeremiah 32, 36, 43, 45). Most scholars, however, date the actual composition of the book of Baruch considerably later than Baruch's own attested sixth-century-BC lifetime, likely to the Hellenistic or even Hasmonean period (roughly the second to first century BC) based on internal linguistic and thematic evidence, including the reinterpretation of Jeremiah's seventy years addressed in entry 5 above.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Baruch. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Baruch in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Baruch is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the full text of Baruch as canonical Scripture, opening (1:1) with the self-identification 'Baruch the son of Neriah' (verified against the published text); most scholars date the book's actual composition to the Hellenistic or Hasmonean period, considerably later than the historical Baruch's sixth-century-BC lifetime.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated the same way as the comparable pseudonymous or pseudepigraphal authorship questions addressed at greater length elsewhere in this project (regarding the Wisdom of Solomon's attribution to Solomon, addressed in its own companion document) -- writing under the name and in the persona of a revered earlier figure closely associated with a specific prophetic tradition (here, Jeremiah's own scribe, a natural choice given the book's extensive engagement with and dependence on Jeremiah's own themes and, per entry 1 above, apparent direct literary dependence on the book of Daniel as well) was a recognized and unremarkable literary convention in the Second Temple period, intended to invoke that figure's own authority and close historical connection to the events described rather than to make a literal claim of authorship the original audience would necessarily have taken as straightforwardly factual. This project does not adjudicate the precise date of composition, noting only that unlike Sirach's own unusually well-documented, securely dated authorship (addressed in its own companion document), Baruch's specific date and mode of composition remain considerably less certain and are the subject of ongoing scholarly discussion, including whether the book represents a single unified composition or a later combination of separately composed sections (a question also touched on in entry 3 above).
