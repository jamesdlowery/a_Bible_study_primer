# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 Maccabees

**A note on this document's place in the series:** As with the six prior deuterocanonical
books addressed in this series, 2 Maccabees is included in only one of the ten translations
surveyed: RSV2CE, a Catholic edition. The other nine (AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB,
NIV, NLT) follow the Protestant and Jewish canon, which excludes it. This document notes, for
each entry, that nine of the ten translations do not contain the book at all. **This document
completes this project's coverage of the standard Catholic deuterocanonical corpus** (Tobit,
Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, 1 Maccabees, and 2 Maccabees).

This document catalogs 6 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical,
literary, and theological questions involving 2 Maccabees, either entirely internal to the
book or in comparison with 1 Maccabees, the Wisdom of Solomon, Genesis, and Jeremiah. For each
item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised and gives the standard
scholarly reconciliation.

**A note on this book's most significant entries:** Entry 3 provides the fuller treatment
previewed in the companion 1 Maccabees document, addressing this book's markedly more
supernatural and graphic account of Antiochus IV's death. Entry 4 supplies the fuller
treatment of the passage most consistently identified by scholars as the Old Testament era's
clearest witness to creation "out of nothing," previewed in the companion Wisdom of Solomon
document. Entry 5 addresses one of the most doctrinally consequential passages in the entire
deuterocanonical corpus -- the scriptural basis frequently cited for the Catholic doctrine of
purgatory and prayer for the dead -- and is presented with genuine even-handedness between
the Catholic and Protestant positions rather than adjudicating the underlying doctrinal
question.

**Verification status:** Entries 3 and 5 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing both Catholic and Protestant perspectives. The remaining entries are historical
and literary questions addressed from standard scholarly positions.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by relevant scholarship, or, where a genuine and sincerely held doctrinal disagreement exists
between major Christian traditions, an honest, even-handed account of it. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary text directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. An explicitly self-described abridgment of a longer, five-volume work by Jason of Cyrene

**References:** 2 Maccabees 2:19-32; 2 Maccabees 15:37-39

**The claim:** 2 Maccabees openly and explicitly describes its own compositional process in unusual detail: the author states that the events he narrates were originally recorded at much greater length by 'Jason of Cyrene' in 'five volumes,' and that his own task has been to 'epitomize' (abridge and condense) that larger work 'into a single composition' (2:19-23) -- even candidly acknowledging the difficulty of the task and asking the reader's indulgence for any shortcomings in the result (2:24-32), with a similar closing acknowledgment at the book's very end (15:38-39, 'if it is poorly done and mediocre, that was the best I could do').

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 2 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 2 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 2 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE includes the full text of 2 Maccabees as canonical Scripture. It reads (2:23): 'all this, which has been set forth by Jason of Cyrene in five volumes, we shall attempt to condense into a single book,' with the epitomist's candid acknowledgment of the difficulty of the task following in 2:24-32 (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not a point of contradiction, but a distinctive and unusually candid literary feature this project highlights explicitly, since no other book addressed anywhere in this series -- canonical or deuterocanonical -- so directly and repeatedly describes its own status as a deliberate abridgment of a separate, larger source work, complete with an authorial apology for its own perceived literary limitations. This transparency is generally read by scholars as a mark of genuine historiographical honesty rather than a weakness undermining the book's reliability -- the author is explicit about his sources and methods in a manner some scholars compare favorably to conventions found in other Hellenistic historical writing of the period, and his goal (explicitly stated at 2:25) was readability and memorability for a general audience rather than the kind of exhaustive detail Jason's original five-volume work apparently offered, which does not survive independently and cannot be directly compared against this epitome.

### 2. An extra-canonical tradition of Jeremiah hiding the Ark of the Covenant, found nowhere in the canonical book of Jeremiah

**References:** 2 Maccabees 2:1-8; Jeremiah (entire book)

**The claim:** The second of the two prefixed letters that open 2 Maccabees (1:10b-2:18) includes an elaborate account, attributed to 'records' the author cites, describing the prophet Jeremiah hiding the tabernacle, the ark, and the altar of incense in a cave on Mount Nebo shortly before Jerusalem's destruction, instructing his companions that the site should remain unknown 'until God gathers his people together again' (2:1-8) -- a specific, detailed tradition found nowhere in the canonical book of Jeremiah itself, nor anywhere else in the Old Testament's own account of the Babylonian conquest.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 2 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 2 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 2 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (2:4-5): 'the prophet, having received an oracle, ordered that the tent and the ark should follow with him... Jeremiah came and found a cave-dwelling, and he brought there the tent and the ark and the altar of incense; then he sealed up the entrance,' with the site to 'remain unknown until God gathers his people together again' (2:7) (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction of the canonical book of Jeremiah (which simply does not address the Ark's fate one way or the other, leaving room for, rather than excluding, an independent tradition on the subject), but as an example of a broader body of extra-canonical Jewish tradition and legend that developed around unresolved questions the canonical texts leave open -- what became of the Ark of the Covenant after the Babylonian destruction of the Temple is a question the Old Testament itself never directly answers, and multiple independent, sometimes mutually inconsistent traditions addressing this gap developed within Second Temple and later rabbinic Judaism (a comparable case being addressed regarding other unresolved historical or narrative gaps within Scripture at several points elsewhere in this project). This specific tradition attributed to Jeremiah, cited here as if drawing on some other document or record the letter's author had access to, is generally treated by scholars as a specific instance of this wider phenomenon of extra-canonical legendary elaboration filling a gap the canonical text leaves open, rather than as a claim directly contradicting anything the book of Jeremiah itself states.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 3. Antiochus IV's death: two markedly different accounts, addressed here in full following the preview in the companion 1 Maccabees document

**References:** 2 Maccabees 9:1-29; 1 Maccabees 6:1-16

**The claim:** This book's account of the persecutor Antiochus IV Epiphanes' death (9:1-29) differs substantially from the version in 1 Maccabees 6:1-16 (addressed briefly in the companion 1 Maccabees document). 1 Maccabees describes a comparatively restrained, almost sympathetic death from grief and psychological anguish following news of his military failures, including a deathbed speech of regret. This book instead describes God directly and dramatically striking Antiochus with 'an incurable and invisible blow' -- his bowels afflicted with agonizing pain, his body 'swarming with worms' while still alive, his flesh visibly rotting away, and a stench so overwhelming his own army could not bear to carry him (9:5-10) -- a considerably more supernatural, graphic, and theologically emphatic account of direct, immediate divine retribution.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 2 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 2 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 2 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (9:5-9): 'the all-seeing Lord, the God of Israel, struck him an incurable and unseen blow. As soon as he ceased speaking he was seized with a pain in his bowels for which there was no relief and with sharp internal tortures'; and further describes worms and a stench so severe his own army could not bear him (verified against the published text) -- considerably more graphic than 1 Maccabees 6's restrained account.

**Reconciliation:** Modern scholars addressing this specific difference, including detailed academic treatment of what one recent analysis calls 'two versions of the Maccabean story,' generally attribute it to each book's own distinct theological purpose and literary character, rather than treating either as a simple factual report the other merely supplements. This is a stronger and more genuine divergence than several other 'parallel account' cases addressed elsewhere in this project. 1 Maccabees, composed in a restrained, annalistic Hebrew-historiographical style, addressed in its own companion document as one of the more historically reliable ancient sources available, generally avoids supernatural or miraculous elements throughout, consistent with its version of Antiochus's death. 2 Maccabees, by contrast, is explicitly structured around a theology of direct, visible divine intervention and retribution. The same chapter explicitly frames Antiochus's affliction as a specific instance of bodily, mirrored justice -- the king who tortured others' bodies now suffers torment in his own body (9:5-6), paralleling this book's similarly dramatic account of Heliodorus's earlier divine punishment in chapter 3 -- and its own distinctive emphasis on martyrdom and resurrection, addressed in entry 4 below. This project does not adjudicate which specific account is more historically precise regarding the exact physical cause of Antiochus's death. Both agree on the essential facts -- Antiochus died in the east following his military reversals and his sacrilegious actions against Jerusalem and other temples -- while differing substantially in theological framing, literary elaboration, and specific physical detail. This is a genuine divergence between two ancient historical accounts of the same broader event, addressed candidly rather than harmonized into false agreement.

## Theological Interpretation

### 4. 'God did not make them out of things that existed' -- the key prooftext for creation ex nihilo

**References:** 2 Maccabees 7:28; Wisdom of Solomon 11:17; Genesis 1:1-2

**The claim:** During the extended martyrdom account of a mother and her seven sons (2 Maccabees 7), the mother encourages her youngest son with the statement, 'I beg you, my child, to look at the heaven and the earth and see everything that is in them, and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed. And in the same way the human race came into being' (7:28) -- widely regarded as the clearest and most explicit statement of creation entirely 'out of nothing' (creatio ex nihilo) anywhere in the Old Testament or deuterocanonical corpus, addressed at greater length in the companion Wisdom of Solomon document regarding that book's own more ambiguous statement at 11:17 ('created the world out of formless matter'), which some scholars read as reflecting a different, more Platonic conception of creation from pre-existing material.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 2 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 2 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 2 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (7:28): 'I beseech you, my child, to look at the heaven and the earth and see everything that is in them, and recognise that God did not make them out of things that existed' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as this project's fuller treatment of the specific verse most consistently cited across the sources consulted for the companion Wisdom of Solomon document as the more decisive Old Testament-era witness to ex nihilo creation, in contrast to Wisdom 11:17's own more genuinely disputed wording. Multiple scholarly sources (including Jonathan Goldstein's specialized study of ancient Jewish creation theology, cited in the companion Wisdom of Solomon document) identify 2 Maccabees 7:28 as the clearer and earlier of the two texts on this specific point, generally reading its phrase 'not out of things that existed' (Greek, ouk ex onton) as expressing, more directly than Wisdom 11:17's Platonist-inflected 'formless matter' language, the idea that God's creative act required no pre-existing material substrate whatsoever. Some scholars (including a minority reading noted in the companion Wisdom of Solomon document) hold even this phrase should be read more cautiously as denying only that the specific, already-formed heaven and earth existed as such before creation, rather than making a fully developed metaphysical claim about the absolute absence of any prior substrate -- a nuance the Catechism of the Catholic Church itself addresses by treating this specific verse, rather than Wisdom 11:17, as its own preferred scriptural anchor for the doctrine of creation 'out of nothing.'

## Theological Tensions

### 5. Prayers and a sin offering for the dead -- one of the most doctrinally significant and contested passages in the deuterocanonical corpus

**References:** 2 Maccabees 12:38-46

**The claim:** 2 Maccabees 12:38-46 records that after a battle, Judas Maccabeus's soldiers discover that some of their own fallen comrades had secretly worn forbidden pagan amulets -- a violation of the Law -- and, recognizing this as the reason for their deaths in battle, Judas takes up a collection and sends money to Jerusalem 'to provide for a sin offering' on their behalf, with the text explicitly commending this action as 'holy and pious,' reasoning that it would be 'superfluous and foolish to pray for the dead' if there were no hope of resurrection, and concluding that it is 'a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead, that they may be loosed from their sins' (12:46, in some translations' numbering). This passage has been cited since antiquity, and explicitly at the Council of Trent, as a key scriptural basis for the Catholic doctrine of purgatory and the practice of praying for the dead -- a doctrine Protestant tradition rejects, a disagreement that was itself a significant factor in the Protestant exclusion of the deuterocanonical books from the canon during the Reformation.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 2 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 2 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 2 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders 12:46 as 'Therefore he made atonement for the dead, that they might be delivered from their sin,' the wording most directly cited in Catholic theological and catechetical discussion of this passage.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most doctrinally consequential passages addressed anywhere in this project, and this entry presents the genuine range of scholarly and theological positions rather than adjudicating the underlying doctrinal question, which remains a live and sincerely held disagreement between major Christian traditions. Catholic theologians and apologists (including sources explicitly cited in the Council of Trent's own decrees) read this passage as directly and clearly affirming both the practice of prayer and sacrifice for the dead and the theological presupposition behind it -- that the dead can still be aided by the living toward the full 'blotting out' of sin before their final, complete beatitude, a foundational scriptural building block for the later, more developed doctrine of purgatory. Protestant theologians and apologists generally respond in one or more of several ways: some argue the passage's own theology (offering a sin sacrifice to atone for the specific sin of idolatrous amulet-wearing) does not map precisely onto the later, more developed doctrine of purgatory as a place or process of purification from venial sin generally, representing a related but distinct ancient Jewish practice rather than a direct precedent; others argue that, whatever the passage's own content, its status as non-canonical Scripture (in Protestant reckoning) means it cannot establish binding doctrine regardless of what it states; and some, including a Catholic commentator cited among the sources for this entry, caution that the passage's meaning is itself 'much debated' and should not be treated as though it straightforwardly and precisely anticipates every detail of the later medieval doctrine. This project takes no position on the underlying doctrinal question, noting only that the passage's own plain content -- prayer, sacrifice, and hope of benefit for the already-dead -- is not seriously disputed as accurately described, whatever theological conclusions different traditions draw from it.

## Ethical Tensions

### 6. Razis's suicide -- narrated with apparent approval, in some tension with the sanctity of life

**References:** 2 Maccabees 14:37-46

**The claim:** 2 Maccabees 14:37-46 narrates the death of an elderly, respected Jewish elder named Razis, who, facing imminent capture and torture by hostile soldiers, chooses to take his own life rather than be captured -- described by the text as preferring 'to die nobly rather than to fall into the hands of sinners and suffer outrages unworthy of his noble birth' (14:42). When his initial attempt (falling on his own sword) fails to kill him quickly, he deliberately throws himself from a wall, and finally, while still alive and severely wounded, tears out his own entrails and hurls them at the crowd below, 'calling upon the Lord of life and spirit to give them back to him again' (14:46) -- a death narrated throughout in terms of dignity and noble courage rather than condemnation, standing in some contrast to the generally negative or neutral treatment of suicide elsewhere in Scripture (Saul, Ahithophel, and Judas Iscariot, none of whose deaths by their own hand receive comparable narrative approval).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of 2 Maccabees. It is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of 2 Maccabees in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. 2 Maccabees is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (14:42): Razis, preferring 'to die nobly rather than to fall into the hands of sinners and suffer outrages unworthy of his noble birth,' and (14:46) that he 'tore out his entrails, took them in both hands, and hurled them at the crowd, calling upon the Lord of life and spirit to give them back to him again' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this account within its specific literary and historical context rather than as a general endorsement of suicide as such: Razis's death is presented as an act of defiant resistance against religious persecution and the specific indignity of capture and torture by a hostile, idolatrous power, in the same broader thematic vein as this book's extensive martyrdom narratives (Eleazar in chapter 6, the mother and seven sons in chapter 7), which likewise celebrate steadfast resistance unto death rather than surrender of religious integrity -- Razis's specific manner of death (self-inflicted, rather than at the hands of the persecutors directly) is presented as the logical extension of the same principle these more clearly martyrological accounts establish, framed by the author as heroic resistance to religious persecution specifically, rather than as a general statement approving suicide in ordinary circumstances of despair or personal suffering unrelated to religious persecution. This project does not minimize that the passage's narrative tone (unreserved admiration, with no accompanying moral caveat) differs noticeably from how Scripture elsewhere treats self-inflicted death, and notes this as a genuine feature of the text reflecting the specific literary and theological purposes of its martyrological genre, which readers are not required to generalize into unqualified approval of suicide in other, unrelated circumstances.
