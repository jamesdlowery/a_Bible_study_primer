# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Judith

**A note on this document's place in the series:** As with Tobit, Judith is a deuterocanonical
book included in only one of the ten translations surveyed in this project: RSV2CE, a Catholic
edition. The other nine (AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT) follow the Protestant
and Jewish canon, which excludes Judith as non-canonical. This document notes, for each entry,
that nine of the ten translations do not contain the book at all.

This document catalogs 5 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical,
literary, and theological questions involving the book of Judith, either entirely internal to
the book or in comparison with Genesis, Deuteronomy, Joshua, Exodus, and Judges. For each item,
this document states the claim as it is typically raised and gives the standard scholarly and
Catholic-tradition reconciliation.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1 and 2 address the single most widely discussed historical
difficulty in the entire deuterocanonical corpus -- the book's opening verse identifies
Nebuchadnezzar, the historically well-attested king of Babylon, as "king of the Assyrians"
ruling from Nineveh, a city already destroyed years before his reign began. This document
presents the standard reading, including a direct citation from Pope John Paul II's own 1985
general audience, that the book is best understood as parable or edifying moral narrative
rather than strict historical chronicle -- a reading the book's own opening verse may be
deliberately signaling to its original audience, rather than a simple error requiring
apologetic defense.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by relevant scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary text directly.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 1. 'Nebuchadnezzar, who reigned over the Assyrians in the great city of Nineveh' -- the book's own opening, striking historical anomaly

**References:** Judith 1:1

**The claim:** Judith opens: 'It was the twelfth year of the reign of Nebuchadnezzar, who reigned over the Assyrians in the great city of Nineveh' -- yet Nebuchadnezzar was the historically well-attested king of Babylon (605-562 BC), not of Assyria, and Nineveh, Assyria's capital, had already been destroyed in 612 BC, before Nebuchadnezzar's own reign even began. This is widely regarded as the single most immediately striking historical difficulty in the entire deuterocanonical corpus -- considerably more pronounced than the chronological difficulties addressed in the companion Tobit document, since it involves confusing two entirely different empires and their most famous respective rulers.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Judith. Judith is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Judith in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Judith is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders Judith 1:1 as 'Nebuchadnezzar, who reigned over the Assyrians in the great city of Nineveh,' preserving the same striking combination found in the Greek text underlying all Catholic and Orthodox translations of the book.

**Reconciliation:** This specific detail has generated extensive discussion, including by Catholic scholars and theologians who affirm the book's canonical and inspired status, and this entry presents the leading explanations rather than dismissing the difficulty. Some scholars and apologists note that ancient historians, including Herodotus, sometimes used 'Assyria' loosely to describe the broader Mesopotamian region generally, including Babylon itself, even after the Assyrian Empire's own political end -- suggesting the term may not always have carried the precise, exclusive political meaning modern readers assume. Far more commonly cited, however, is the reading that this opening detail is not a simple error at all, but a deliberate literary signal to the book's original audience that they are reading a parable or symbolic moral narrative rather than a work of strict history. This reading has substantial support: Pope John Paul II, in a 1985 general audience, explicitly described Tobit, Judith, and Esther together as having 'the character of allegorical and moral narrative rather than history properly so called.' On this reading, the book's own first verse deliberately combines the two greatest and most famous enemies of Israel from two different historical periods -- Nebuchadnezzar personally, and the Assyrian empire generally -- into a single composite antagonist. One modern defender of the book compares this to a work of fiction pitting a heroine against 'Adolf Hitler, king of the Russians,' where the anachronism itself signals genre rather than indicating historical incompetence -- especially given that the heroine's own name, Judith (Hebrew Yehudit), means simply 'Jewess' or 'Lady Jew,' itself a strong signal of the story's symbolic, representative character. Separately, some scholars propose the narrative's 'Nebuchadnezzar' encodes a later historical figure the author's contemporary audience would have recognized more readily than a modern reader can, with the Persian king Artaxerxes III Ochus, who campaigned extensively in the region around 350 BC, the most commonly proposed candidate.

## Literary Composition

### 2. A book widely read, including by the Catholic magisterium itself, as parable rather than strict history

**References:** Judith 1:1; Judith 2:1-13; Judith 4:3

**The claim:** Beyond the opening verse's Nebuchadnezzar/Assyria confusion (addressed in entry 1), Judith's narrative contains further chronological and geographical difficulties -- the story is explicitly set shortly after the Jewish community's return from Babylonian exile (4:3, referencing the recent rebuilding of the Temple), a period Nebuchadnezzar himself predates by decades, and the town of 'Bethulia,' the story's central besieged location, cannot be identified with confidence with any known historical site -- raising the question of how a book containing this concentration of unresolved historical and geographical difficulty came to be regarded as canonical Scripture by two major Christian traditions.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Judith. Judith is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Judith in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Judith is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (4:3): 'they had only recently returned from exile,' with the town of 'Bethulia,' the story's central location, unidentifiable with any known historical site (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This question is directly addressed by the standard resolution offered in entry 1: rather than treating these details as errors requiring explanation away, mainstream Catholic and Orthodox scholarship, including at the highest levels of Catholic teaching authority, has long read Judith as belonging to a recognized ancient literary genre -- edifying, didactic historical fiction or parable, using a loosely historical setting to convey a specific moral and theological message (God's deliverance of the faithful, however powerless, from an overwhelming and blasphemous oppressor) -- rather than as a strict historical chronicle demanding precise chronological and geographical accuracy in every detail. This genre classification is treated as fully compatible with the book's canonical and inspired status by the traditions that recognize it, in the same way this project has addressed comparable literary and genre questions regarding other biblical narratives (including, at a more limited scale, the companion Tobit document's treatment of that book's own historical difficulties) -- inspiration and genre are treated as compatible with, rather than requiring the exclusion of, non-literal or symbolic historical narrative throughout the history of biblical interpretation.

## Ethical Tensions

### 3. Judith's premeditated deception and killing of Holofernes -- deception for a righteous cause

**References:** Judith 10:1-4; Judith 11:5-19; Judith 13:1-10; Joshua 2:1-7; Judges 4:17-21; Exodus 1:15-20

**The claim:** Judith's central narrative involves an extensive, deliberate campaign of deception: she adorns herself beautifully specifically to seduce and disarm the Assyrian general Holofernes (10:1-4), tells him a series of calculated falsehoods about her own people's supposed vulnerability and about revealing God's plans to him (11:5-19), and ultimately beheads him with his own sword while he lies in a drunken stupor in his private tent (13:1-10) -- an extended, premeditated pattern of deception in the service of achieving her people's deliverance, raising the same general question this project has addressed at several points regarding other biblical figures who employ deception for a purportedly righteous end (Rahab's deception of the spies in Joshua 2, the Hebrew midwives' deception of Pharaoh in Exodus 1, and Jael's deception and killing of Sisera in Judges 4).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Judith. Judith is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Judith in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Judith is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (13:15-16): 'The Lord has struck him down by the hand of a woman... it was my face that tricked him to his destruction,' following Judith's beheading of Holofernes as he lay 'stretched out on his bed... overcome with wine' (13:2) (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This is treated the same way as the comparable cases addressed at length in this project's companion Joshua, Exodus, and Judges documents, and Judith's own narrative is widely regarded as one of the most extended and deliberately developed examples of this recurring pattern in the entire biblical and deuterocanonical corpus. Most commentators read Judith's actions, like Rahab's, Jael's, and the midwives' before her, as narrated approvingly within a specific context of just, defensive warfare against an existential and explicitly blasphemous threat (Holofernes' own campaign is presented throughout the book as directly and deliberately impious, demanding worship of Nebuchadnezzar as a god, 3:8, 6:2), rather than as a general endorsement of deception in ordinary circumstances -- Judith's own extended prayer before undertaking her mission (9:1-14) explicitly frames her intended action as entrusted to God's own justice and asks for success specifically in defense of the besieged, defenseless community, a framing this project has addressed elsewhere as characteristic of how Scripture treats deception undertaken specifically within the extraordinary context of resisting a violent, existential national threat, rather than as a general ethical principle licensing deceit.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 4. Judith invokes Simeon's violence at Shechem as an approved precedent -- though Genesis itself has Jacob condemn that same violence

**References:** Judith 9:2-4; Genesis 34:25-31; Genesis 49:5-7

**The claim:** In her extended prayer before confronting Holofernes, Judith explicitly invokes her ancestor Simeon's violent revenge on the city of Shechem (following the rape of Dinah, Genesis 34:25-31) as a positive precedent and model for God's own righteous vindication of the wronged: 'you gave into the hands of Simeon, one of my ancestors, a sword to take revenge on those aliens who had loosed the girdle of a virgin to defile her... and you gave their wives for a prey, and their daughters to captivity' (9:2-4). Yet Genesis itself, in Jacob's own deathbed pronouncement over his sons, sharply condemns this very same act rather than approving it: 'Simeon and Levi are brothers; weapons of violence are their swords... Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce, and their wrath, for it is cruel' (Genesis 49:5-7).

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Judith. Judith is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Judith in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Judith is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (9:2-4): 'O Lord God of my father Simeon, to whom thou gavest a sword to take vengeance on the strangers who loosed the girdle of a virgin to defile her... thou gavest their wives for a prey and their daughters to captivity' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as a contradiction within a single coherent moral evaluation, but as Judith's own prayer selectively invoking one legitimate aspect of a complex, morally mixed episode -- the underlying justice of the grievance itself (the sexual violation of Dinah, and the deceitful manner in which Shechem's men were induced to accept circumcision before the attack, which Genesis 34 itself narrates without moral commentary on the initial wrong Simeon and Levi were responding to) -- without necessarily endorsing every specific detail Jacob's later curse specifically condemns (the sons' own use of deceit regarding the covenant sign of circumcision to accomplish the attack, and what Jacob evidently judged to be a disproportionate, indiscriminate massacre extending beyond the specific individual guilty party, Shechem himself, to the entire town). Judith's prayer functions as an appeal to God's own record of vindicating the wronged and defenseless (a theme, not a specific moral endorsement of every detail of how Simeon and Levi carried out their own act of vengeance), a rhetorical and devotional use of an ambiguous scriptural precedent that does not require resolving Genesis's own more complex, mixed evaluation of the episode Jacob himself condemns even while its underlying grievance (Dinah's violation) remained real and serious.

## Law and Practice

### 5. Achior the Ammonite's conversion -- an apparent resolution of Deuteronomy's exclusion of Ammonites, paralleling Ruth

**References:** Judith 14:5-10; Deuteronomy 23:3-6

**The claim:** Judith 5-6 introduces Achior, an Ammonite military commander serving under Holofernes, who accurately recounts Israel's history to his own commander and warns that Israel cannot be defeated while faithful to their God -- a warning Holofernes rejects with fury, nearly having Achior killed. After witnessing Judith's victory and Holofernes' death, Achior converts fully to the God of Israel and is circumcised, joining 'the house of Israel' permanently (14:5-10) -- raising the same question this project has addressed at length in the companion Ruth and Nehemiah documents regarding Deuteronomy 23:3-6's exclusion of Ammonites (along with Moabites) from full participation in Israel's covenant assembly.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, CSB, ESV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT:** This translation does not include the book of Judith. Judith is part of the deuterocanonical books accepted as Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but is excluded from the Protestant and Jewish canon this translation follows.
- **KJV:** The original 1611 printing of this translation included the book of Judith in a separate Apocrypha section placed between the Testaments -- read there for edification, but explicitly not treated as canonical Scripture on a par with the 66-book Protestant canon, consistent with the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Most KJV editions in common use today omit the Apocrypha section entirely. Judith is accepted as canonical Scripture by the Catholic Church and Eastern Orthodox tradition, but has not been treated as canonical by the Protestant or Jewish traditions this translation's 66-book canon reflects.
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE reads (14:10): 'when Achior saw all that the God of Israel had done, he believed firmly in God, and was circumcised, and joined the house of Israel, remaining so to this day' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in the companion Ruth document, which addresses Deuteronomy 23:3-6's scope as most likely concerning formal corporate covenant status for Ammonites and Moabites as nations, rather than barring any individual member of those nations who personally and fully renounced their own people's gods and allegiance to embrace Israel's God and covenant, exactly as Ruth the Moabite is shown doing in that book. Achior's account in Judith is widely read as a further, parallel illustration of this same principle -- an individual Ammonite, initially serving Israel's enemies, undergoes a genuine, complete conversion (marked by circumcision, the specific covenant sign) after directly witnessing God's power and Israel's vindication, and is fully and permanently incorporated into the covenant community as a result, reinforcing rather than contradicting the same reading of Deuteronomy's original exclusion already addressed regarding Ruth: it is a bar to persistent foreign national identity and allegiance to foreign gods, not to genuine individual conversion and covenant embrace, regardless of one's nation of origin.
