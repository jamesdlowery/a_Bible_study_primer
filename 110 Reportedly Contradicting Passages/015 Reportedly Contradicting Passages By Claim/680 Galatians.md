# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Galatians

This document catalogs 8 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Galatians,
either entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Acts, 1
Corinthians, Jeremiah, and Micah documents, and the underlying Genesis narrative. For each
item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV**
specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording
materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the
standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 3 addresses a genuinely technical,
actively debated question of Hebrew grammar underlying Paul's own argument in Galatians 3:16,
checked against C. John Collins's peer-reviewed article examining the question directly (Tyndale Bulletin 54, no. 1 (2003): 75-86).
Entry 5 addresses one of the most actively and sincerely debated questions in contemporary
Christian ethics -- the scope of Galatians 3:28's "neither male nor female" -- which this
project does not adjudicate.

**A note on candor:** Entries 3 and 5 are presented with genuine scholarly and theological
diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution. Entry 1 addresses a historically
significant episode of one apostle publicly correcting another, presented directly rather than
minimized.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly or
theological diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are
encouraged to consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Paul publicly opposes Peter 'to his face' at Antioch

**References:** Galatians 2:11-14

**The claim:** Galatians 2:11-14 records Paul directly and publicly confronting the apostle Peter (Cephas) at Antioch: 'when Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned. For before certain men came from James, he was eating with the Gentiles; but when they came he drew back and separated himself, fearing the circumcision party... I said to Cephas before them all, If you, though a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you force the Gentiles to live like Jews?' -- a striking account of one apostle publicly rebuking another for a specific moral and practical failure, with real significance for how apostolic authority and fallibility are understood across different Christian traditions.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Galatians 2:11-14 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a historically significant episode of apostolic correction, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a historically and theologically significant episode this project addresses directly rather than minimizing. Most commentators, across a wide range of traditions (including Catholic scholarship, which affirms Peter's own unique role while not treating him as personally infallible in matters of conduct), read this episode as Paul confronting a specific instance of Peter's own inconsistent, fear-driven behavior (withdrawing from table fellowship with Gentile believers under social pressure from a more conservative Jewish-Christian faction) rather than a challenge to Peter's own apostolic office, doctrinal authority, or standing as such -- the substance of Paul's objection concerns Peter's practical conduct failing to match his own already-established convictions (Peter himself had earlier eaten freely with Gentiles, and the broader Jerusalem Council, addressed in the companion Acts document, had already affirmed Gentile inclusion without requiring circumcision or full Jewish dietary observance), not a doctrinal correction of false teaching Peter had promoted. This episode is generally read as illustrating that even apostles remained personally fallible and subject to correction in specific matters of conduct, a distinction most traditions hold is compatible with recognizing Peter's own unique apostolic role and, in traditions that affirm it, later papal authority regarding doctrine specifically, since the Antioch incident concerns Peter's personal conduct under social pressure rather than a formal doctrinal pronouncement.

## Chronology

### 2. Galatians 2's Jerusalem visit and the Acts 15 Jerusalem Council -- addressed at length in the companion Acts document

**References:** Galatians 2:1-10; Acts 15:1-29; Acts 11:27-30

**The claim:** Galatians 2:1-10 describes Paul's own account of a Jerusalem visit addressing Gentile circumcision, whose relationship to Acts 15's Jerusalem Council (the same event, or an earlier visit) is addressed at length in the companion Acts document as one of the most substantively debated chronological questions in New Testament scholarship.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Galatians 2:1-10 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Acts document for the fuller discussion of this chronological question.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Acts document, which presents both major positions -- that Galatians 2 and Acts 15 describe the same event (a position held by Craig Keener and others), or that Galatians 2 corresponds instead to the earlier 'famine relief' visit of Acts 11:27-30, with Galatians written before the Acts 15 Council occurred (a minority but reportedly growing position) -- without adjudicating between them, since this remains a live and actively studied question among serious scholars with direct implications for the broader chronological reconstruction of Paul's ministry and letters.

## Theological Interpretation

### 3. The 'offspring... who is Christ' -- Paul's argument from the singular form of 'seed'

**References:** Galatians 3:16; Genesis 22:17-18

**The claim:** Galatians 3:16 builds an argument on the grammatical number of a specific Hebrew word: 'Now the promises were made to Abraham and to his offspring. It does not say, "And to offsprings," referring to many, but referring to one, "And to your offspring," who is Christ.' Critics have long objected that the underlying Hebrew word for 'seed' or 'offspring' (zera) is what grammarians call a collective singular noun -- grammatically singular in form while regularly referring to a plural, collective group of descendants (as the same word plainly does elsewhere in the same Abrahamic narrative, e.g., Genesis 13:16 and 15:5, comparing Abraham's 'seed' to the dust of the earth and the stars of the sky) -- raising a question of whether Paul's argument rests on a grammatical technicality that does not actually hold in the underlying Hebrew text he is interpreting.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Galatians 3:16 and the underlying Genesis passages plainly and consistently as given; this remains a genuinely and technically debated question of Hebrew grammar and ancient exegetical method, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and technically debated question among Hebrew grammarians and biblical scholars, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. A detailed grammatical study (C. John Collins, "Galatians 3:16: What Kind of Exegete Was Paul?" Tyndale Bulletin 54, no. 1 (2003): 75-86) examines this question directly, concluding that Paul was specifically drawing on Genesis 22:17-18 (rather than the earlier, more explicitly collective uses of 'seed' in Genesis 13 or 15), and that close grammatical analysis of the Hebrew text shows 'seed' functions differently across different passages within the same Abrahamic narrative -- in some contexts (Genesis 12, 15) accompanied by plural verbs and pronouns consistent with a genuinely collective sense, but in other contexts (including, on this reading, Genesis 22:17-18) accompanied by grammatically singular verbal and pronominal agreement suggesting a specific individual referent rather than a purely collective one, meaning Genesis's own text already contains the kind of singular/plural distinction Paul's argument draws on, rather than Paul inventing a distinction absent from the original. Other scholars and critics maintain that this defense is unconvincing, noting the overwhelming, well-attested pattern of 'seed' functioning as a collective term throughout the Abrahamic narrative as a whole, and that Paul's specific argumentative move (treating the singular form as excluding a plural referent) does not reflect how the term was understood in ancient Hebrew usage or in early Jewish interpretation more broadly, representing instead a homiletical or rhetorical argument characteristic of Second Temple Jewish exegetical method (which regularly drew theological conclusions from specific grammatical or textual features in ways modern historical-grammatical exegesis would not endorse as strict argumentation) rather than a claim resting on straightforward linguistic analysis. This project does not adjudicate between these positions, noting that serious, technically competent scholars occupy both sides of this specific grammatical question.

### 4. Hagar and Sarah as 'allegory' -- Paul's own explicit, non-literal reading of the Genesis narrative

**References:** Galatians 4:21-31; Genesis 16:1-16; Genesis 21:1-21

**The claim:** Galatians 4:21-31 reinterprets the historical narrative of Abraham's two sons -- Ishmael, born to the slave woman Hagar, and Isaac, born to the free woman Sarah -- as representing two covenants: Hagar representing the Sinai covenant and present, earthly Jerusalem 'in slavery with her children,' and Sarah representing the 'Jerusalem above,' the covenant of promise and freedom. Paul explicitly names his own interpretive method: 'Now this may be interpreted allegorically' (4:24) -- raising a question about whether this represents a legitimate reading of the Genesis narrative's own historical content, or a reinterpretation that goes beyond what the original text itself intends.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Galatians 4:21-31 plainly and consistently as given, including Paul's own use of the term 'allegory' (or, in some translations, 'symbolically' or 'figuratively'); this entry addresses a matter of interpretive method, not a contradiction, and is not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as a documented feature of how New Testament authors, and Paul specifically, drew theological meaning from Old Testament historical narratives using interpretive methods common within Second Temple Jewish exegesis. This is a pattern addressed at several other points throughout this project regarding the New Testament's use of Old Testament texts, including the typological readings of Hosea 11:1 in Matthew 2:15 and Joel 2:28-32 in Acts 2, addressed in their own companion documents. Most commentators do not read Paul's use of the term 'allegory' here as a claim that the underlying Genesis narrative did not actually occur historically; Paul's own argument depends on the historical reality of Hagar's status as a slave and Sarah's as free, and of Ishmael's and Isaac's respective births, as the basis for the further, additional theological meaning he draws from it. Instead, it identifies his own theological, typological application of that genuinely historical narrative to illuminate the present situation facing the Galatian churches -- the conflict between those insisting on law-based, Sinai-covenant religious observance and those embracing the freedom of the new covenant. This is a use of 'allegory' closer to what later interpreters would more precisely distinguish as typology, drawing a theologically significant historical pattern or correspondence from a real, historical event, than to allegory in the stricter, more common ancient philosophical sense of treating a narrative's historical claims as unimportant or fictional, with only its symbolic meaning mattering. This project does not treat this as requiring readers to conclude Paul denied the historicity of the Genesis account he is interpreting.

## Theological Tensions

### 5. 'Neither male nor female... all one in Christ Jesus' -- spiritual equality, or a claim about social roles?

**References:** Galatians 3:26-29; 1 Corinthians 11:2-16; 1 Corinthians 14:34-35

**The claim:** Galatians 3:28 states, 'There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus' -- a striking, egalitarian-sounding statement that some read as teaching the dissolution of all social and role-based distinctions, including gender roles, among believers. This raises a question about how this statement relates to Paul's own apparently more differentiated teaching regarding gender roles and conduct elsewhere in his letters (addressed at length in the companion 1 Corinthians document regarding 11:2-16 and 14:34-35), and to his own acceptance, without direct condemnation, of the social institution of slavery as it existed in his own context (addressed pastorally, rather than abolished outright, in his letter to Philemon).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Galatians 3:26-29 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most actively and sincerely debated questions in contemporary Christian ethics and theology, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most actively and sincerely debated questions in contemporary Christian theology and ethics, directly bound up with ongoing disagreements between 'egalitarian' and 'complementarian' theological traditions regarding gender roles specifically, and this project does not adjudicate the underlying question, consistent with its treatment of comparably contested theological and ethical questions elsewhere in this series. Most commentators agree Galatians 3:28's immediate, explicit context concerns equal standing and full inclusion in the specific privileges of covenant membership and salvation ('for you are all one in Christ Jesus,' with the preceding verses addressing baptism and being 'clothed with Christ,' 3:27) -- the passage's own primary point is that Jew and Gentile, slave and free, male and female all have equal access to salvation and full covenant membership through faith in Christ, without ethnic, social, or gender status determining one's standing before God, a point of substantial and largely uncontested significance regardless of one's position on the further question. The genuinely disputed question concerns how far this principle of spiritual equality in Christ extends into the separate question of social roles, church leadership structure, and household relationships -- egalitarian interpreters read Galatians 3:28 as establishing a broad principle that should, when consistently applied, dissolve distinctions in role and authority as well as standing before God, viewing Paul's own more differentiated instructions elsewhere as reflecting first-century cultural accommodation rather than timeless prescription; complementarian interpreters read Galatians 3:28 as addressing salvation and covenant standing specifically, holding that Paul's own more detailed instructions on gender roles in worship and household relationships elsewhere represent his own consistent, non-contradictory application of created gender distinctions that Galatians 3:28 was never intended to dissolve. This project takes no position on which reading is correct.

## Ethical Tensions

### 6. 'Let him be accursed' -- strong language toward those preaching a different gospel

**References:** Galatians 1:6-9

**The claim:** Galatians 1:8-9 pronounces a severe curse on anyone, even an angel, who preaches a gospel contrary to the one Paul had originally delivered to the Galatians: 'even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed... let him be accursed' -- repeated twice for emphasis -- language of unusual severity that some read as in tension with the broader New Testament emphasis on patience, gentleness, and love, even toward those in doctrinal error.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Galatians 1:8-9 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing a foundational threat to the gospel's own core content from ordinary matters of pastoral patience and correction, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this severe language as reflecting the specific, serious nature of the situation Paul is addressing -- not an ordinary doctrinal disagreement or a matter of Christian charity toward differing, secondary theological opinions, but a direct assault on the very foundation of the gospel message itself (the specific error Paul addresses throughout this letter, requiring circumcision and law-observance as necessary additions to faith in Christ for salvation, which Paul treats as a fundamental corruption striking at the heart of grace and the sufficiency of Christ's own work, rather than a minor variation in emphasis or practice). This is generally read as consistent with, rather than contradicting, the New Testament's broader pattern of reserving its strongest, most severe language specifically for threats to the gospel's own core content and the salvation of those who might be misled by it (a documented pattern also addressed elsewhere in this project regarding false prophets and teachers, e.g., in the companion Jeremiah and Micah documents), while the same New Testament, including this same letter (Galatians 6:1-2, addressing how to gently restore someone caught in sin), consistently calls for patience and gentleness in matters of ordinary pastoral care and correction that do not involve this kind of foundational doctrinal threat.

### 7. Slavery regulated by the Law vs. 'neither slave nor free' in Christ

**References:** Exodus 21:2-11; Leviticus 25:44-46; Galatians 3:28; Philemon 15-16

**The claim:** The Mosaic Law regulates the institution of slavery rather than prohibiting it -- specifying terms for Hebrew indentured servants (Exodus 21:2-11) and permitting the acquisition of foreign slaves as property from surrounding nations (Leviticus 25:44-46) -- and the New Testament likewise instructs slaves to obey their masters (Ephesians 6:5-8; Colossians 3:22-24) without commanding masters to free them outright. Yet Paul writes, 'There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus' (Galatians 3:28), and in his letter to Philemon appeals for the runaway slave Onesimus to be received back 'no longer as a slave but... as a beloved brother' (Philemon 15-16). This raises the question of how the New Testament's language of radical spiritual equality relates to its own apparent acceptance of slavery as a continuing social institution.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Exodus 21:2-11, Leviticus 25:44-46, Galatians 3:28, and Philemon 15-16 plainly and consistently as given; this is a matter of interpreting the relationship between spiritual equality and social institutions across the biblical canon, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Scholars and theologians addressing this question generally do not treat Galatians 3:28 and the New Testament's household codes as flatly contradictory, but as operating on different, if related, levels. Galatians 3:28 is widely read as a statement about standing before God and status within the believing community -- declaring that distinctions of ethnicity, social class, and gender carry no bearing on one's acceptance in Christ or inheritance of the promise -- rather than a direct decree abolishing slavery as a civil institution throughout the Roman Empire, which neither Paul nor any other New Testament writer explicitly calls for. At the same time, most interpreters recognize the letter to Philemon as putting exactly this theological principle into concrete practice: Paul does not simply return Onesimus without comment but explicitly grounds his appeal in the master and slave's new, shared identity as brothers in Christ, a status Paul insists is incompatible with the older hierarchy, even while stopping short of directly commanding Philemon to free Onesimus outright. Historians of the early church generally view passages like Galatians 3:28 and the transformed master-slave relationship modeled in Philemon as having planted principles that early Christian communities began working out in practice, and which later Christian abolitionist movements (both ancient, as some patristic voices condemned slavery on explicitly Christian grounds, and modern, particularly in the 18th and 19th centuries) would draw on directly -- Galatians 3:28 in particular has been called, by more than one modern scholar, the 'Magna Carta' of the Christian abolitionist movement -- even though the New Testament writers themselves did not call for the institution's immediate, wholesale legal abolition in their own historical context.

### 8. Should we bear each other's burdens, or does each person bear their own?

**References:** Galatians 6:2, 5

**The claim:** Galatians 6:2 instructs, 'Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ.' Only three verses later, 6:5 states, 'For every man shall bear his own burden' -- an apparently direct, close-proximity contradiction within the same short passage.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard resolution notes that Paul uses two different Greek words for 'burden' in these two verses, each carrying a distinct sense: 6:2's baros describes a heavy, oppressive weight -- consistent with the immediate preceding context (6:1) of restoring someone overtaken in a fault or struggling under a serious spiritual burden they cannot carry alone, calling for mutual support and gentle restoration within the community. 6:5's phortion instead describes an ordinary load or pack, of the kind each person is expected to carry as their own basic, personal responsibility -- consistent with its own immediate context (6:3-4's warning against comparing oneself favorably to others and instead testing one's own work), addressing each person's non-transferable accountability for examining and answering for their own conduct, rather than a heavy crisis requiring communal help. Paul uses two different words for two different kinds of load: overwhelming burdens the community should help carry together, and ordinary personal responsibility each person must own for themselves -- not a contradiction about a single kind of burden.
