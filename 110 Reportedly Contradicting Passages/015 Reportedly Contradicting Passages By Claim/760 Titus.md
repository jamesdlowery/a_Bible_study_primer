# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Titus

This document catalogs 4 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving Titus, the
third and final Pastoral Epistle addressed in this series, either entirely internal to the
letter or in comparison with the companion 1 Timothy and Romans documents, Acts, 1
Corinthians, John, and Ephesians. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most notable entries:** Entry 1 addresses Paul's citation and
endorsement of a sweeping ethnic generalization from a pagan poet, treated with direct,
candid attention to both its logical dimension (the classical "liar's paradox") and its
ethical dimension, rather than minimized. Entry 3 addresses "the washing of regeneration," a
genuinely significant and actively contested basis for the ongoing baptismal regeneration
debate between major Christian traditions.

**A note on candor:** Entry 3 is presented with genuine theological diversity of opinion
rather than a single forced resolution, consistent with this project's treatment of
comparably contested sacramental questions.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 3 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing the genuine range of positions on each question, including the historical
attribution of the Epimenides quotation and specific denominational arguments regarding
baptismal regeneration. The remaining entries are addressed from standard harmonization
approaches, several of which build on treatments already established in the companion 1
Timothy and Romans documents.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine theological diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Ethical Tensions

### 1. 'Cretans are always liars' -- Paul quotes a sweeping ethnic generalization from a pagan poet and calls it 'true'

**References:** Titus 1:12-13

**The claim:** Titus 1:12-13 records Paul quoting, and directly endorsing, a sweeping negative characterization of an entire people: 'One of the Cretans, a prophet of their own, said, "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons." This testimony is true.' The quotation is traditionally attributed to the sixth-century-BC Cretan poet-philosopher Epimenides, and Paul's own explicit endorsement ('this testimony is true') raises both an ethical question (is Paul, and by extension Scripture, endorsing a blanket negative ethnic or national stereotype?) and a well-known logical puzzle (since the statement 'Cretans are always liars' was itself made by a Cretan, generating the classical 'liar's paradox' associated with Epimenides' name in the history of philosophy and logic).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Titus 1:12-13 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a matter of ancient rhetorical convention and ethical interpretation, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** On the logical question: most commentators note Paul was almost certainly not invoking, or even aware of, the strict philosophical liar's paradox later associated with this saying (a technical problem in formal logic developed by later Greek philosophers using this same line as an example) -- in its original and Pauline context, the statement functions as proverbial hyperbole rather than a strict, universal logical claim, an established ancient literary convention (this specific line, or close variants of it, is independently attested as an already well-known proverbial saying about Cretans among other ancient Greek writers, including the poet Callimachus, well before and independent of any connection to Paul), comparable to a modern speaker using a familiar, exaggerated cultural stereotype rhetorically without intending each word to be parsed as a rigorous, universally quantified logical proposition. On the ethical question: this project addresses the passage candidly rather than minimizing its sweeping character. Paul's own immediate point in context is not a claim about the fixed, essential moral nature of every individual Cretan without exception, but a rhetorical device supporting his specific instruction to Titus regarding certain false teachers active in the Cretan churches specifically (1:10-11, 'there are many who are insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers... who must be silenced'), using an already well-known, self-critical Cretan proverb about the surrounding culture's own general reputation to reinforce the specific, urgent need for correction Titus faced in that setting -- a rhetorical strategy of citing a group's own widely circulated self-description, similar to how a contemporary speaker might cite a culture's own proverbs about itself, rather than an anthropological or theological claim about the fixed moral character of every individual member of an entire people for all time. This project notes, without endorsing the practice of extending ethnic generalizations more broadly, that Paul elsewhere approvingly cites non-biblical Greek literature to make specific points (Acts 17:28's citation of Epimenides and Aratus; 1 Corinthians 15:33's citation of the playwright Menander), reflecting an established practice of engaging with, rather than wholesale endorsing every aspect of, the broader literary and cultural context of his audience.

## Theological Interpretation

### 3. 'The washing of regeneration' -- a genuine, actively contested basis for the baptismal regeneration debate

**References:** Titus 3:5; John 3:5; Ephesians 5:26

**The claim:** Titus 3:5 states, 'he saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit' -- language directly connected to a genuine, actively contested denominational debate regarding baptismal regeneration: whether water baptism itself is the specific means or instrument through which God regenerates a believer (a position historically associated with Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, and some other traditions), or whether the 'washing' language here is instead a symbolic or metaphorical description of the inward, spiritual regeneration accomplished by the Holy Spirit through faith, of which physical baptism serves only as an outward sign or testimony (a position historically associated with Baptist and many broader evangelical traditions).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Titus 3:5 plainly and consistently as given; this remains one of the most actively and sincerely debated sacramental questions in contemporary Christian theology, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This project does not adjudicate this actively and sincerely debated sacramental question, which remains a genuine, significant point of difference between major Christian traditions. Those favoring a baptismal-regeneration reading note that the Greek term for 'washing' (loutron, a term for a literal bath or washing, used in the New Testament only here and in the closely related Ephesians 5:26) most naturally suggests an actual washing rather than a purely metaphorical one, that numerous prominent historic commentators and even some Baptist grammarians (including A.T. Robertson) have conceded this verse most naturally refers to water baptism, and that other New Testament passages using similar language (John 3:5's 'born of water and the Spirit'; Acts 22:16's 'be baptized and wash away your sins'; 1 Peter 3:21's direct statement that 'baptism... now saves you') are read as mutually reinforcing this understanding. Those favoring a purely metaphorical or symbolic reading note that Titus 3:5 itself never explicitly mentions 'baptism' by name (using only the more general term 'washing'), that the passage's own immediate emphasis falls on God's mercy and the Holy Spirit's regenerating work rather than on any human act or ritual as such (with the passage's opening clause explicitly excluding 'works done by us' from any role in salvation), and that the term 'washing' can be, and elsewhere in Scripture sometimes is, used in a purely metaphorical sense for spiritual cleansing without necessarily denoting a literal physical washing. This project takes no position on which reading is correct.

### 4. 'Grace... bringing salvation for all people' -- addressed at length in the companion Romans document

**References:** Titus 2:11; Romans 5:12-19

**The claim:** Titus 2:11 states, 'For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people' -- language connecting to the same question addressed at length in the companion Romans document regarding Romans 5:18's similarly universal-sounding 'all men' language and its relationship to salvation's scope.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Titus 2:11 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Romans document for the fuller discussion of this question regarding the scope of salvation language.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Romans document, which addresses the standard resolution: 'all people' in passages of this kind is generally read as referring to the removal of any ethnic, social, or national restriction on who may receive God's grace (all kinds and categories of people, without distinction, rather than a claim of universal salvation for every individual without exception regardless of personal faith), consistent with Paul's own repeated emphasis elsewhere that salvation is received specifically through faith. This same chapter's own immediate continuation (2:12, describing grace as 'training us to renounce ungodliness... and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives') is generally read as addressing those who have actually received this grace through faith, not asserting that literally every individual, regardless of response, is automatically saved.
