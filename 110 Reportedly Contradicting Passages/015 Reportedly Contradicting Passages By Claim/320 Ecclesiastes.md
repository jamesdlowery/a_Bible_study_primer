# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Ecclesiastes

This document catalogs 11 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable
compositional questions involving the book of Ecclesiastes, either entirely internal to the
book or in comparison with Genesis, Isaiah, Revelation, 1 Corinthians, Luke, 2 Corinthians,
Matthew, and Proverbs. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically
raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the
translation's own wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in
English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Ecclesiastes is compact but unusually dense with contested material. This document has 11 entries rather than the full 12 used for the longest books in this series,
since several additional candidate questions overlap substantially with material already
covered at length in the companion Job and Proverbs documents (particularly the
retribution-theology question) -- repeating that ground here would add length without adding
substance.

**A note on candor:** Entry 8 (the epilogue's confident conclusion against the book's
skeptical body) and entry 9 (Solomonic authorship) are both presented with genuine scholarly
uncertainty rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine uncertainty remains
among scholars, an honest account of what is and is not established. Readers are encouraged
to consult the primary texts directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'There is nothing new under the sun' -- vs. God doing 'a new thing'

**References:** Ecclesiastes 1:9-10; Isaiah 43:19; Revelation 21:5

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 1:9-10 states flatly, 'What has been is what will be, and what has been done is what will be done, and there is nothing new under the sun... there is no remembrance of former things.' This appears to be in direct tension with later scriptural affirmations that God does bring about genuinely new things: 'Behold, I am doing a new thing' (Isaiah 43:19), and 'Behold, I am making all things new' (Revelation 21:5).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ecclesiastes 1:9-10, Isaiah 43:19, and Revelation 21:5 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading Ecclesiastes' claim within its specific 'under the sun' scope, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Ecclesiastes 1:9-10 in its specific context -- an extended reflection on the endless, wearying cyclical patterns of purely natural and human activity 'under the sun' (the sun rising and setting, rivers flowing to the sea, generations coming and going, 1:4-7), and specifically on the futility of human striving and achievement viewed from a strictly this-worldly, repetitive vantage point -- rather than as a categorical metaphysical claim ruling out any possibility of genuine novelty in the whole of reality, including God's own future redemptive action. Isaiah 43:19 and Revelation 21:5 both explicitly describe God's own decisive, unprecedented redemptive intervention breaking into and transforming the natural order Ecclesiastes describes as endlessly repeating on its own -- precisely the kind of qualitatively different divine action that a purely 'under the sun,' humanly-observed perspective would have no basis to anticipate or claim, which is consistent with, rather than contradicted by, Qoheleth's specific complaint about the wearying sameness of ordinary, unaided human experience and toil.

### 2. 'Man has no advantage over the beasts' -- vs. human distinctiveness and resurrection hope elsewhere

**References:** Ecclesiastes 3:19-21; Genesis 1:26-27; 1 Corinthians 15:12-22

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 3:19-21 observes that both humans and animals share the same fate of death and return to dust, and asks, 'who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward and the spirit of the beast goes down into the earth?' -- language that appears to deny or at least radically question human beings' unique spiritual destiny and standing above the animal creation, in apparent tension with Genesis 1:26-27's account of humanity uniquely made in God's image, and with the New Testament's confident teaching on human bodily resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:12-22).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ecclesiastes 3:19-21 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the passage's specific epistemological scope (what can be known through observation alone) rather than a settled denial, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this passage as describing the shared physical fate of mortality that both humans and animals experience 'under the sun' -- both die, both return to dust, and this specific, observable physical process makes no visible distinction between the two from the vantage point of ordinary human observation, which is Qoheleth's consistent and repeatedly stated frame of reference throughout the book. The rhetorical question 'who knows' is generally read not as a confident denial of any distinct human spiritual destiny, but as an honest acknowledgment of the limits of what can be established through unaided human observation and reason alone (again, 'under the sun') -- a limitation the fuller scope of biblical revelation, given through God's own further disclosure rather than through human observation, directly addresses and answers in texts like Genesis 1 and 1 Corinthians 15. Ecclesiastes' honest agnosticism about what cannot be settled by observation is compatible with, rather than contradicting, later revelation settling the question through a different means of knowledge.

### 3. 'The dead know nothing' -- vs. conscious existence after death elsewhere in Scripture

**References:** Ecclesiastes 9:5, 10; Luke 16:19-31; Revelation 6:9-10; 2 Corinthians 5:8

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 9:5 states, 'the dead know nothing, and they have no more reward, for the memory of them is forgotten,' and 9:10 adds, 'there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol, to which you are going' -- language read by some as denying any conscious existence after death. This stands in apparent tension with Jesus' account of the rich man and Lazarus, both fully conscious and aware after death (Luke 16:19-31), the martyrs depicted as consciously crying out from under the heavenly altar (Revelation 6:9-10), and Paul's own stated preference to be 'away from the body and at home with the Lord' (2 Corinthians 5:8), implying continued conscious existence.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ecclesiastes 9:5, 10 and the later New Testament passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing Ecclesiastes' specific scope (disconnection from earthly affairs) as distinct from a comprehensive doctrinal statement on the nature of the intermediate state, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length regarding a closely parallel tension in the book of Job (addressed in the companion Job document regarding Job 7:9-10, 14:7-12, and 19:25-27). As with that case, most commentators read Ecclesiastes 9:5, 10 as describing death's effect specifically on a person's participation in, awareness of, and ability to act within the ongoing affairs of the living world 'under the sun' -- the dead no longer have any 'reward' or share in earthly projects, relationships, or knowledge of what continues happening among the living, and Sheol (the general Old Testament term for the realm of the dead, itself an ambiguous and not fully theologically developed concept at the time Ecclesiastes was written) is here described as a place without ongoing worldly activity or work of the kind Qoheleth has been discussing throughout the book, rather than as a settled doctrinal statement excluding any form of conscious existence whatsoever. The fuller, later biblical revelation on the intermediate state and resurrection (developed considerably further in the New Testament than in the wisdom literature's earlier, more limited statements) is generally read as clarifying and completing rather than contradicting Ecclesiastes' narrower, 'under the sun' observation about the dead's disconnection from earthly affairs.

### 4. 'Be not overly righteous... do not make yourself too wise' -- vs. the call to complete devotion

**References:** Ecclesiastes 7:16-18; Matthew 5:48

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 7:16 instructs, 'Be not overly righteous, and do not make yourself too wise. Why should you destroy yourself?' -- language that, read at face value, appears to counsel moderation in the very pursuit of righteousness and wisdom the rest of Scripture consistently and unreservedly commends, including Jesus' own command, 'You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect' (Matthew 5:48).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ecclesiastes 7:16-18 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading the warning as directed against self-righteous legalism specifically, not genuine righteousness or wisdom as such, and on reading the passage's own immediate qualifying context, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Ecclesiastes 7:16 not as counseling against genuine righteousness or wisdom as such (which the book elsewhere consistently commends, e.g., 7:19, 'wisdom gives strength to the wise man'), but as warning specifically against a self-righteous, rigid, and ultimately self-destructive perfectionism -- an over-scrupulous, judgmental legalism that exhausts and 'destroys' a person through impossible self-imposed standards, or a related over-confidence in one's own wisdom and moral judgment that leads to arrogant, harsh treatment of others (a concern the surrounding verses, 7:15-22, explicitly develop, including the immediately following warning in 7:20 that 'there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and never sins,' which qualifies the whole passage by grounding it in universal human sinfulness and limitation). This is generally read as compatible with, rather than opposed to, Jesus' call to genuine, whole-hearted devotion in Matthew 5:48 (itself given in a context, the Sermon on the Mount, that consistently redirects righteousness away from external, self-satisfied legalism and toward genuine heart-transformation) -- both texts, on this reading, oppose the same underlying error of self-righteous, joyless legalism from different angles.

### 5. 'Time and chance happen to them all' -- vs. Proverbs' confident providential ordering

**References:** Ecclesiastes 9:11; Proverbs 16:33; Proverbs 10:27

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 9:11 observes, 'the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, nor bread to the wise, nor riches to the intelligent, nor favor to the men of skill; but time and chance happen to them all' -- an observation emphasizing the apparent randomness and unpredictability of outcomes in life, standing in some tension with Proverbs' confident, providence-affirming claims that even seemingly random events (such as the casting of lots) are entirely governed by God ('the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD,' Proverbs 16:33), and that wisdom and righteousness reliably produce favorable outcomes (Proverbs 10:27, addressed at greater length in the companion Proverbs document).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ecclesiastes 9:11 and Proverbs 16:33, 10:27 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the human experience of unpredictability from the theological claim of underlying divine governance, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Ecclesiastes 9:11 as describing the same reality Proverbs 16:33 describes, from a different and complementary vantage point: 'time and chance' (Hebrew eth and pega, better rendered 'an unforeseen occurrence' rather than a claim of true metaphysical randomness outside any governing order) describes how outcomes appear and are experienced from the limited standpoint of human observation and prediction 'under the sun' -- skill, strength, and wisdom do not mechanically guarantee success in every individual instance, and unforeseen, uncontrollable circumstances genuinely do intervene and overturn expected outcomes, a real and honestly acknowledged feature of ordinary experience. This is fully compatible with Proverbs 16:33's theological claim that God sovereignly governs even events that appear random or uncontrolled from a human vantage point -- the two texts describe the same reality (apparently unpredictable outcomes) from two different, complementary perspectives: Ecclesiastes describing how such outcomes are experienced and observed by humans lacking full knowledge or control, and Proverbs affirming the ultimate divine governance operating behind and within that same apparent unpredictability, not two competing claims about whether outcomes are actually random or actually governed.

### 6. Is money good or bad, given that this book calls it the 'answer' to everything?

**References:** Ecclesiastes 10:19; Ecclesiastes 5:10; 1 Timothy 6:10

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 10:19 states, 'A feast is made for laughter, and wine maketh merry: but money answereth all things' -- language read by some as a strong, positive endorsement of money's power and value. Elsewhere, this same book states, 'he that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver' (5:10), and Paul writes that 'the love of money is the root of all evil' (1 Timothy 6:10).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 10:19 is best read as a dry, observational statement about money's practical, functional usefulness in worldly affairs -- money genuinely does provide the practical means to secure feasts, comfort, and many of life's ordinary necessities and pleasures, an unremarkable, descriptive point about how the world works 'under the sun,' consistent with the verse's own surrounding context of proverbial observations about ordinary life rather than a sustained theological argument. This is not a moral endorsement of money as an object of love, devotion, or ultimate security, a pursuit this very book explicitly warns produces only endless, unsatisfied craving (5:10) rather than genuine contentment. Money's real, practical usefulness (10:19) and the folly of loving or trusting in money as a source of ultimate satisfaction or security (5:10; 1 Timothy 6:10) are not competing claims -- Scripture consistently distinguishes money's legitimate, functional use from an inordinate love of or reliance on it, the same distinction this document applies elsewhere regarding wine's legitimate use versus its abuse (see the companion Proverbs document's entry on wine).

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 7. Retribution affirmed -- then immediately observed to fail, within the same passage

**References:** Ecclesiastes 8:12-13; Ecclesiastes 8:14

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 8:12-13 states a confident, traditional retribution principle: 'though a sinner does evil a hundred times and prolongs his life, yet I know that it will be well with those who fear God... but it will not be well with the wicked.' The very next verse, without any transition or qualification, states the opposite as an observed reality: 'there is a vanity that takes place on earth, that there are righteous people to whom it happens according to the deeds of the wicked, and there are wicked people to whom it happens according to the deeds of the righteous' (8:14).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses plainly and consistently as directly following each other without transition or resolution; the book's own juxtaposition of the two claims is the point, not an error requiring correction, and this is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This entry parallels the deliberate back-to-back tension in Proverbs 26:4-5 (addressed at greater length in the companion Proverbs document), and is best read the same way: rather than an accidental contradiction, this is Qoheleth's own characteristic method throughout the book -- stating a traditional wisdom principle honestly and without qualification (8:12-13, echoing confident retribution language found throughout Proverbs and the Psalms), then immediately and equally honestly acknowledging, from his own observed experience, that this principle does not hold without exception in every individual case (8:14). Ecclesiastes as a whole is widely understood as a sustained meditation holding together both halves of this tension without fully resolving it into a single tidy formula -- affirming that ultimate justice belongs to God and that fearing God remains right and good (the book's own final word, addressed in entry 8 below), while refusing to pretend that this principle straightforwardly explains every individual's actual experience within a single human lifetime 'under the sun.'

### 8. 'Better than both is he who has not yet been' -- documented lament, or the author's settled theology?

**References:** Ecclesiastes 4:2-3; Ecclesiastes 6:3-5; Genesis 1:28

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 4:2-3 states, in reflecting on the oppression observed in the world, 'I thought the dead who are already dead more fortunate than the living who are still alive; but better than both is he who has not yet been, who has not seen the evil deeds done under the sun.' A similarly stark statement recurs at 6:3-5 regarding a person who lives long but finds no satisfaction. This starkly negative assessment of life itself and even of being born stands in apparent tension with Genesis's own foundational, positive affirmation that human life and procreation are part of God's good creation design ('be fruitful and multiply,' Genesis 1:28, spoken immediately after God's own declared verdict that his completed creation, including humanity, was 'very good,' Genesis 1:31).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ecclesiastes 4:2-3 and 6:3-5 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing an honest record of situational despair from the book's own broader, more positive final theology of life, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this passage, along the same lines addressed for Job's own dark statements about his birth in the companion Job document (regarding Job 3), as an honest, unflinching record of despair experienced and expressed at a specific, difficult point within a longer wisdom reflection wrestling with observed injustice and oppression (4:1, explicitly the trigger for this specific statement), not as the book's own final, settled theological verdict on the value of human life or procreation as such -- a verdict the book itself elsewhere directly qualifies and moves beyond, both through its own repeated 'eat, drink, and enjoy' counter-refrain (addressed in entry 10 above, affirming the genuine goodness of ordinary life) and its concluding call to remember one's Creator specifically during the days of one's youth and life (12:1), which presupposes life's fundamental value and the appropriateness of having lived it, rather than regretting one's existence. Scripture elsewhere models exactly this same pattern of recording, without either censoring or theologically endorsing as final doctrine, a genuine sufferer's most extreme statements of anguish (see also this project's treatment of several psalms of lament).

## Literary Composition

### 9. The epilogue's confident 'fear God and keep his commandments' -- vs. the skeptical tone of the book's body

**References:** Ecclesiastes 12:9-14; Ecclesiastes 1:2; Ecclesiastes 2:17

**The claim:** The great majority of Ecclesiastes maintains a skeptical, world-weary, and at points seemingly despairing tone -- 'vanity of vanities, all is vanity' (1:2), 'I hated life' (2:17), and repeated observations of life's apparent meaninglessness and injustice. Yet the book's closing verses shift decisively to a confident, traditional, and theologically resolved conclusion: 'Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man. For God will bring every deed into judgment' (12:13-14) -- a tone and content some readers find difficult to reconcile smoothly with the preceding eleven-plus chapters.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both the skeptical body and the epilogue's confident conclusion plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here concerns composition history and literary structure, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, including many with a high view of the book's unity and inspiration, are divided on how best to understand this shift, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Some read the epilogue (12:9-14, itself written partly in the third person about 'the Preacher,' unlike the book's dominant first-person voice) as the original author's own deliberate, carefully placed conclusion -- intentionally allowing the skeptical, searching body of the book to run its full course honestly, without premature theological resolution, before arriving at the settled, traditional answer only at the very end, a recognized rhetorical strategy for leading a reader through genuine doubt toward a hard-won, rather than merely assumed, conclusion. Others read the epilogue as a later editorial frame, added by a different hand (the 'frame-narrator' some scholars identify based on the third-person references to 'the Preacher' both here and at 1:1, 7:27, and 12:9) specifically to provide the skeptical body of the book with an orthodox theological conclusion suitable for its inclusion in the canon, without this being read as fraudulent or illegitimate, but rather as a recognized and unremarkable feature of how wisdom literature was assembled and framed in the ancient world (a compositional question with some parallel to the Elihu speeches and Job 28 addressed in the companion Job document). Both readings preserve the epilogue's full canonical authority; they differ only on its precise compositional origin.

### 10. Solomonic authorship claimed -- yet strained by an odd detail and much later linguistic evidence

**References:** Ecclesiastes 1:1, 12, 16; Ecclesiastes 2:7, 9

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes 1:1 identifies its speaker as 'the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem,' traditionally understood as Solomon, the only son of David to reign as king over a united Israel from Jerusalem. Yet 1:16 has the speaker claim, 'I have acquired great wisdom, surpassing all who were over Jerusalem before me' -- a curious statement if the speaker were Solomon, since only one king (David, his own father) had reigned in Jerusalem before him, making 'all who were over Jerusalem before me' an odd plural for a single predecessor. Separately, the book's Hebrew contains a number of late linguistic features (including apparent Aramaic and Persian loanwords) that many scholars -- both critical and a number of conservative -- read as indicating a substantially later date of composition than Solomon's tenth-century-BC reign.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Ecclesiastes 1:1, 12, 16 and 2:7, 9 exactly as given, without resolving the authorship question through their wording; this is a matter of historical and linguistic scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated question among careful scholars across the theological spectrum, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Those defending Solomonic authorship offer several responses to the 1:16 detail: some note that Jerusalem had been ruled by various Canaanite (Jebusite) kings for many centuries before David's conquest of the city (2 Samuel 5), and that Solomon's statement may reasonably be read as including all of these earlier rulers of the same city in his comparison, not only his single Israelite predecessor; others note the phrase need not refer strictly to kings at all, but more broadly to 'all who were' wise or notable in Jerusalem before him. On the linguistic evidence, defenders of Solomonic authorship note Solomon's extensive international trade and diplomatic contacts (1 Kings 10) could plausibly account for early loanwords entering his own vocabulary, while critics respond that the specific density and character of the linguistic features found throughout the book more strongly suggest a much later linguistic environment than isolated loanwords would produce. Many conservative scholars today hold a mediating position: that the book preserves substantially Solomonic content and wisdom (consistent with its detailed, specific self-descriptions of unmatched wealth, building projects, and wisdom matching Solomon's own biography in 1 Kings) later compiled, edited, or given its final linguistic form by someone else -- a position that, like the discussion of Nehemiah 12's high priestly genealogy addressed in the companion Nehemiah document, treats later editorial involvement as compatible with substantial authentic authorship rather than as a wholesale fabrication.

### 11. 'Eat, drink, and find enjoyment' -- a recurring counter-refrain to the dominant 'vanity' theme

**References:** Ecclesiastes 2:24; Ecclesiastes 3:12-13; Ecclesiastes 5:18-19; Ecclesiastes 8:15; Ecclesiastes 1:2

**The claim:** Ecclesiastes' most famous refrain, repeated throughout the book, declares that everything is 'vanity' (or 'meaningless,' depending on translation) and 'a striving after wind' (1:2, 14, and repeatedly). Yet the same book just as persistently repeats a second, considerably more positive refrain at several key points: 'there is nothing better for a person than that he should eat and drink and find enjoyment in his toil' (2:24; similarly 3:12-13, 5:18-19, 8:15) -- language some read as difficult to square with the book's dominant pessimistic verdict.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both refrains plainly and consistently at each of their several repetitions throughout the book; the harmonization rests on recognizing the two refrains as complementary halves of the book's single, sustained argument, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these two refrains not as competing conclusions the book fails to resolve, but as its two deliberately paired central messages, held together throughout: the 'vanity' refrain consistently targets the futility of seeking ultimate, lasting meaning, control, or permanent achievement through purely human effort, wisdom, or pleasure-seeking pursued as an end in itself (the specific projects and pursuits chronicled at length in chapter 2), while the 'eat, drink, and enjoy' refrain consistently follows as the book's own positive, repeatedly offered alternative -- receiving each day's ordinary, God-given portion of food, work, and simple enjoyment gratefully, as a gift from God's own hand (explicitly grounded in this way at 2:24-25, 'apart from him who can eat or who can have enjoyment?'), rather than searching for a permanent, self-generated meaning that 'under the sun' human effort alone can never secure. Read together across the whole book, 'vanity' names what does not and cannot ultimately satisfy when pursued as life's own end, while 'eat, drink, and enjoy' names the modest, real, and genuinely good portion available to those who receive ordinary life gratefully from God rather than demanding it yield more permanent meaning than it can bear -- both are the book's own settled, complementary conclusions, not a contradiction it leaves unresolved.
