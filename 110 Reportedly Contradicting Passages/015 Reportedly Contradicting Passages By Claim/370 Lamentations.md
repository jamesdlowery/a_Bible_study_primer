# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Lamentations

This document catalogs 7 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable literary and
theological questions involving the book of Lamentations, either entirely internal to the book
or in comparison with Deuteronomy, James, 2 Kings, Jeremiah, and 1 Samuel. For each item, this
document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically
renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how
sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly
reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Lamentations is a short (5-chapter) book of unified poetic laments. This
document has 7 entries rather than the full 12 used for longer books in this series,
consistent with the approach already taken for Ruth, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and Song of
Solomon.

**A note on this book's literary character:** Entry 1 addresses one of the most admired and
well-documented literary features covered anywhere in this project -- the book's careful
acrostic structure across its first four chapters, and that structure's deliberate collapse in
the closing chapter. Entry 2 (traditional attribution to Jeremiah) is presented with genuine
scholarly diversity of opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entry 1's structural claims were checked against a wide, deliberately
diverse range of academic and devotional sources, all of which converge on the same account of
the acrostic pattern and its deliberate breakdown in chapter 5. The remaining entries are
theological and literary questions where all ten translations render the same underlying text
(to the extent an acrostic pattern in the Hebrew letters can be rendered at all in English) and
the harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. A carefully ordered acrostic structure -- that breaks down entirely in the final chapter

**References:** Lamentations 1; Lamentations 2; Lamentations 3; Lamentations 4; Lamentations 5

**The claim:** Lamentations 1, 2, and 4 are each 22-verse acrostic poems, with each verse beginning with a successive letter of the 22-letter Hebrew alphabet; chapter 3 extends this into a triple acrostic of 66 verses (three verses per letter), widely regarded as the book's structural and thematic high point. Chapter 5, the book's closing poem, also has exactly 22 verses -- but abandons the alphabetic pattern entirely, with no verse beginning with any particular Hebrew letter in sequence, raising a question about why the book's own carefully sustained literary structure collapses at precisely its final and most despairing chapter.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each chapter's actual verse count and content faithfully, though the Hebrew alphabetic acrostic pattern itself (a feature of the Hebrew letters beginning each verse) is necessarily invisible in English translation, since no English translation attempts to reproduce the acrostic in the target language; the LSB's translators' foreword specifically discusses this feature and its significance for readers, though the acrostic pattern itself is a matter of the underlying Hebrew, not of English wording choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as an error, oversight, or textual corruption, but as a widely recognized and deliberate literary and theological device. The great majority of commentators, across a wide range of theological and critical approaches, read the breakdown of the acrostic form in chapter 5 as intentionally mirroring and enacting the book's own content at that specific point. Having exhausted the alphabet's full range, 'from A to Z,' three times over in expressing the totality of Jerusalem's devastation across chapters 1-4, the poem's final movement ends not in resolution but in a tentative, unanswered plea: 'why do you forget us forever... unless you have utterly rejected us' (5:20, 22). This is widely read as using the very collapse of its own governing literary structure to enact, formally, the collapse of order, comprehension, and resolution the book's content describes -- refusing to impose a falsely tidy formal conclusion on genuinely unresolved grief. This is treated by scholars as one of the most studied and admired literary techniques in the entire Hebrew Bible, not as a flaw requiring explanation away.

### 2. Traditional attribution to Jeremiah -- absent from the Hebrew text itself

**References:** Lamentations 1:1

**The claim:** Lamentations is traditionally and near-universally attributed to the prophet Jeremiah, an association reflected in the ancient Greek Septuagint's added introductory note ('And it came to pass, after Israel was taken captive... Jeremiah sat weeping and wailed this lament over Jerusalem'), the Latin Vulgate, and the Jewish Talmud. The Hebrew text of Lamentations itself, however, never names its author at any point, and some modern scholars, noting certain stylistic and thematic differences between Lamentations and the book of Jeremiah, question whether Jeremiah personally composed all or any of its five poems.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the Hebrew text's own silence about its author consistently; several editions include a traditional introductory heading identifying the book with Jeremiah as an editorial or traditional note rather than as part of the inspired Hebrew text itself, following the same convention reflected in the ancient Septuagint and Vulgate.

**Reconciliation:** This is a matter of historical and literary authorship attribution rather than an internal contradiction, and this entry does not force a single resolution. Defenders of Jeremianic authorship note strong circumstantial support: Jeremiah was an eyewitness of Jerusalem's fall (he is explicitly present during and after the siege throughout his own book, e.g., Jeremiah 39-44), his own book records him composing laments for other occasions (2 Chronicles 35:25 explicitly credits Jeremiah with composing a lament for King Josiah's death, showing he was known as a composer of exactly this genre), and the ancient, unbroken tradition of attribution (reflected across Jewish and Christian sources for over two thousand years) carries real historical weight even without an explicit internal claim. Those questioning strict, sole Jeremianic authorship note the anonymous nature of the Hebrew text itself, along with observations that the book's five poems differ somewhat in perspective, tone, and specific theological emphasis from one another and from Jeremiah's own prophetic book, suggesting to some scholars that the collection may represent multiple authors or a communal composition process reflecting the shared grief of survivors, compiled and later associated with Jeremiah's well-known reputation as Judah's great lamenting prophet. Many conservative scholars hold that Jeremiah's authorship of at least the core material remains the best-supported and most traditional position, while acknowledging the text's own silence on the matter.

## Theological Tensions

### 3. 'The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases' -- a burst of hope in the middle of unrelenting despair

**References:** Lamentations 3:22-24; Lamentations 2:20; Lamentations 4:10; Lamentations 5:20-22

**The claim:** Lamentations 3:22-24 contains one of the most beloved, hope-filled passages in the entire Bible -- 'The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness' -- appearing in the near-exact center of a book otherwise devoted almost entirely to unrelenting grief and horror over Jerusalem's destruction, including graphic descriptions of mothers driven to eating their own children during the siege (2:20, 4:10) and a closing chapter that ends not in resolution but in an anguished, unanswered question ('why do you forget us forever?... unless you have utterly rejected us,' 5:20, 22).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Lamentations 3:22-24 and the book's surrounding laments plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the book's own deliberate structural and thematic design, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this juxtaposition not as an inconsistency but as the book's own carefully constructed theological center, reinforced by its structural placement: chapter 3's triple acrostic is both the longest and most personally reflective poem in the collection, and its turn toward hope (3:21, 'but this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope') occurs specifically after the poem has voiced some of the rawest personal anguish in the entire book (3:1-20 describes the speaker's own affliction in the darkest terms), showing that the hope expressed is not naive optimism detached from the surrounding suffering but a hard-won affirmation reached from within its very depths. The book's return to unresolved lament in chapter 5 is likewise not read as contradicting or undoing chapter 3's affirmation, but as honestly representing the ongoing, lived reality of grief that does not resolve neatly even after a genuine moment of theological clarity and hope -- the book as a whole is widely understood to model exactly this pattern (affirming God's faithfulness as a settled truth while continuing to voice raw, unresolved grief) rather than requiring one mood to cancel out or invalidate the other, a pattern this project has addressed in comparable terms regarding Ecclesiastes' and the Psalter's own coexistence of confident faith and honest lament.

### 4. 'Is it not from the mouth of the Most High that good and bad come?'

**References:** Lamentations 3:37-38; James 1:13

**The claim:** Lamentations 3:37-38 asks rhetorically, 'Who has spoken and it came to pass, unless the Lord has commanded it? Is it not from the mouth of the Most High that good and bad come?' -- language attributing both good and calamitous outcomes to God's own sovereign command, raising the same general question addressed elsewhere in this project regarding whether this is compatible with James 1:13's statement that God 'cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Lamentations 3:37-38 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same distinction addressed at greater length regarding Isaiah 45:7, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length in this project's general document set and the companion Isaiah document, both addressing the closely parallel language of Isaiah 45:7. As with that case, the Hebrew term here (ra) covers a broad range from moral evil to simple misfortune, calamity, or adversity, and this passage -- occurring in the specific context of a chapter reflecting on the legitimacy of complaint and affliction under God's sovereign governance of history (3:31-33 immediately qualifies this by affirming God 'does not afflict from his heart' or willingly bring grief) -- is generally read as affirming God's comprehensive sovereignty over all historical outcomes, including judgment and calamity brought upon a sinful nation, rather than asserting that God personally originates or tempts anyone toward moral wickedness, which is the specific claim James 1:13 denies.

### 5. 'Our fathers sinned... and we bear their iniquities' -- yet the same chapter also confesses, 'we have sinned'

**References:** Lamentations 5:7; Lamentations 5:16; Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20

**The claim:** Lamentations 5:7 states, 'Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities' -- language that reads as attributing the current generation's suffering to a preceding generation's guilt, in apparent tension with the individual-responsibility principle addressed at length elsewhere in this project (Deuteronomy 24:16, Ezekiel 18:20). Yet only nine verses later, the same chapter states plainly, 'the crown has fallen from our head; woe to us, for we have sinned!' (5:16) -- the community's own direct acknowledgment of its own, present guilt, seemingly in tension with 5:7's attribution of blame to the fathers.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both verses plainly and consistently as given, within the same short chapter; the harmonization rests on recognizing generational consequence and personal guilt as compatible, simultaneously true realities, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these two statements, occurring within the same short poem, as complementary rather than contradictory expressions of a single, complex reality -- one addressed at length regarding Exodus 20:5, Ezekiel 18, and Jeremiah 31:29-30 in this project's companion documents. A community can rightly acknowledge both that it now bears painful, ongoing consequences originally set in motion by earlier generations' accumulated unfaithfulness (5:7) -- a claim about historical causation and consequence, not a claim that the present generation is personally guilty of the fathers' specific sins -- and that it, too, has continued and added to that pattern of unfaithfulness in its own right and bears real, personal responsibility for its own sin (5:16). Both statements can be, and in the community's own honest self-reflection here are, simultaneously true without contradiction. This is precisely the kind of layered, both/and acknowledgment of generational consequence and personal guilt this project has addressed as compatible, rather than competing, claims in several other contexts.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 6. Mothers eating their own children during the siege -- a horrifying, specifically predicted covenant curse

**References:** Lamentations 2:20; Lamentations 4:10; Deuteronomy 28:53-57

**The claim:** Lamentations twice describes, in the starkest possible terms, mothers driven by starvation during the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem to eat their own children ('should women eat the fruit of their womb, the children of their tender care?' 2:20; 'the hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children,' 4:10) -- among the most horrifying images in the entire Bible.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both Lamentations passages and Deuteronomy 28:53-57 plainly and consistently as given, without softening the horror of either; there is no translation variance bearing on this entry, which addresses a fulfillment relationship rather than a contradiction.

**Reconciliation:** Rather than presenting a tension requiring resolution, this is generally read as a stark, historically documented fulfillment of a specific covenant curse spelled out in explicit, chilling detail centuries earlier. Josephus records a similar account during the later Roman siege of Jerusalem in AD 70. Deuteronomy 28:53-57 explicitly warns that persistent covenant unfaithfulness would result in exactly this horror during a future siege: 'you shall eat the fruit of your womb, the flesh of your sons and daughters... the most tender and refined woman among you... will begrudge to the husband of her bosom, to her son and to her daughter, her afterbirth.' This entry is included not as an alleged contradiction but as a notable case, similar to a few others addressed elsewhere in this project, where a specific, graphic prophetic warning finds a documented, exact historical fulfillment -- consistency, in this instance, rather than tension, between an earlier warning and a later historical account.

### 7. 'The LORD's anointed' -- applied to King Zedekiah, whose own unfaithfulness the book elsewhere condemns

**References:** Lamentations 4:20; 2 Kings 24:19-20; Jeremiah 52:1-3

**The claim:** Lamentations 4:20, lamenting the capture of the fleeing King Zedekiah described in 2 Kings 25:5-7, calls him 'the breath of our nostrils, the LORD's anointed' -- a title of high honor and divine sanction. Yet 2 Kings 24:19-20 and Jeremiah 52:1-3 both explicitly condemn Zedekiah's own reign as thoroughly unfaithful ('he did what was evil in the sight of the LORD'), and his rebellion against Babylon is presented as the direct proximate cause of Jerusalem's final destruction -- raising a question about how a condemned, unfaithful king can still be honored with the title 'the LORD's anointed.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Lamentations 4:20 and the condemnations of Zedekiah in 2 Kings and Jeremiah plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing the dignity of an office from a personal commendation of its holder's character, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read 'the LORD's anointed' (Hebrew mashiach, from which 'Messiah' derives) here as describing the dignity and divine sanction attached to the office of Davidic kingship itself, not as a personal commendation of Zedekiah's individual character or conduct -- a distinction with clear precedent elsewhere in Scripture, most notably in David's own repeated refusal to harm King Saul specifically because Saul was 'the LORD's anointed' (1 Samuel 24:6, 10; 26:9, 11), even though Saul himself had already been rejected by God for his own unfaithfulness (addressed at greater length in this project's companion 1 Samuel document). On this reading, Lamentations 4:20 laments the loss of the last legitimate holder of the divinely established Davidic office -- a real object of national grief regardless of that particular office-holder's personal failures -- consistent with, rather than contradicting, the same book's and the wider canon's clear condemnation of Zedekiah's own specific unfaithfulness as the proximate cause of the disaster being lamented.
