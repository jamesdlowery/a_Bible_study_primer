# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in James

This document catalogs 3 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving James, either
entirely internal to the letter or in comparison with the companion Romans and 2 Corinthians
documents, Matthew, and 2 Timothy. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 2 addresses the anointing of the
sick with oil (5:14-15), a genuinely and actively debated passage with real, ongoing
sacramental significance across major Christian traditions. Entry 3 resolves the apparent
tension between "God tempts no one" and the Lord's Prayer's "lead us not into temptation"
through the genuine, demonstrated semantic flexibility of the underlying Greek term
(peirasmos), which James's own chapter uses in both a positive ("trial") and negative
("temptation to sin") sense within the same eleven verses.

**A note on candor:** Entry 2 is presented with genuine denominational diversity of opinion
rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entries 2 and 3 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing the full range of positions on each question. The remaining entry is addressed
by full cross-reference to the companion Romans document.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine denominational
diversity remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to
consult the primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Theological Tensions

### 1. 'Justified by works and not by faith alone' -- addressed at length in the companion Romans document

**References:** James 2:14-26; Romans 3:28; Romans 4:1-5

**The claim:** James 2:24 states, 'you see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone' -- the primary source text for the classic tension with Paul's own statements in Romans (addressed at length in the companion Romans document), central to the doctrinal disputes of the Protestant Reformation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render James 2:14-26 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Romans document for the fuller discussion of the faith/works question.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Romans document, which addresses the standard resolution: Paul's argument addresses how a sinner is initially declared righteous before God (denying that human merit or law-observance can earn this status), while James addresses the separate question of how genuine, saving faith can be recognized as authentic through its observable fruit -- both authors agree that authentic faith and good works are inseparably connected, addressing the relationship from two different angles and against two different specific errors.

## Theological Interpretation

### 2. Anointing the sick with oil -- does this promise guaranteed healing, and what is the oil's own significance?

**References:** James 5:14-15

**The claim:** James 5:14-15 instructs, 'Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up.' Read as an unconditional promise, this appears to guarantee healing for anyone who receives this specific ritual -- a promise that does not match common experience, since not everyone prayed for and anointed in this way recovers from illness, and connecting to a genuine, actively practiced denominational difference regarding the significance of the oil itself and the ritual's own sacramental status.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render James 5:14-15 plainly and consistently as given; this remains an actively debated sacramental and pastoral question among major Christian traditions, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and actively debated passage with real, ongoing denominational significance, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. The Catholic Church, and, in a related but distinct form, Eastern Orthodoxy, reads this passage as the scriptural basis for the sacrament of Anointing of the Sick, historically called Extreme Unction in the West, understood as conferring grace, forgiveness of sins, and spiritual, and when it is God's will physical, strengthening through a priest's ministration. Most commentators across traditions note, though, that the passage itself describes anointing tied to sickness generally, not specifically to the point of death, meaning some historical practice narrowing the sacrament to a 'last rites' context goes beyond the passage's own stated occasion. Many Protestant and evangelical commentators reject a sacramental reading, holding that the oil itself carries no special power. On this view, the actual healing is attributed entirely to 'the prayer of faith' the passage names as the operative cause, with the oil serving a merely symbolic function of consecration or dedication to God, comparable to anointing's symbolic use elsewhere in Scripture, e.g. 1 Samuel 10:1. A minority of commentators propose the oil served a genuinely medicinal purpose common in the ancient world, citing Isaiah 1:6 and Luke 10:34's use of oil as a treatment, understood as complementing rather than competing with prayer. On the question of guaranteed healing, most commentators across these positions agree the passage should not be read as an absolute, unconditional formula guaranteeing physical recovery in every case regardless of other factors. The letter's own broader emphasis on God's sovereign will (4:13-15, 'if the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that') is generally read as implicitly qualifying this promise as well. This is consistent with the wider New Testament's own acknowledgment that faithful prayer for healing is not always followed by physical recovery in this life; compare Paul's own companion Trophimus, left sick in Miletus (2 Timothy 4:20), and Paul's own unhealed 'thorn in the flesh,' addressed in the companion 2 Corinthians document. This passage is generally read as a genuine, meaningful promise regarding the ordinary, expected pattern of believing prayer for the sick, exercised in submission to God's ultimate will, rather than a mechanical guarantee operating independently of it.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 3. 'God tempts no one' -- vs. the Lord's Prayer's petition, 'lead us not into temptation'

**References:** James 1:2-3, 12-14; Matthew 6:13

**The claim:** James 1:13 states plainly, 'Let no one say when he is tempted, "I am being tempted by God," for God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one.' Yet Jesus himself teaches his disciples to pray, in the Lord's Prayer, 'lead us not into temptation' (Matthew 6:13) -- a petition that seems to presuppose God could, at least in principle, lead someone into temptation, otherwise there would be no reason to ask him not to.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render James 1:2-3, 12-14, and Matthew 6:13 plainly and consistently as given, preserving the same underlying flexible term across both passages; the harmonization rests on recognizing this genuine semantic range (temptation to evil, versus trial or testing more broadly) rather than a contradiction, and is not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but as resting on the genuine flexibility of a single underlying Greek term (peirasmos, and its related verb peirazo) that both passages share, and which is translated variously across different contexts as 'temptation,' 'trial,' or 'testing' depending on which sense best fits the immediate context -- a flexibility James's own chapter itself demonstrates directly, using the same root word to describe circumstances believers should 'count... all joy' (1:2, clearly a positive 'trial' or 'test' sense) before turning, just eleven verses later, to warn against attributing this same experience's temptation toward evil specifically to God (1:13, clearly the negative 'enticement to sin' sense). Most commentators read James 1:13 as addressing this second, narrower sense specifically -- God never entices or lures anyone toward moral evil, since evil desire, James's own very next verse states, arises from a person's 'own desire' (1:14), not from God -- while the Lord's Prayer's petition addresses the broader, related sense of being led into circumstances of trial or difficult testing that could become an occasion for sin if not met with God's own provided help, asking God not merely for the absence of difficult testing, but specifically for protection from being overcome by it and delivered from the evil one within it (the petition's own second half, 'but deliver us from evil,' generally read as clarifying and completing the same single request rather than introducing an unrelated addition). On this reading, both passages are true and fully compatible: God himself never actively entices anyone toward sin (James's specific point), while believers are still taught to ask for God's protective guidance through the difficult trials and testing circumstances of life that could, apart from his help, become occasions for succumbing to sin (the Lord's Prayer's own specific request).
