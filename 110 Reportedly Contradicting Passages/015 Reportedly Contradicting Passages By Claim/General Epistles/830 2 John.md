# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in 2 John

This document catalogs 3 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving 2 John, one of
the shortest books in the New Testament (13 verses), either entirely internal to the letter or
in comparison with the companion 1 John document, Matthew, and Romans. For each item, this
document states the claim as it is typically raised and gives the standard scholarly
reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this letter's most significant entries:** Entry 3 addresses the genuinely debated
"two Johns" question -- whether "the elder" who wrote this letter (and 3 John) is the same
apostle who wrote the Gospel of John and 1 John, or a distinct historical figure known to
scholarship as "John the Elder," a question resting on famously thin and ambiguous evidence
from the early bishop Papias, known only through a later quotation by Eusebius.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1 and 3 are presented with genuine scholarly diversity of
opinion rather than a single forced resolution.

**Verification status:** Entries 1 and 3 were both checked in detail against multiple sources
representing the range of scholarly positions on each question, including the specific ancient
testimony (Papias, via Eusebius, and Irenaeus's competing account) underlying entry 3.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary text and the cross-references directly.

## Names and Identity

### 1. 'The elect lady' -- an individual woman, or a symbolic reference to a local church?

**References:** 2 John 1:1, 4-5, 13

**The claim:** 2 John is addressed to 'the elect lady and her children' (1:1), with the author expressing joy that 'some of your children' were 'walking in the truth' (1:4) and closing with greetings from 'the children of your elect sister' (1:13) -- language that has generated genuine, ongoing scholarly disagreement over whether 'the elect lady' names an actual individual woman (with 'children' referring literally to her own sons and daughters), or functions as a symbolic address to a local church (with 'children' referring to its members, and the 'elect sister' referring to the sister congregation from which the author writes).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 John 1:1, 4-5, and 13 plainly and consistently as given, without resolving the identification question in the text itself; this is a matter of historical and literary interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated identification question, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. Those favoring a literal individual reading (including GotQuestions and a Goodreads-hosted introduction) note that reading 'children' as anything other than actual offspring requires additional interpretive steps not obviously supported by the text's own plain wording, and that the letter's warm, personal tone throughout reads naturally as address to an individual and her family. Those favoring a church or congregational reading (including a Bible.org exegetical commentary and a 'Gospel Saves' introduction connecting the image to the broader biblical pattern of describing God's people as a bride or wife, e.g., Galatians 4:26) note that closing the letter with greetings from 'the children of your elect sister' more naturally suggests a parallel, sister congregation than a literal biological sister with her own children, and that this reading fits a documented ancient convention of addressing a congregation collectively as a woman or household. A mediating position (defended in detail by Marg Mowczko, and reflected in the Reading Acts commentary) holds 'the elect lady' was an actual individual -- specifically, a woman serving as the leader or host of a house church -- with 'her children' referring to the members of her household and congregation together, combining elements of both the individual and congregational readings. This project does not adjudicate between these positions.

## Ethical Tensions

### 2. 'Do not receive him into your house or give him any greeting' -- vs. the broader New Testament ethic of love and hospitality

**References:** 2 John 1:10-11; Matthew 5:44; Romans 12:20

**The claim:** 2 John 1:10-11 gives an unusually severe instruction regarding a specific category of false teacher: 'if anyone comes to you and does not bring this teaching, do not receive him into your house or give him any greeting, for whoever greets him takes part in his wicked works.' This raises a question about how this severe restriction -- refusing even a basic greeting -- relates to the broader New Testament ethic of love, hospitality, and even love extended toward one's enemies (Matthew 5:44, 'love your enemies'; Romans 12:20, 'if your enemy is hungry, feed him').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 John 1:10-11 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the narrow, specific scope of the instruction (refusing material support to itinerant teachers of a specific, defining doctrinal error) rather than a general command against ordinary courtesy or love, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this instruction as addressing a narrow, specific category of person and situation -- traveling teachers who denied the core, defining truth this letter and its companion 1 John repeatedly identify as essential (that Jesus Christ had genuinely 'come in the flesh,' 1:7, addressed at greater length in the companion 1 John document regarding the same community's struggle with early docetic or proto-Gnostic teaching) -- rather than establishing a general principle of refusing ordinary social courtesy, hospitality, or love to anyone holding views the author disagreed with. The specific practical concern in view is generally understood to be the ancient custom of itinerant teachers relying on local Christian households for lodging, material support, and a base from which to spread their own teaching to the wider community -- extending this specific kind of substantive, materially supportive hospitality (rather than an ordinary greeting encountered in the marketplace) to a teacher actively working to undermine the community's foundational confession of Christ would function as direct material and reputational support for that specific false teaching's spread, which the letter's own stated concern (1:8, 'so that you may not lose what we have worked for') is directly aimed at preventing. This is generally read as a narrow, situation-specific instruction protecting a vulnerable community from actively enabling and materially supporting the spread of a specific, defining doctrinal error, rather than as a general command overriding the broader New Testament ethic of love and kindness toward all people, including opponents, in ordinary social contexts.

## Literary Composition

### 3. 'The elder' -- the same John who wrote the Gospel and 1 John, or a distinct 'John the Elder'?

**References:** 2 John 1:1; 3 John 1:1

**The claim:** 2 John (and its companion letter, 3 John) both open with the author identifying himself only as 'the elder,' without further name -- unlike 1 John, which supplies no explicit self-identification at all. Tradition has long identified this 'elder' with the apostle John, author of the fourth Gospel and 1 John. Since at least the nineteenth century, however, a body of scholarship has proposed that a distinct historical figure, 'John the Elder,' separate from the apostle, may have authored these two shorter letters (and, in some versions of this theory, further Johannine material) -- a theory resting substantially on a single, difficult-to-interpret statement from the early second-century bishop Papias, preserved only in a later quotation by the fourth-century historian Eusebius.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 2 John 1:1 and 3 John 1:1 exactly as given, using only 'the elder' without further identification; this authorship question is a matter of historical scholarship resting on ancient testimony outside the biblical text itself, not resolved through translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated historical question resting on famously difficult, fragmentary evidence, and this entry presents the range of positions rather than a single certain answer. Papias's own original work does not survive; the only access to his statement is through Eusebius's own quotation and interpretation of it, in which Eusebius describes Papias listing 'John' twice -- once grouped among the apostles, and once separately, later in the same list, alongside a certain 'Aristion,' both described using the title 'elder' rather than 'apostle.' Eusebius himself concluded this showed 'there were two persons in Asia that bore the same name,' and this reading has been followed by a number of modern scholars proposing that this second, distinct 'John the Elder' -- not the apostle -- authored 2 and 3 John (and, in some versions of the theory, contributed to or authored the Gospel and Revelation as well). Other scholars (including, among ancient sources, Irenaeus, who described Papias simply as a 'hearer of John' without distinguishing two Johns) argue Eusebius may have misread or over-interpreted Papias's own original statement, which could equally be read as referring to the same single apostle John twice, first in a formal list of apostles and again in a separate, personal reminiscence -- meaning the entire 'two Johns' theory may rest on a mistaken inference by Eusebius rather than on Papias's own original, intended meaning. This project does not adjudicate this question, noting that it remains a genuinely disputed matter resting on frustratingly thin, ambiguous ancient evidence, quite distinct in kind from (though comparable in the genuine uncertainty it generates to) some of the other New Testament authorship questions addressed elsewhere in this series.
