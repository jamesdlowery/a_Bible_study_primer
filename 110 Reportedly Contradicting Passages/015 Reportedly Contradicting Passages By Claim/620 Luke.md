# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Luke

This document catalogs 17 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the Gospel of
Luke, either entirely internal to the book, in comparison with Luke's own second volume (Acts),
or in comparison with Matthew, John, and Josephus's historical record. For each item, this
document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the **KJV** specifically
renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own wording materially affects how
sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly
reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on this Gospel's most significant entries:** Entry 1 addresses the census under
Quirinius, widely regarded as the single most significant historical-chronological difficulty
in this Gospel, checked against a genuinely wide range of proposed solutions including a
peer-reviewed Journal of Biblical Literature article (Kochenash 2024). Entry 3 addresses a distinctive
internal question -- an apparent timeframe discrepancy between Luke's own Gospel and his own
later book, Acts -- checked against sources spanning conservative harmonization, specialized
academic study, and Bart Ehrman's more critical reading.

**A note on candor:** Entries 1 and 3 are presented with genuine scholarly diversity of
opinion rather than a single forced resolution. Entry 5 addresses a real internal tension
(the sword instruction and its subsequent rejection) with the standard symbolic reading rather
than treating the tension as illusory.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 1. The census under Quirinius -- the most significant historical-chronological question in Luke

**References:** Luke 2:1-5

**The claim:** Luke 2:1-2 dates Jesus' birth to a census 'while Quirinius was governor of Syria,' and the surrounding narrative places this same birth during the reign of Herod the Great (Luke 1:5, and Matthew's parallel account, Matthew 2:1). The Jewish historian Josephus, however, independently and explicitly dates Quirinius's governorship of Syria and his own well-documented census to AD 6 -- roughly ten years after Herod the Great's death in 4 BC, since Herod's death predates Quirinius's attested governorship by a full decade. This is widely regarded as one of the most significant historical-chronological difficulties in the entire New Testament.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 2:1-2 essentially identically, following the same underlying Greek text; the harmonization question addressed here is a matter of historical and linguistic interpretation (including a live translation question regarding whether the Greek more naturally reads 'this was the first census... while Quirinius was governor' or 'this census took place before Quirinius was governor'), not a matter the ten translations resolve differently from one another in their main text.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely and extensively debated historical question among serious scholars, including many with a high view of Luke's own historical reliability elsewhere, attested at multiple other points by independent archaeological and historical confirmation of Luke's specific titles, places, and officials. This entry presents the leading proposed resolutions rather than a single confident answer. First, some scholars, following the second-century church father Tertullian's own explicit statement that the relevant census occurred under a different, earlier governor named Saturninus, propose that Quirinius held some other administrative role specifically overseeing the census itself, distinct from but coordinated with the sitting governor's own office, at that earlier date. Luke's own wording, on this view, may accurately describe Quirinius's specific administrative involvement without requiring him to have held the full title of governor at that time. Second, other scholars, citing inscriptional evidence including the fragmentary 'Lapis Tiburtinus' inscription, which some read as referring to an individual who served as legate of Syria on two separate occasions, propose Quirinius held an earlier, otherwise poorly attested governorship or command in the region before his later, better-documented AD 6 tenure, allowing an earlier census under his own authority to have occurred during Herod's own final years. Third, a linguistic solution holds that Luke's own Greek wording, more literally translated, could mean 'this census took place before Quirinius was governor of Syria' rather than 'this was the first census taken while Quirinius was governor,' effectively distinguishing the census Luke describes from the later, better-attested one Josephus records. Fourth, some scholars propose Luke's own chronological framing serves a primarily theological and literary purpose, deliberately associating Jesus' birth narrative with the same Quirinius census Luke's own later work, Acts 5:37, explicitly references in a different context, rather than functioning as a strictly precise historical anchor. Michael Kochenash develops this reading at length ("The Census in Luke 2: Using Josephus to Make Sense of Luke's Irreconcilable Chronology," Journal of Biblical Literature 143, no. 3 (2024): 503-522). This project does not adjudicate between these proposed solutions. The question remains a genuinely live one among careful historians and biblical scholars, some of whom continue to regard it as Luke's most significant historical difficulty and others of whom regard one or more of the above harmonizations as adequate.

### 2. A remarkably precise, multi-anchor historical dating for John the Baptist's ministry

**References:** Luke 3:1-2

**The claim:** Luke 3:1-2 dates the beginning of John the Baptist's ministry with unusual precision, anchoring it to no fewer than six separate historical reference points simultaneously: 'the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar, Pontius Pilate being governor of Judea, and Herod being tetrarch of Galilee, and his brother Philip tetrarch of the region of Ituraea and Trachonitis, and Lysanias tetrarch of Abilene, in the high-priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas' -- a level of historical specificity unmatched anywhere else in the four Gospels, and independently checkable against known Roman and Jewish administrative history.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 3:1-2's six historical anchors exactly as given; this entry addresses a matter of historical corroboration, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction, but is included here, similar to comparable cases addressed elsewhere in this project (Amos's earthquake dating, Nahum's chronological window), as a notable point of historical corroboration -- five of the six figures Luke names are independently and precisely attested in Roman and Jewish sources with dates matching Luke's own implied chronology (roughly AD 28-29) without difficulty. The sixth figure, 'Lysanias, tetrarch of Abilene,' was for some time treated as a possible anachronism by critical scholars, since an earlier, well-attested ruler of the same name and region (a king, not tetrarch, of Chalcis) had died decades before Luke's implied date -- however, subsequent archaeological discovery of an inscription referring to a 'Lysanias the tetrarch' at Abila, datable to precisely the period Luke describes, is now generally read by most scholars as confirming a second, later ruler of the same name held the specific position and title Luke describes, resolving what had been regarded as one of Luke's more significant potential historical errors and instead becoming a frequently cited example of Luke's own demonstrated historical carefulness being subsequently vindicated by later archaeological discovery.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 3. Luke's own resurrection-to-ascension timeframe: a single day in the Gospel, forty days in Acts -- by the same author

**References:** Luke 24:1-53; Acts 1:1-11

**The claim:** Luke's Gospel narrates the resurrection, the Emmaus road appearance, Jesus' appearance to the assembled disciples, and his ascension all within what reads as a single continuous day (24:1-53, with 24:13 explicitly stating the Emmaus encounter occurred 'that very day,' and no time indicator anywhere in the chapter suggesting more than one day elapses before the ascension at Bethany in 24:50-51). Acts 1:3, however -- written by the same author, and explicitly presented as a continuation of the same 'first book' addressed to the same recipient, Theophilus (Acts 1:1) -- states that Jesus 'presented himself alive... appearing to them during forty days, and speaking about the kingdom of God' before his ascension, a considerably longer timeframe than Luke 24's own narrative appears to describe.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 24 and Acts 1:1-11 plainly and consistently as given, without harmonizing the apparent timeframe difference in the text itself; some translations note the textual variant at Luke 24:51 discussed above.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated internal question -- notable precisely because it involves the same author's own two connected works, rather than a comparison between two different authors' independent accounts -- and this entry presents the range of proposed solutions rather than a single certain answer. Some commentators, including the more critical scholar Bart Ehrman, read this as a genuine compositional inconsistency, proposing that Luke initially intended his Gospel to conclude with the ascension occurring on the same day as the resurrection, and that Acts 1:3's forty-day span represents either a later theological development in Luke's own thinking between writing the two volumes, or an admission that Luke was not attempting a strict chronological account in his Gospel's own closing verses. A widely offered harmonization instead reads Luke 24's own narrative as a deliberately compressed literary summary that does not explicitly claim every event occurred on a single day -- the chapter simply does not supply the kind of explicit time-lapse notices (such as 'some days later' or 'after this') found elsewhere in ancient historical writing to mark a gap, a stylistic compression rather than an explicit, contradictory chronological claim, allowing the fuller forty-day period from Acts to be read into the apparent gap between 24:49 and 24:50 without requiring either text to be corrected. A further proposal, discussed in specialized New Testament scholarship on Luke-Acts, holds that Luke 24:51's specific phrase describing Jesus being 'carried up into heaven' is itself a disputed textual variant (omitted in some early manuscripts), and that if this specific phrase is a later addition, Luke's Gospel may never have explicitly narrated a visible ascension at all, with Acts 1:9's account representing the first and only visible ascension narrative in Luke's combined two-volume work -- removing the apparent contradiction entirely on this specific textual-critical reading, though this does not resolve every aspect of the broader question. This project does not adjudicate between these proposed solutions.

### 4. Luke's own genealogy of Jesus -- addressed at length in the companion Matthew document

**References:** Luke 3:23-38; Matthew 1:1-17

**The claim:** Luke's genealogy of Jesus (3:23-38) diverges substantially from Matthew's own genealogy (1:1-17), addressed at length in the companion Matthew document as one of the most extensively discussed harmonization questions in the New Testament. Luke's version is also distinctive in tracing the line all the way back to Adam (rather than stopping at Abraham, as Matthew does) and in explicitly qualifying Joseph's paternity with the phrase 'being the son, as was supposed, of Joseph' (3:23).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 3:23-38 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Matthew document for the fuller discussion of this genealogy's relationship to Matthew's own.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Matthew document, which addresses the majority reading (Matthew tracing Joseph's legal, royal descent through Solomon; Luke tracing Mary's own biological descent through Nathan, with Joseph listed as Heli's son-in-law per ancient convention) and the reasons this reading is generally preferred. Luke's own distinctive extension of the genealogy back to Adam (rather than stopping at Abraham) is generally read as consistent with this Gospel's own broader emphasis, relative to Matthew's more specifically Jewish-audience-oriented Gospel, on Jesus' significance for all humanity rather than for Israel specifically -- Adam, unlike Abraham, is the shared ancestor of all people rather than specifically of the Jewish nation, a detail consistent with Luke's demonstrated interest elsewhere in Gentile inclusion and universal salvation.

### 5. 'Let him who has no sword sell his cloak and buy one' -- followed shortly by a rebuke for using one

**References:** Luke 22:35-38; Luke 22:49-51; Matthew 26:52

**The claim:** At the Last Supper, Jesus instructs his disciples, 'let the one who has no sword sell his cloak and buy one' (22:36), and when the disciples report having two swords already, Jesus responds, 'it is enough' (22:38) -- language read by some as a direct instruction to arm themselves. Only hours later, in Gethsemane, when a disciple (identified in John 18:10 as Peter) uses a sword to strike the high priest's servant, Jesus immediately rebukes the action, saying, according to Matthew's parallel account, 'put your sword back into its place, for all who take the sword will perish by the sword' (Matthew 26:52), and, per Luke's own account, heals the wounded servant (22:51) -- raising a question about whether Jesus' own earlier instruction to acquire swords is compatible with his subsequent, sharp rejection of their actual use.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 22:35-38, Luke 22:49-51, and Matthew 26:52 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading Jesus' instruction to acquire swords as symbolic preparation rather than a literal command to armed resistance, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Jesus' instruction in 22:36 as symbolic or preparatory rather than as a literal command to engage in armed combat -- the immediately following verses make clear Jesus is speaking about an approaching, dramatic change in circumstances ('for I tell you that this Scripture must be fulfilled in me: And he was numbered with the transgressors,' 22:37, quoting Isaiah 53:12), preparing the disciples for a period of hostility and danger rather than instructing them toward literal violent resistance; Jesus' own response to the disciples' report of having two swords already ('it is enough,' 22:38) is widely read as a mild, somewhat exasperated closing of a conversation the disciples had misunderstood too literally, rather than confirmation that two swords would be an adequate armed force for eleven men facing an arresting party. This reading is directly confirmed by Jesus' own explicit and immediate rejection of actually using the sword just hours later, which most commentators treat as clarifying, rather than contradicting, his earlier symbolic point -- the swords' purpose, on this reading, was never intended for combative use, and the disciples' own literal misunderstanding of Jesus' figurative statement, followed by their attempt to use a sword literally, is precisely the misunderstanding Jesus corrects immediately upon its occurrence.

### 6. The Sermon on the Plain -- addressed at length in the companion Matthew document

**References:** Luke 6:17-49; Matthew 5:1-7:29

**The claim:** Luke's account of Jesus' extended ethical teaching, delivered 'on a level place' (6:17), differs in setting, length, and specific wording from Matthew's Sermon on the Mount, addressed at length in the companion Matthew document, including Luke's own distinctive series of 'woes' addressed to the rich and comfortable (6:24-26), absent from Matthew's version.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 6:17-49 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Matthew document for the fuller discussion of this question.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Matthew document, which presents both major positions -- that this represents a single occasion reported with each Gospel's own selective compression and emphasis, or two genuinely distinct occasions on which Jesus delivered substantially similar core teaching -- without adjudicating between them. Luke's own distinctive inclusion of explicit 'woes' balancing the Beatitudes is generally read as consistent with this Gospel's demonstrated broader concern, relative to Matthew, with economic reversal and God's particular concern for the poor (a theme reflected at several other points unique to Luke's Gospel, including the Magnificat, 1:46-55, and the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, 16:19-31).

### 7. Luke's own resurrection account -- addressed at length in the companion Matthew document

**References:** Luke 24:1-12; Matthew 28:1-10; Mark 16:1-8; John 20:1-18

**The claim:** Luke's resurrection account describes an unspecified larger group of women finding the tomb empty and encountering 'two men... in dazzling apparel' (24:4), without the direct encounter with the risen Jesus at the tomb itself that Matthew's account includes -- one of several specific points of difference among the four Gospels' resurrection narratives addressed at length in the companion Matthew document.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 24:1-12 plainly and consistently as given; see the companion Matthew document for the fuller discussion of the resurrection narratives' differing details across all four Gospels.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated fully in the companion Matthew document, which addresses the standard approach to these differing resurrection-narrative details: each Gospel's own selective emphasis and access to different eyewitness testimony, combined over a short span of time and multiple overlapping visits to the tomb by different individuals and groups, rather than any author's carelessness or fabrication, with all four Gospels agreeing on the central, most theologically significant facts (the tomb was empty, angelic messengers were present, and Jesus was seen alive afterward).

### 8. Who washed Jesus's feet with her tears and dried them with her hair -- and is this the same woman and event described in the other Gospels?

**References:** Luke 7:36-50; Matthew 26:6-13; Mark 14:3-9; John 12:1-8

**The claim:** Luke 7:36-50 describes an unnamed 'woman... which was a sinner' anointing and washing Jesus's feet with her tears and hair at a Pharisee's house early in Jesus's Galilean ministry. Matthew 26:6-13 and Mark 14:3-9 describe an unnamed woman anointing Jesus's head at Simon the leper's house in Bethany, shortly before the crucifixion. John 12:1-8 describes Mary of Bethany anointing Jesus's feet and wiping them with her hair, also at Bethany, shortly before the crucifixion. This has led some readers, and a long tradition in Western art and popular piety, to conflate all these accounts into a single woman (often identified with Mary Magdalene), while others read them as describing two or three distinct incidents.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most contemporary biblical scholars, including conservative commentators, distinguish at least two, and quite plausibly three, separate incidents: Luke's account is explicitly set earlier, in Galilee, involving an unnamed 'sinner' whose specific identity Luke does not disclose (and whom Luke's own text nowhere identifies with Mary Magdalene, a separate figure Luke introduces only in the very next chapter, 8:2, without any indication of prior connection to this story); Matthew's and Mark's account, and John's account, both occur later, in Bethany, shortly before the crucifixion, and are widely read as the same single event, with John additionally and specifically naming the woman as Mary of Bethany (sister of Martha and Lazarus) -- a different woman, at a different time and place, from Luke's earlier, unnamed penitent sinner in Galilee. The popular tradition identifying Luke's sinful woman with Mary Magdalene has no explicit textual basis in any Gospel and is generally regarded by modern biblical scholarship (including, notably, a formal 1969 clarification by the Roman Catholic Church's own liturgical calendar reforms, which separated the feast days of Mary Magdalene, Mary of Bethany, and Luke's unnamed sinner) as a later devotional conflation of at least two, and likely three, originally distinct women and occasions.

### 9. When did Satan enter Judas -- before the Last Supper, or during it?

**References:** Luke 22:3-6; John 13:27

**The claim:** Luke 22:3 states, 'Then entered Satan into Judas,' immediately before Judas goes to negotiate his betrayal with the chief priests -- placed before the Last Supper in Luke's narrative sequence. John 13:27, however, places a description of Satan entering Judas at the Last Supper itself, immediately 'after the sop' Jesus gives him.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read these as describing two distinct stages in a progressive deepening of satanic influence over Judas, rather than two competing claims about a single moment: Luke 22:3 describes an initial temptation or entrance that prompts Judas's original decision to seek out the chief priests and negotiate the betrayal, while John 13:27 describes a further, more complete and decisive possession or influence at the Supper itself, marking the point at which Judas's course became final and irreversible, immediately preceding his departure to carry out the betrayal already set in motion. A progressive intensification of the same influence across two distinct moments in the same unfolding sequence of events is a coherent and unremarkable reading, not a contradiction about a single fixed instant.

### 10. Did Jesus drink the vinegar offered to him on the cross, or refuse it?

**References:** Luke 23:36; Matthew 27:34, 48; John 19:28-30

**The claim:** Luke 23:36 describes soldiers offering Jesus vinegar as an act of mockery during the crucifixion, without specifying whether he drank it. Matthew 27:34 has Jesus, before the crucifixion, taste but refuse a drugged wine mixture. John 19:28-30, near the moment of death, has Jesus say 'I thirst,' after which he is given vinegar on a sponge, which -- per John's account -- he receives, immediately followed by his statement 'It is finished.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The Gospels together describe two separate offerings at two different points, not a single, ambiguous event: an earlier offering, before the crucifixion began, of a drugged wine mixture customarily given as a merciful sedative to those about to be crucified, which Jesus tastes and deliberately refuses (Matthew 27:34; Mark 15:23), apparently choosing to face the crucifixion with full clarity; and a later, separate offering, near the moment of death, of plain sour wine or vinegar on a sponge, extended in response to his stated thirst, which he does receive (John 19:28-30). Luke 23:36's brief mention of soldiers offering vinegar in mockery is most naturally read as referring to this general mocking gesture repeated or continued at some point during the crucifixion, compatible with either or both of the more detailed offerings the other Gospels describe, rather than asserting a single, exclusive claim about whether Jesus drank on the cross at all.

## Theological Interpretation

### 11. 'Today you will be with me in paradise' -- a comma-dependent translation question, and its relationship to John's post-resurrection statement

**References:** Luke 23:43; John 20:17

**The claim:** Luke 23:43 records Jesus' words to the repentant thief crucified beside him: 'Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise' -- understood by most Christian traditions as promising the thief's soul would be with Jesus in paradise that very day, following their deaths. Ancient Greek manuscripts contain no punctuation, however, meaning the placement of the comma is entirely an editorial and translational decision rather than something the original text itself specifies -- some minority translations (most notably those produced by or associated with Jehovah's Witnesses, who hold a distinctive theology regarding the nature and timing of the afterlife) instead punctuate the sentence 'Truly I say to you today, you will be with me in paradise,' shifting 'today' to modify the act of speaking rather than the timing of the promised presence in paradise, thereby removing any claim about exactly when the thief would join Jesus there. Separately, John 20:17 has the resurrected Jesus tell Mary Magdalene, 'I have not yet ascended to the Father' -- raising a question of whether this is compatible with Jesus and the thief both being 'in paradise' together on the very day of the crucifixion, days before this later statement to Mary.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations surveyed in this project render Luke 23:43 with the traditional punctuation ('today you will be with me in paradise'); the minority punctuation ('I say to you today, you will be with me in paradise') is not reflected in any of the ten, and is associated primarily with translations produced outside this survey's scope.

**Reconciliation:** On the punctuation question: the overwhelming majority of translations and scholars, including those with no doctrinal stake in the outcome, favor the traditional punctuation ('today you will be with me in paradise'), on both grammatical grounds (the Greek word order and typical usage patterns in Luke's own writing elsewhere favor this reading) and consistency with the broader immediate narrative context, which is specifically and urgently about the timing of the thief's own hoped-for vindication ('remember me when you come into your kingdom,' 23:42), making a reading that removes any specific promise about timing considerably less contextually fitting; the alternative punctuation is generally regarded by mainstream biblical scholarship as driven by prior theological commitment rather than by the grammar or context of the passage itself. On the relationship to John 20:17: most commentators distinguish between the Hebrew and broader ancient Jewish concept of 'paradise' or an intermediate blessed state (understood as a form of conscious presence with God or with the righteous dead prior to a fuller, final resurrection and ascension) from the specific, later theological concept of a completed ascension to the Father's own full heavenly presence and glory -- on this reading, Jesus' spirit (in his human nature) could be truly and consciously 'with' the thief in this intermediate paradisal state immediately following death, while the fuller, glorified ascension to the Father described in John 20:17 (referring to a later, distinct event following the physical resurrection) remained still future at the specific moment John's Gospel records, without these two claims describing the same specific state or event.

## Literary Composition

### 12. Luke's own explicit statement of his historiographical method -- a unique feature among the four Gospels

**References:** Luke 1:1-4

**The claim:** Luke opens his Gospel with an explicit, first-person statement of his own compositional method and purpose, unique among the four canonical Gospels: 'Inasmuch as many have undertaken to compile a narrative of the things that have been accomplished among us, just as those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses... it seemed good to me also, having followed all things closely for some time past, to write an orderly account for you, most excellent Theophilus, that you may have certainty concerning the things you have been taught' (1:1-4).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 1:1-4 plainly and consistently as given; this entry addresses a distinctive literary and historiographical feature, not a contradiction, and is not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a point of contradiction, but a distinctive literary feature this project highlights explicitly, since none of the other three Gospels includes a comparable direct statement of authorial method, sources, and purpose. Luke's explicit acknowledgment that 'many' earlier written accounts already existed before his own (consistent with the majority scholarly view that Luke drew on Mark's Gospel and at least one other source, commonly designated 'Q,' among his own written sources), combined with his stated claim to have 'followed all things closely' and consulted eyewitness testimony, is widely read by scholars, including many outside any particular theological tradition, as placing Luke's Gospel self-consciously within the recognized genre conventions of ancient Greco-Roman historiography, which regularly included this kind of explicit methodological preface -- a genre marker some scholars read as lending Luke's own narrative claims additional historiographical weight relative to the other Gospels' more unadorned narrative openings, though this project does not treat this as settling any specific historical question addressed elsewhere regarding Luke's content.

### 13. Did Judas remain among the twelve through the Last Supper, and did he receive communion?

**References:** Luke 22:19-23; Matthew 26:20-29; Mark 14:17-25; John 13:21-30

**The claim:** Luke's account of the Last Supper places Jesus's institution of the bread and cup (Luke 22:19-20) before his announcement that his betrayer is present at the table (Luke 22:21-23) -- in this sequence, Judas appears still to be present when Jesus institutes what became the Lord's Supper. Matthew (26:20-29) and Mark (14:17-25), by contrast, both narrate the betrayal announcement before the institution of the bread and cup, and John's account (13:21-30) has Jesus identify Judas with a piece of bread and explicitly narrates Judas leaving the gathering immediately afterward, with no mention of the Eucharistic institution following. This raises a specific, historically debated question: was Judas still present, and did he receive the bread and cup, when Jesus instituted the Lord's Supper?

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Luke 22:19-23, along with the parallel accounts in Matthew, Mark, and John, plainly and consistently as given; the question of Judas's presence at the institution turns on how the accounts' differing sequences are harmonized, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This has been a genuine subject of theological debate since antiquity, without a single settled consensus. Prominent early church fathers, including Augustine and John Chrysostom, read Luke's account as reflecting the actual chronological order of events and concluded that Judas was present for, and received, the bread and cup before departing -- understanding this as an act of extraordinary divine patience, giving Judas one final opportunity to repent even after his betrayal was already underway. An alternative, and arguably more common, harmonization instead treats Luke's sequence as topically rather than strictly chronologically arranged (a recognized feature of ancient historiography, and one Luke's own Gospel displays elsewhere), and gives interpretive priority to Matthew, Mark, and John's shared order, under which the betrayal is identified and Judas departs (explicitly narrated in John 13:30) before the institution of the bread and cup that follows in Matthew 26:26-29 and Mark 14:22-25 -- meaning Judas would not have been present for the Eucharistic institution in this reading. The Roman Catholic magisterium has not dogmatically resolved the question, and it remains an open matter of scholarly and pastoral discussion rather than a claim any Gospel writer would have regarded as inconsistent with the others.

## Genealogy

### 14. Was Joseph's father Heli, or Jacob?

**References:** Luke 3:23; Matthew 1:16

**The claim:** Luke 3:23 names Joseph's father as 'Heli.' Matthew 1:16 instead names Joseph's father as 'Jacob.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This specific question is part of the broader genealogy-harmonization question addressed at length in this document's entry on Luke's genealogy generally, and in the companion Matthew document: the majority position holds that Matthew traces Joseph's own legal, biological paternal line (Jacob being Joseph's actual father), while Luke traces Mary's own biological line, with Joseph listed as Heli's son not by blood but by marriage -- Heli being Mary's father and thus Joseph's father-in-law, listed according to the ancient convention of reckoning a son-in-law as a 'son' in a legal genealogy lacking a male heir of his own body. On this reading, Jacob was Joseph's biological father (per Matthew) and Heli was Joseph's father-in-law, Mary's father (per Luke) -- both genealogies accurately naming a different, correctly-related 'father' figure for Joseph depending on which line (legal/paternal versus biological/maternal) each Gospel is tracing.

## Theological Tensions

### 15. Was Mary blessed, given that Jesus redirects a woman's praise of her away from mere motherhood?

**References:** Luke 11:27-28; Luke 1:42, 48

**The claim:** When a woman in the crowd calls out, 'Blessed is the womb that bare thee, and the paps which thou hast sucked,' Jesus responds, 'Yea rather, blessed are they that hear the word of God, and keep it' (11:27-28) -- language some read as Jesus deflecting or even denying his mother's blessedness. Yet Luke's own Gospel elsewhere has Elizabeth declare Mary 'blessed... among women' (1:42) and has Mary herself prophesy that 'all generations shall call me blessed' (1:48).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jesus's response is best read not as denying Mary's blessedness (which this same Gospel elsewhere clearly affirms) but as redirecting the woman's praise toward its truest and deepest basis: rather than mere physical motherhood -- an accident of biology the woman's exclamation focuses on -- Jesus identifies genuine, ultimate blessedness with hearing and keeping God's word, a description that, far from excluding Mary, describes her own defining characteristic throughout Luke's infancy narrative (her own response to the angel's message, 'be it unto me according to thy word,' 1:38, and Luke's own note that she 'kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart,' 2:19, 51). On this reading, Jesus's statement actually affirms the true source of Mary's own blessedness -- her faithful hearing and keeping of God's word -- over the more superficial basis (mere physical maternity) the woman's exclamation had emphasized, rather than denying that Mary was blessed at all.

### 16. Who are wiser -- the children of this world, or the children of light?

**References:** Luke 16:8; 1 Corinthians 1:20; 1 Corinthians 3:19

**The claim:** Luke 16:8, concluding the parable of the shrewd (or 'unjust') steward, has Jesus observe that 'the children of this world are in their generation wiser than the children of light' -- seemingly commending worldly wisdom. Paul, however, states that 'the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God' (1 Corinthians 3:19) and asks, 'hath not God made foolish the wisdom of this world?' (1 Corinthians 1:20).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Jesus's statement in the parable is a descriptive observation about practical shrewdness and diligence in pursuing one's own goals, not a moral or spiritual endorsement of worldly wisdom as ultimately wise in the sense Paul denies. The parable's own point, drawn explicitly in the following verses (16:9), is that Jesus's followers should bring the same kind of purposeful, forward-looking shrewdness to their eternal, spiritual priorities that worldly people characteristically bring to their temporal, self-interested ones -- a challenge to imitate the world's practical diligence in service of a categorically different, spiritually wise goal, not a claim that the world's wisdom is truly or ultimately wise in the deeper theological sense Paul addresses (the world's fundamental orientation apart from God, which Paul consistently calls foolish regardless of its practical cleverness in pursuing worldly ends).

### 17. Can a thief go to heaven, given Paul's statement that thieves will not inherit the kingdom of God?

**References:** Luke 23:39-43; 1 Corinthians 6:9-11

**The claim:** Luke 23:39-43 has Jesus promise the repentant criminal crucified beside him, 'today shalt thou be with me in paradise.' 1 Corinthians 6:9-10, however, states plainly that 'thieves... shall [not] inherit the kingdom of God.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 1 Corinthians 6:9-10's list describes unrepentant, ongoing patterns of life characterizing those who persist in such sins without turning to Christ -- and Paul's very next verse supplies the resolution directly within the same passage: 'and such were some of you: but ye are washed, but ye are sanctified, but ye are justified' (6:11), explicitly describing genuine conversion out of exactly these listed sins, including theft, as the normal Christian experience Paul is writing to describe among his own readers. The repentant thief on the cross, turning to Christ in genuine faith at the point of death and receiving Christ's own promise of paradise, is a clear instance of precisely this kind of conversion from a life of thieving to faith in Christ -- 1 Corinthians 6:9-10 excludes the unrepentant, persistent practice of such sins from inheriting the kingdom, not everyone who has ever committed theft and then genuinely turned to Christ, which is exactly the transformation the thief on the cross experiences in Luke's own narrative.
