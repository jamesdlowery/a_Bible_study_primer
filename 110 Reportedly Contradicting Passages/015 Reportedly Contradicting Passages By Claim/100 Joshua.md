# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Joshua

This document catalogs 19 commonly cited alleged contradictions involving the book of
Joshua, either entirely internal to Joshua or between Joshua and a directly related passage
elsewhere (Deuteronomy, Numbers, Judges, 2 Samuel, Ezra, Nehemiah, Isaiah, Hebrews, James).
For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the
**KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own
wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives
the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Three entries in this set (5, 7, and 3) are presented without a single,
confident resolution, because serious, Scripture-affirming interpreters genuinely disagree --
whether the "long day" of Joshua 10 is literal or poetic, whether Rahab's commended faith
extends to endorsing her lie, and whether the Hebrew pronouns in Joshua 7:25 indicate Achan's
children were executed or merely present as witnesses. These are flagged as live questions
rather than given a manufactured harmonization, consistent with this project's practice
elsewhere (see the Levite-census entry in the companion Numbers document) of acknowledging
where the evidence genuinely does not settle the matter.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where none commands real consensus,
an honest account of the live disagreement. Readers are encouraged to consult the primary
texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Was the conquest of Canaan totally complete, or was much land left unconquered?

**References:** Joshua 11:23; Joshua 21:43-45; Joshua 13:1-6; Joshua 15:63; Joshua 16:10; Joshua 17:12-13; Judges 1:19-36

**The claim:** Joshua 11:23 states plainly, 'So Joshua took the whole land, according to all that the LORD had spoken to Moses... and the land had rest from war.' Joshua 21:43-45 goes further: 'the LORD gave to Israel all the land... not one word of all the good promises... had failed.' Yet the same book explicitly states, near the end of Joshua's life, 'there remains yet very much land to possess' (13:1), and repeatedly notes specific peoples that were not driven out (the Jebusites from Jerusalem, 15:63; the Canaanites in Gezer, 16:10; the Canaanites in the Manasseh territory, 17:12-13). Judges 1 catalogs an extensive further list of unconquered towns and peoples across nearly every tribal territory.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of these passages plainly and consistently as given, without softening either the totalizing summary statements or the specific lists of unconquered territory; the harmonization concerns recognizing the different senses in which 'the whole land' language functions, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Joshua's sweeping summary statements (11:23; 21:43-45) as describing the decisive breaking of organized, nation-wide Canaanite military resistance and the securing of Israel's overall possession of the land in covenant/legal terms -- the land had been given, and its major coalitions defeated, fulfilling God's promise in the sense that mattered most to the book's theological argument -- rather than a claim that literally every inhabitant of every town had already been displaced at that specific moment. The book's own later chapters (13:1-6 explicitly, immediately following the sweeping summary) and Judges 1 then supply the remaining, ongoing, town-by-town task left to each tribe individually, a task the text elsewhere attributes to Israel's own subsequent failure of resolve and obedience rather than to any deficiency in what God had accomplished (compare Judges 1:19, 27-33, and Judges 2:1-3's explicit rebuke). Summary statements of national-scale, decisive victory sitting alongside acknowledgment of substantial unfinished local work are a recognized feature of ancient Near Eastern conquest narratives generally, not unique to Joshua.

### 2. Jerusalem: captured and burned, yet the Jebusites continued dwelling there unconquered

**References:** Judges 1:8; Joshua 15:63; Judges 1:21; 2 Samuel 5:6-9

**The claim:** Judges 1:8 states, 'the men of Judah fought against Jerusalem and captured it and struck it with the edge of the sword and set the city on fire.' Yet Joshua 15:63 states that Judah 'could not drive out the Jebusites who lived in Jerusalem,' and Judges 1:21 similarly states that Benjamin 'did not drive out the Jebusites who lived in Jerusalem,' adding that they 'dwelt with the people of Benjamin in Jerusalem to this day' -- and 2 Samuel 5:6-9 records David having to capture the city from the Jebusites for the first time generations later.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of these passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing a raid or partial destruction from the securing of the city's fortified stronghold, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that Judges 1:8 describes a raid that inflicted real, serious damage on the lower or outer parts of the city (consistent with an ancient city's common division between a more exposed lower town and a well-fortified, more easily defended citadel or upper stronghold), without Judah's forces securing permanent occupation or control of the city as a whole -- the Jebusites evidently retained or recovered the city's fortified core, which is why Joshua 15:63, Judges 1:21, and the continued Jebusite presence 'to this day' all describe an unconquered population, and why David's later capture of the city (2 Samuel 5:6-9) is treated in the text as a first, decisive seizure of the stronghold itself, not a re-conquest of something Israel had already fully controlled since Joshua's day.

### 3. Ai left 'a heap of ruins to this day' -- yet later listed among inhabited towns

**References:** Joshua 8:28; Ezra 2:28; Nehemiah 7:32; Isaiah 10:28

**The claim:** Joshua 8:28 states that Joshua 'burned Ai and made it forever a heap of ruins, as it is to this day.' Yet centuries later, Ezra 2:28 and Nehemiah 7:32 both list 'Ai' (or 'Aija') among towns whose returning former residents are counted after the Babylonian exile, and Isaiah 10:28 mentions Ai as a town an invading army passes through on its way toward Jerusalem -- both apparently describing an inhabited, functioning settlement long after Joshua's destruction.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render 'Ai' consistently across all the relevant passages, without adjusting the name or adding a distinguishing note between the original site and any later resettlement; the harmonization is a matter of historical-geographical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization holds that the site was destroyed and left unoccupied at the time Joshua's narrative was substantially composed or its concluding notice added ('as it is to this day' being a formula elsewhere in the Old Testament indicating the situation at the time of writing or a subsequent editorial notice, not a claim binding for all subsequent history), and that the town was at some later point resettled or rebuilt nearby -- a common pattern for ancient Near Eastern sites, where a destroyed settlement's name persisted and was reapplied to a rebuilt town on or near the same location generations or centuries afterward (a pattern also seen with Jericho itself, destroyed in Joshua 6 and explicitly rebuilt under a curse centuries later in 1 Kings 16:34). The 'Aija'/'Ai' of Ezra, Nehemiah, and Isaiah is generally understood by commentators as this later resettlement, not a claim that Joshua's original destruction never actually happened or was not thorough.

### 4. Were twelve memorial stones set up at Gilgal, or left standing in the middle of the Jordan riverbed?

**References:** Joshua 4:1-9, 19-20

**The claim:** Joshua 4:3-8 has the LORD instruct that twelve men, one from each tribe, take twelve stones out of the Jordan riverbed and carry them to the camp, where Joshua sets them up at Gilgal (confirmed at 4:20). Joshua 4:9, in the midst of the same account, states that Joshua 'set up twelve stones in the midst of Jordan, in the place where the feet of the priests... stood' -- language some read as describing a second, separate set of twelve stones left in the riverbed itself.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read the text as describing two distinct memorials built from the crossing, both explicitly narrated: one set of twelve stones carried out of the river and erected at Gilgal as the primary, publicly visible monument (4:3-8, 20), and a second set of twelve stones placed in the riverbed itself, at the exact spot where the priests carrying the ark had stood, as a hidden, symbolic monument visible only when the river was low or dry (4:9) -- a memorial specifically to the miraculous crossing point itself, distinct in location and purpose from the Gilgal monument meant to prompt future generations' questions (4:6-7, 21-24). Ancient Near Eastern building projects of this kind are not infrequently accompanied by more than one commemorative marker, and nothing in the text requires the two accounts to describe a single set of stones narrated twice.

### 5. Who drove the descendants of Anak from Hebron -- Joshua, or Caleb?

**References:** Joshua 11:21-22; Joshua 15:13-14; Judges 1:20

**The claim:** Joshua 11:21-22 states that 'Joshua came at that time, and cut off the Anakims from the mountains, from Hebron... there was none of the Anakims left in the land of Israel.' Yet Joshua 15:13-14, in the account of the tribal land allotments, credits Caleb personally with driving out 'the three sons of Anak' from Hebron, and Judges 1:20 repeats this same attribution to Caleb.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization reads Joshua 11:21-22 as describing Joshua's broader, nation-level military campaign against the Anakite strongholds across the hill country generally (of which Hebron was one target among several named in the same verse, alongside Debir and Anab), commanding and leading Israel's combined forces, while Joshua 15:13-14 and Judges 1:20 describe a later, specific, local mopping-up action -- Caleb personally leading his own portion of Judah's forces to complete the removal of a particular remaining Anakite clan from the Hebron area he had specifically been promised (a promise recorded as far back as Numbers 13:22 and Deuteronomy 1:36), consistent with how Joshua 11:22 itself immediately qualifies its 'none left' claim by noting Anakim did remain in Philistine territory (Gaza, Gath, Ashdod). Israel's overall commander is credited with the campaign's success in the summary account, while the individual commander who completed a specific local action is separately credited in the more detailed, tribe-specific narrative -- a pattern of general/specific attribution addressed elsewhere in this project regarding other conquest narratives.

### 6. Did Balak actually wage war against Israel, or only attempt to have them cursed?

**References:** Joshua 24:9; Numbers 22:1-24:25

**The claim:** Joshua 24:9, in Joshua's farewell historical recital, states that 'Balak the son of Zippor, king of Moab, arose and warred against Israel.' The full narrative of this episode in Numbers 22-24, however, never records Balak's forces engaging Israel in actual combat; instead, Balak repeatedly sends messengers to hire the prophet Balaam to curse Israel, a spiritual and diplomatic strategy rather than a military engagement.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Hebrew military vocabulary, like its English counterpart, is regularly used to describe a hostile campaign or act of aggression against a people more broadly than literal, direct combat specifically -- Balak's entire, extended effort to secure a curse against Israel (itself explicitly understood throughout Numbers 22-24 as intended to weaken and defeat Israel by supernatural rather than conventional military means, e.g. Numbers 22:6, 'for I know that he whom thou blessest is blessed, and he whom thou cursest is cursed') is naturally described as Balak 'warring against' or making war on Israel in the broad sense of waging a hostile campaign against them, regardless of the specific tactic chosen. This entry's underlying episode -- Balaam's oracles and Balak's persistent hostility -- is treated at greater length in this project's Numbers companion document.

## Law and Ethics

### 7. Were Achan's children executed alongside him for his sin?

**References:** Joshua 7:24-25; Joshua 22:20; Deuteronomy 24:16

**The claim:** Joshua 7:24 states that Achan's sons and daughters (along with his livestock, tent, and possessions) were brought to the Valley of Achor together with him. Verse 25 then states 'all Israel stoned him with stones... and they burned them with fire, after they had stoned them with stones' -- read by many as meaning the children were executed alongside their father, which would appear to directly contradict Deuteronomy 24:16's principle that children shall not be put to death for a parent's sin.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, LSB:** Most translations preserve the Hebrew's shift between singular and plural pronouns across verse 25 similarly to the KJV, without resolving in the translation itself whether 'them' includes the children or refers only to property and animals; readers are encouraged to compare a formal-equivalence translation's exact pronoun pattern at this verse directly, since the case for one reading over the other rests substantially on this detail.
- **CSB:** CSB renders v.25 with explicit plural language throughout -- 'all Israel stoned them to death. They burned their bodies' -- more directly suggesting the family's inclusion than the KJV's singular-then-plural pattern (verified against the published text).
- **ESV:** ESV renders v.25 'And all Israel stoned him with stones. They burned them with fire and stoned them with stones,' preserving the KJV's singular-then-plural shift ('him' then 'them') and its underlying ambiguity (verified against the published text).
- **KJV:** KJV renders v.25 'And all Israel stoned him with stones, and burned them with fire, after they had stoned them with stones' -- preserving the Hebrew's shift from singular ('him') to plural ('them') exactly, which is the basis for the argument that only Achan was stoned (verified against the published text).
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders v.25 'So all Israel stoned him with stones; and they burned them with fire after they had stoned them with stones,' preserving the KJV's singular-then-plural shift and its underlying ambiguity (verified against the published text).
- **NASB:** NASB renders v.25 with plural pronouns throughout -- 'all Israel stoned them with stones; and they burned them with fire after they had stoned them with stones' -- rather than the KJV's singular-then-plural shift ('him'... 'them'), making the reading that the family was included in the execution appear more natural in NASB's English than in the KJV's (verified against the published text).
- **NIV:** NIV's rendering ('Then all Israel stoned him, and after they had stoned the rest, they burned them') makes the distinction between Achan and 'the rest' more explicit in English than most other translations (verified against a published rendering of this passage).
- **NLT:** NLT renders v.25 with the most explicit statement of all ten translations surveyed -- 'all the Israelites stoned Achan and his family and burned their bodies' -- naming the family directly rather than leaving the question to pronoun ambiguity (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** The base RSV renders v.25 'And all Israel stoned him with stones; they burned them with fire, and stoned them with stones,' preserving the KJV's singular-then-plural shift and its underlying ambiguity (verified against the published RSV text); RSV2CE's Old Testament preserves this wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated point among translators and commentators rather than a case with a single settled answer. Several considerations are offered on the side of the children not having been executed. First, the Hebrew pronouns in verse 25 shift from singular ('him,' referring to Achan specifically, in the stoning clause) to plural ('them,' most naturally read as his possessions and animals, in the burning clause). Several older Jewish and Christian commentators, including Rashi and others cited across the commentary tradition, take this shift as evidence that only Achan himself was executed, with his family brought only to witness the judgment as a deterrent, and his goods and animals destroyed by fire. Second, no explicit narrative statement anywhere in Joshua flatly states 'and they killed his sons and daughters.' On the other side, some commentators read the plural pronouns in verse 25 as including the family in the stoning as well as the burning, taking Joshua 22:20's later remark that Achan 'did not perish alone in his iniquity' as most naturally implying that others died alongside him, not merely that his possessions were also destroyed. If the children were in fact executed, the case would stand as a specific, narrated instance of collective or familial punishment -- one most conservative harmonists would treat similarly to other pre-Sinai-law-era or extraordinary covenant-violation episodes, comparable to how the entire households of Korah's fellow rebels perished together in Numbers 16, rather than as a claim that Deuteronomy 24:16's principle for ordinary judicial administration had been abandoned. If the children were not executed, as a first reading of Hebrew grammar in verse 25 allows, no tension with Deuteronomy 24:16 arises at all.

### 8. Rahab and the Gibeonites spared -- consistent exceptions, or a broken total-destruction command?

**References:** Joshua 2:1-21; Joshua 6:22-25; Joshua 9:1-27; Deuteronomy 20:16-18

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 20:16-18 commands that nothing that breathes be left alive among the Canaanite nations within the land. Yet Joshua spares Rahab and her family because she hid the Israelite spies and professed faith in Israel's God (Joshua 2, 6:22-25), and later, after being deceived by the Gibeonites into a peace treaty, honors that covenant and spares the Gibeonite cities rather than destroying them (Joshua 9), despite Israel's leaders recognizing they had been tricked.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each passage plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing the text's own stated grounds for each exception (Rahab's profession and action; the binding nature of a sworn oath), not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Both cases are treated within the text itself as falling outside, rather than violating, the total-destruction command: Rahab is spared explicitly because of her own action and profession of allegiance to Israel's God (Joshua 2:9-11; the New Testament later commends her specifically for this, Hebrews 11:31, James 2:25), removing her from the category of an unrepentant enemy combatant the command addresses, in a manner some compare to how surrendering or defecting individuals are treated differently from a resisting population elsewhere in ancient warfare law (compare Deuteronomy 20:10-11's provisions for cities that make peace). The Gibeonites' case is more directly a matter of an oath: once Israel's leaders had sworn a covenant of peace 'by the LORD, the God of Israel' (9:18-19), the text treats that oath as binding even though obtained by deception, on the principle -- affirmed elsewhere in the same book and the wider Old Testament -- that an oath sworn in God's name may not be broken merely because the other party to it deceived you (compare the seriousness with which Israel's later violation of this same Gibeonite covenant under Saul is treated centuries afterward, provoking a three-year famine, 2 Samuel 21:1-2). In neither case does the text treat the sparing as an unauthorized breach of the command; both are narrated as falling under recognized, principled exceptions.

### 9. Rahab's deception of the king's men -- commended for her faith, but did she sin by lying?

**References:** Joshua 2:1-7; Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25

**The claim:** Rahab actively lies to the king of Jericho's men about the spies' whereabouts (Joshua 2:4-5), hiding them and sending the pursuers on a false trail. The New Testament commends her by name for faith (Hebrews 11:31) and for her works (James 2:25) in connection with this very episode -- raising the question of whether Scripture is commending, or merely overlooking, a lie.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Joshua 2:4-5 and both New Testament commendations plainly and consistently as given; the question addressed here is a live matter of ethical and exegetical interpretation, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is a genuinely debated point among conservative interpreters rather than a single, settled resolution, and is treated here candidly as such. Some hold that Hebrews and James commend Rahab specifically for her faith and hospitable action (receiving and protecting the spies, and her stated confession of Israel's God, 2:9-11), without either New Testament passage commenting on, endorsing, or condemning the deception as a distinct, separate element of the episode -- on this view, Scripture simply does not address the ethics of the lie itself one way or the other. Others (a position with some precedent in classical Christian ethical writing, alongside a minority view) hold that in an extreme situation of protecting innocent life from unjust violence, deceiving an aggressor bent on murder is not classified by Scripture as the kind of falsehood the ninth commandment addresses (which concerns false witness against a neighbor in the community, not misleading a hostile aggressor), a position that also appeals to the similarly commended deception of the Hebrew midwives toward Pharaoh in Exodus 1:15-21. A third position holds that Rahab's lie was itself a real, if understandable and forgivable, moral failure -- an imperfect action within a larger act of genuine faith that Scripture chooses to commend for its central, praiseworthy element without thereby approving every detail of how it was carried out (a pattern some see paralleled in Scripture's frank, unvarnished honesty about the flawed conduct of other commended figures, such as Abraham's own deceptions regarding Sarah). Serious, Scripture-affirming interpreters hold all three positions.

## Land and Inheritance

### 10. Hebron given to Caleb -- yet also listed as a Levitical city and city of refuge

**References:** Joshua 14:13-14; Joshua 21:11-13

**The claim:** Joshua 14:13-14 records Hebron given to Caleb 'for an inheritance, because he wholly followed the LORD.' Joshua 21:11-13, in the later allotment of towns to the Levites, lists Hebron among the cities given to the priestly Levites (the sons of Aaron) as one of the cities of refuge.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Joshua 21:12's explicit clarification (fields to Caleb, town to the Levites) plainly and consistently as given; the resolution comes directly from the text's own wording, not from translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Joshua 21:12 itself supplies the resolution directly in the same passage that raises the apparent tension: 'but the fields of the city and its villages they gave to Caleb the son of Jephunneh as his possession.' The town of Hebron itself, with its residential and administrative structures, functioned as a Levitical city (housing priests and serving as a city of refuge, categories applied to towns generally throughout Israel regardless of which tribe's territorial allotment they fell within) while the surrounding agricultural fields and villages remained Caleb's personal inheritance -- a distinction between a town's function as a Levitical/refuge city and the underlying land's tribal ownership that the text draws explicitly, not an unnoticed inconsistency.

### 11. Were Zorah and Eshtaol allotted to Dan, or to Judah?

**References:** Joshua 15:20, 33; Joshua 19:40-41

**The claim:** Joshua 15:33 lists Zorah and Eshtaol among the towns in the lowland allotted to the tribe of Judah. Joshua 19:40-41, in the separate allotment given to the tribe of Dan, again lists Zorah and Eshtaol as part of Dan's territory.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of several towns in the border region between Judah's and Dan's allotments that the text itself lists under both tribes, reflecting the well-documented reality that Israel's tribal boundaries described overlapping zones of settlement and influence rather than the sharply exclusive, mutually impermeable lines of a modern political map -- border towns in a shared frontier zone were not infrequently claimed by, or associated with, more than one tribe simultaneously, particularly (as here) where one tribe's allotment (Dan's) proved too small or contested to fully settle on its own (a situation the book of Judges explicitly narrates for Dan, whose territory was under such pressure from the Amorites and Philistines that a large portion of the tribe eventually migrated north entirely, Judges 1:34-35; 18:1-31). Samson, later described as being 'of the family of the Danites' and active in exactly this Zorah/Eshtaol border region (Judges 13:2, 25; 16:31), confirms the area's genuine, ongoing association with Dan alongside its inclusion in Judah's broader territorial listing.

### 12. Was Aijalon a town of Dan, or of Ephraim?

**References:** Joshua 19:40-42; Joshua 21:23-24; 1 Chronicles 6:66, 69

**The claim:** Joshua 19:40-42 lists Aijalon among the towns allotted to the tribe of Dan. Joshua 21:23-24 then lists Aijalon among the cities given to the Kohathite Levites out of Dan's territory, consistent with this. Yet 1 Chronicles 6:66, 69, giving a parallel list of Levitical cities, instead lists Aijalon among the cities given out of the territory of Ephraim.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization notes that Dan's original allotment (Joshua 19:40-48) bordered directly on Ephraim's territory. Dan's tribe, as noted elsewhere in this document, struggled to fully settle its allotted territory and eventually relocated a large portion of itself far to the north (Judges 18). As a result, towns in the contested Dan/Ephraim border zone, including Aijalon, are elsewhere in Scripture associated with Ephraim as well -- compare 1 Chronicles 8:13, which places 'the sons of Elpaal,' from Benjamin, driving out the inhabitants of Gath and building Aijalon, suggesting the town's tribal association shifted or was disputed over time. 1 Chronicles' parallel Levitical-city list was compiled centuries after Joshua and reflects the territorial and administrative realities of its own later era, rather than a static snapshot of Joshua's original allotment. It most plausibly reflects Aijalon's tribal association after Dan's territorial contraction and partial relocation, rather than contradicting Joshua's record of the original, earlier allotment.

### 13. Who purchased the burial plot at Shechem where Joseph was buried -- Jacob, or Abraham?

**References:** Joshua 24:32; Genesis 33:18-19; Acts 7:15-16

**The claim:** Joshua 24:32 states plainly that the plot of ground at Shechem where Joseph's bones were buried was 'the parcel of ground which Jacob bought of the sons of Hamor.' Stephen's speech in Acts 7:15-16, however, attributes this same purchase to Abraham.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is addressed at length in this project's Genesis companion document (see its entry on the Abraham/Jacob/Shechem burial-site question, which treats Genesis 23, Genesis 33, Joshua 24:32, and Acts 7:15-16 together); in brief, Joshua's own record here is straightforward and consistent with Genesis 33:18-19's account of Jacob's purchase, and the difficulty lies entirely in Stephen's compressed speech in Acts, which most commentators read as telescoping two separate historical facts -- Abraham's purchase of the cave of Machpelah and Jacob's later, separate purchase at Shechem -- into a single summarized sentence, rather than reflecting any inconsistency within the Old Testament's own record of the Shechem transaction, which Joshua 24:32 states plainly and without ambiguity.

## Miracles and Literary Genre

### 14. The sun standing still over Gibeon -- a literal cosmic event, or a poetic citation from the Book of Jashar?

**References:** Joshua 10:12-14

**The claim:** Joshua 10:12-14 describes the sun and moon standing still at Joshua's command during the battle at Gibeon, 'about a whole day,' with the text explicitly noting this account is quoted 'in the Book of Jashar' (a now-lost source Joshua's author draws on elsewhere for poetic material, compare 2 Samuel 1:18). Some ask whether this is meant as a literal, unique astronomical event or as poetic/hyperbolic battle-victory language of a kind common in ancient Near Eastern military literature, and whether reading it literally creates tension with an ordinary understanding of physics and astronomy.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passage plainly and consistently as given, including its explicit citation of the Book of Jashar; the question addressed here is one of literary genre and interpretation, not a matter of translation choice or an inter-textual conflict.

**Reconciliation:** Conservative interpreters are themselves divided on this question, which distinguishes it from most other entries in this document set. Some hold the passage describes a genuine, miraculous, one-time suspension or extension of the earth's rotation -- or, in some proposals, a supernatural extension or refraction of daylight without altering the earth's actual rotation -- consistent with a plain reading of the narrative prose surrounding the poetic quotation. Others note that the quoted material is explicitly drawn from the poetic Book of Jashar, and that ancient Near Eastern victory accounts elsewhere use vivid, hyperbolic celestial imagery ('the sun stood still') as a literary convention for describing an unusually long, decisively favorable day of battle rather than literal astronomical language. On that reading, the passage is poetic hyperbole embedded within an otherwise historical prose narrative, similar to how Judges 5:20's poetic claim that 'the stars fought from heaven' against Sisera is not generally read as a literal astronomical claim. This entry does not resolve which reading is correct. It is included because the question in view is literal-versus-poetic genre, not a conflict between two competing biblical texts, and serious, Scripture-affirming interpreters land on both sides.

## Chronology and Composition

### 15. The reading of the Law at Mount Ebal -- commanded before entering the land, narrated only after several campaigns

**References:** Deuteronomy 27:1-8; Deuteronomy 11:29-30; Joshua 8:30-35

**The claim:** Deuteronomy 27 instructs that, on the very day Israel crosses the Jordan, an altar is to be built on Mount Ebal and the Law read aloud with the blessings and curses pronounced from Ebal and Gerizim. Joshua narrates this ceremony only in chapter 8, after the crossing of the Jordan, the fall of Jericho, and both the failed and successful campaigns against Ai -- apparently well after 'the very day' Deuteronomy's instruction specifies. Separately, this passage (Joshua 8:30-35) appears at a notably different point in the book's overall sequence in some ancient manuscript witnesses, including the Dead Sea Scrolls fragment 4QJoshua-a and, in a different but related way, the Old Greek (Septuagint) tradition, compared to its position in the Masoretic Hebrew text followed by all ten translations in this survey.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations follow the Masoretic Hebrew text's placement of Joshua 8:30-35 in the main text; none of the ten departs from the Masoretic order at this point. The underlying manuscript question is itself more contested among specialists than sometimes presented: while the Septuagint's own placement of this material is well documented as differing from the Masoretic order, the once-common claim that the Dead Sea Scrolls fragment 4QJoshua-a relocates the entire passage has been challenged by more recent scholarship (van der Meer, with support from Tov), which argues 4QJoshua-a instead inserts only a smaller exegetical expansion of verses 34-35 elsewhere in the narrative while retaining verses 30-35 themselves in the same position as the Masoretic Text. This project does not adjudicate this specialist textual-critical debate, noting only that it remains more genuinely unsettled than a simple two-tradition contrast would suggest.

**Reconciliation:** On the timing question, most commentators read Deuteronomy 27's instruction as specifying what must happen once Israel is in a position to carry it out, near the very outset of the land campaign, rather than requiring it literally within twenty-four hours of the Jordan crossing while still facing Jericho's fortified walls -- ancient administrative and ceremonial instructions of this kind are not always read as demanding same-day execution regardless of intervening military necessity, and Joshua 8:30-35's placement immediately after Ai's fall (the first secured, safe position within striking distance of Ebal and Gerizim) is generally read as the narratively and logistically earliest reasonable opportunity, not a late or delayed afterthought. On the separate manuscript-placement question, this is a genuine and independently interesting textual-critical fact: 4QJoshua-a places a form of this material earlier in the narrative (closer to the Jordan crossing), which most textual scholars treat as evidence of an alternate literary arrangement or edition of Joshua circulating in the Second Temple period, alongside the arrangement reflected in the Masoretic Hebrew text that all ten translations in this survey follow -- a documented case, similar in kind to several New Testament and Old Testament textual questions addressed elsewhere in this project, though considerably less discussed since no modern English translation adopts the alternate order in its main text.

### 16. Two covenant-renewal ceremonies -- Mount Ebal (Joshua 8) and Shechem (Joshua 24) -- the same event told twice, or two distinct occasions?

**References:** Joshua 8:30-35; Joshua 24:1-28

**The claim:** Joshua 8:30-35 records a covenant/law-reading ceremony at Mount Ebal, near Shechem, early in the conquest. Joshua 24 records another covenant-renewal ceremony explicitly held 'at Shechem' at the very end of Joshua's life, decades later, with its own separate gathering of the tribes, its own extended historical recital, and its own covenant document written and a memorial stone set up -- leading some to ask whether these are two accounts of a single event that have been duplicated in the text, or two genuinely separate occasions.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both ceremonies plainly and consistently as given, preserving their distinct settings, participants, and content; the resolution is a matter of narrative and historical interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The great majority of commentators, across a wide range of theological and critical approaches, read these as two distinct events separated by years or decades: chapter 8's ceremony is explicitly tied to the specific instructions of Deuteronomy 27 (building an altar, reading the blessings and curses, near the very start of the conquest), while chapter 24's ceremony is Joshua's own final, personal farewell covenant-renewal address, explicitly framed as occurring in his old age ('now I am about to go the way of all the earth,' 23:14) after the land had been substantially allotted to the tribes -- a distinct kind of ceremony (a covenant renewal led personally by the aging leader, addressing Israel's specific temptation toward the gods their ancestors had served 'beyond the River' and in Egypt, 24:14-15) rather than a repeated or duplicated telling of the earlier Ebal event. Shechem's recurring significance as a covenant site across both ceremonies (and earlier still, in the patriarchal narratives, Genesis 12:6-7; 33:18-20) reflects its established importance as a location, not evidence that a single event has been narrated twice.

## Law and Covenant Sign

### 17. Circumcision 'a second time' at Gilgal -- had the covenant sign of Genesis 17 truly lapsed for forty years?

**References:** Joshua 5:2-9; Genesis 17:9-14; Deuteronomy 10:16

**The claim:** Joshua 5:2 has the LORD tell Joshua to 'circumcise the sons of Israel a second time' at Gilgal, and the surrounding verses explain that the entire generation born during the forty years of wilderness wandering had not been circumcised. This raises a question about whether Israel had abandoned the sign of the Abrahamic covenant, described in Genesis 17:9-14 as a perpetual obligation for every male 'throughout your generations,' for an entire generation.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Joshua 5:2-9 plainly and consistently as given; the resolution is drawn directly from the historical explanation the passage itself provides, not from translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Joshua 5:4-7 itself supplies the historical explanation directly in the same passage: the uncircumcised generation consisted specifically of those born after the exodus from Egypt, during the years of wandering that followed Israel's rebellion at Kadesh-barnea (the unbelief following the spies' report, recounted in Numbers 13-14), and the text attributes the lapse to the practical difficulties and, implicitly, the divine judgment of that specific extended wilderness period rather than to an ordinary, casual neglect of the covenant sign under normal circumstances. 'A second time' (or, in some translations, 'again') most naturally refers to a second, nation-wide circumcision event (the first being the original observance under Abraham's household and its continuation through Isaac and Jacob's line down to those who left Egypt already circumcised) rather than any individual within this new generation being circumcised twice. The episode is presented as a specific, extraordinary feature of the wilderness generation's unique, judgment-marked history, resolved decisively and permanently upon entering the land, not as an ongoing or recurring practice.

## Numbers and Chronology

### 18. Caleb's stated age at 85 -- does the wilderness chronology actually hold together?

**References:** Joshua 14:6-10; Numbers 13:1-2; Numbers 14:33-34; Deuteronomy 2:14

**The claim:** In Joshua 14:6-10, Caleb states he was 40 years old when sent as one of the twelve spies (referencing Numbers 13), and that 45 years have passed since then (accounting for 38 additional years of wilderness wandering after the initial return from spying, plus roughly 7 years of the conquest itself), making him 85 at the time of speaking -- inviting a check of whether these figures are internally consistent with the separately stated 40-year wilderness period (Numbers 14:33-34) and 38-year span from Kadesh-barnea (Deuteronomy 2:14).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of the relevant ages and time markers identically; no translation differences are involved in this case.

**Reconciliation:** Working through the text's own explicit figures produces a self-consistent chronology without requiring any harmonization beyond careful reading: the spies' mission occurred in the second year after the Exodus (compare Numbers 10:11 and following, dating the departure from Sinai), Caleb was 40 at that time, the ensuing rebellion added 38 more years of wandering (Deuteronomy 2:14, measured from Kadesh-barnea to crossing the Zered, consistent with the 40-year total measured from the Exodus itself, Numbers 14:34, once the roughly two years already elapsed before the spy mission are accounted for), and a further approximately 5-7 years elapsed during the initial conquest campaigns narrated in Joshua before the land began to be allotted -- yielding Caleb's stated 85 (40 + 38 + roughly 7) without contradiction. This entry is included as a caution that not every apparent 'chronology problem' raised in popular lists withstands a careful check of the text's own explicitly stated figures, several of which resolve immediately once laid out in sequence, consistent with a similar caution offered for a Genesis chronology question in the general document set.

## Theological Tensions

### 19. Is death the final, common end for everyone, or is there life beyond it?

**References:** Joshua 23:14; 1 Corinthians 15:20-22; John 11:25-26

**The claim:** Joshua's farewell address states, 'Behold, this day I am going the way of all the earth,' an idiom describing death as the common destiny awaiting every person. The New Testament, by contrast, presents death as a defeated enemy and affirms resurrection and eternal life for believers (1 Corinthians 15:20-22; John 11:25-26).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** 'The way of all the earth' is a standard Old Testament idiom (used similarly by David at 1 Kings 2:2) acknowledging the universal, inescapable fact of physical death as the common experience of every mortal human being -- a straightforward statement about mortality, not a theological claim about what does or does not lie beyond death, a question the phrase itself is not addressing one way or the other. The New Testament's teaching on resurrection and eternal life does not dispute that physical death is the universal experience Joshua describes; rather, it addresses what follows that universal event for those united to Christ, affirming that physical death, while remaining the common 'way of all the earth' in this present age, has been decisively overcome and will ultimately be reversed (1 Corinthians 15:26's 'the last enemy that shall be destroyed is death') -- two statements about different stages of the same reality rather than competing claims about whether death itself currently occurs.
