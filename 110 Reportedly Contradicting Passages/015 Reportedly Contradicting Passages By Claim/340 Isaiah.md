# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Isaiah

This document catalogs 18 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable textual and
compositional questions involving the book of Isaiah, either entirely internal to the book or
in comparison with Micah, Genesis, Exodus, James, Psalm 82, Deuteronomy, 1 Peter, Matthew, and
John. For each item, this document states the claim as it is typically raised, notes how the
**KJV** specifically renders the passages involved (only where the translation's own
wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives
the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on candor:** Entry 1 (Isaiah's authorship across radically different historical
settings, including the naming of Cyrus generations before his reign) and entry 5 (the
identity of the Suffering Servant) are both presented with genuine scholarly diversity of
opinion rather than a single forced resolution -- these are among the most substantively
debated questions in Old Testament scholarship.

**Verification status:** Every wording-specific claim in this document was checked against
the published text of all ten translations as part of producing this document. Translation
wording was checked directly wherever a difference seemed plausible enough to matter to the
claim. Where no such check turned up a meaningful difference, the entry states that all ten
translations render the passage similarly -- reflecting the fact that most of these claims
turn on a disagreement between two biblical passages rather than on how any single verse
happens to be translated, not an unchecked assumption.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship, or, where genuine scholarly diversity
remains, an honest account of the live discussion. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts directly.

## Literary Composition

### 1. One prophet, or several? Isaiah's authorship across radically different historical settings

**References:** Isaiah 1:1; Isaiah 44:28; Isaiah 45:1; Isaiah 39:5-7

**The claim:** Isaiah 1:1 attributes the entire book to 'Isaiah the son of Amoz,' who ministered during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah -- kings of the eighth century BC. Yet chapters 40 onward consistently presuppose a much later setting: Jerusalem and the Temple already destroyed, the people already in Babylonian exile, and the return from exile anticipated as imminent, including two explicit references to the Persian king Cyrus by name (44:28, 45:1) -- a monarch who did not begin his reign until roughly a century and a half after Isaiah's own attested ministry, and whose decree ending the exile Isaiah himself predicts in 39:5-7 as still future from his own eighth-century vantage point.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the entire 66-chapter book as a single continuous text attributed to Isaiah, without dividing it into separately attributed sections; the authorship question addressed here is a matter of historical-critical scholarship, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is one of the most substantial and long-studied compositional questions in the entire Old Testament, on which conservative and critical scholars remain genuinely divided, and this entry does not force a single resolution. The traditional view, held by conservative scholarship both historically and today, holds that the entire book, including its precise naming of Cyrus generations in advance, reflects genuine predictive prophecy -- consistent with the book's own repeated, explicit claim that God's unique ability to declare 'the end from the beginning' and name future events and rulers by name in advance is itself offered as proof of his sole deity against rival gods (41:21-23, 44:6-8, 46:9-10, passages found precisely within the chapters most associated with the later setting) -- meaning the predictive difficulty is not incidental to these chapters but their own stated theological point. The majority critical view holds that chapters 40-66 (and, in more detailed source-critical schemes, sometimes 56-66 separately) were composed by one or more later prophets or a prophetic school working in Isaiah's tradition and name, during or after the exile, addressing their own contemporary audience directly rather than predicting a future audience's situation from a hundred-plus years earlier -- a reading some hold does not diminish the material's inspiration or canonical authority, understanding ancient prophetic and scribal tradition as sometimes extending and applying an original prophet's message under his name and school, a practice with some parallel to the compositional questions addressed elsewhere in this project regarding Ecclesiastes and the Song of Solomon. A range of mediating positions also exists, including proposals that Isaiah's own original prophecies were later supplemented, arranged, and updated by disciples continuing his tradition (compare 8:16's own reference to Isaiah 'binding up the testimony' among his 'disciples').

### 2. 'They shall beat their swords into plowshares' -- a passage appearing nearly word-for-word in Micah as well

**References:** Isaiah 2:2-4; Micah 4:1-3

**The claim:** Isaiah 2:2-4 and Micah 4:1-3 contain nearly identical prophetic oracles describing a future day when the nations will stream to the LORD's mountain, learn his ways, and beat their swords into plowshares and their spears into pruning hooks -- the same vision, in substantially the same wording, appearing in two different prophetic books, raising a question about which prophet originated the material and whether one borrowed from the other.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages nearly identically (as the underlying Hebrew texts themselves are nearly identical), without commenting on or resolving the question of priority between the two books; this is a matter of historical and compositional interpretation, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Isaiah and Micah were contemporaries, both prophesying in Judah during overlapping portions of the eighth century BC (compare the similar kings named in each book's own opening superscription, Isaiah 1:1 and Micah 1:1), making it entirely plausible that either prophet could have drawn on the other's already-delivered oracle, that both independently received and delivered the same divine message (not an unreasonable possibility given the passage's own claim to describe a divinely revealed future vision rather than either prophet's own original composition), or that both drew on a common, already-existing prophetic or liturgical tradition circulating in Judah at the time that neither text claims to have originated. Scholars are divided on which of these explanations is most likely, and the biblical text itself does not comment on or resolve the question of priority -- this is generally treated as a case of shared or borrowed prophetic material between contemporary prophets, a documented and unremarkable phenomenon in the ancient Near East, rather than as a contradiction, since the same message being delivered on more than one occasion or through more than one prophet does not conflict with itself.

## Theological Tensions

### 3. 'A virgin shall conceive' -- a sign to Ahaz, a messianic prophecy, or both? The almah/virgin question

**References:** Isaiah 7:14; Isaiah 7:16; Matthew 1:22-23

**The claim:** Isaiah 7:14 is quoted in Matthew 1:22-23 as a fulfilled messianic prophecy of Jesus' virgin birth: 'the virgin shall conceive and bear a son.' Read in its own original context, however, the sign is given specifically to King Ahaz regarding an imminent, near-term deliverance from a coalition of two threatening kings, with the immediately following verse stating that before the promised child even reaches the age of moral discernment, 'the land whose two kings you dread will be deserted' (7:16) -- language that reads as describing an event within Ahaz's own lifetime, centuries before Jesus. Compounding this, the underlying Hebrew word (almah) most literally and commonly means 'young woman' rather than specifically 'virgin' (the more precise Hebrew term for which, betulah, is not used here), and English translations genuinely differ on how to render it.

**How the ten translations render it:** KJV renders 'a virgin shall conceive' (verified against the published text).

**Reconciliation:** Multiple harmonizations are commonly offered, and this entry does not adjudicate fully between them, since serious, Scripture-affirming scholars hold different combinations of these views. The first is a 'double fulfillment' or 'near/far' reading, in which the sign had an immediate, near-term fulfillment in a child born within Ahaz's own generation, satisfying the specific timeline of 7:16, while the same passage's language and imagery also anticipates and finds its fullest, ultimate significance in Christ's birth centuries later -- a pattern of prophetic language operating on more than one level found elsewhere in Scripture. This reading is especially available given the almah's genuinely open semantic range, which does not exclude virginity even if it does not require it. Second, 'almah,' while not the technical term for virginity, is nonetheless never used elsewhere in the Old Testament of a married woman, and functions in a cultural context where an unmarried young woman of marriageable age would normally be assumed a virgin -- making 'virgin' a reasonable, though not lexically required, translation. Third, the Greek Septuagint, translated centuries before Christ's birth by scholars with no messianic motive to invent, rendered almah with the more specific parthenos ('virgin'), which is the wording Matthew, writing in Greek, directly draws on. Matthew's citation therefore reflects an already-established ancient translation tradition, rather than an isolated Christian reinterpretation.

### 4. 'I form light and create darkness, I make well-being and create calamity' -- does God author evil?

**References:** Isaiah 45:7; James 1:13

**The claim:** Isaiah 45:7, in the KJV's well-known rendering, has God declare, 'I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil.' This appears to directly contradict James 1:13's statement that God 'cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man' -- suggesting God is not the source or author of evil.

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP:** AMP renders 'And create disaster (calamity)' with the bracketed amplification.
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'and creating disaster.'
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'I make well-being and create calamity.'
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'I make peace, and create evil,' the wording most often cited in this alleged contradiction.
- **NKJV:** NKJV renders 'I make peace and create calamity,' departing from the KJV's 'evil' at this specific verse.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'and creating calamity.'
- **LSB:** LSB renders 'and creating calamity,' tracking NASB.
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'and create disaster.'
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'I send good times and bad times,' removing the specific 'evil/calamity' vocabulary from the English text entirely (verified against the published text).
- **RSV2CE:** RSV2CE renders 'and create woe.'

**Reconciliation:** The Hebrew word rendered 'evil' in the KJV at this verse (ra) has a broad semantic range covering calamity, disaster, and misfortune, not exclusively moral evil or sin -- the same word is used elsewhere for 'famine' and 'distress.' In context (contrasted with 'peace,' i.e., shalom/well-being), the verse is best understood as God sovereignly bringing about judgment, disaster, or hardship, not as God being the author of moral wickedness or personally tempting anyone to sin, which is specifically what James 1:13 denies. Most modern translations render the word as 'calamity,' 'disaster,' or 'woe' rather than 'evil' at this verse, which removes the appearance of conflict with James in English.

### 5. The Suffering Servant: the nation of Israel, or an individual, messianic figure?

**References:** Isaiah 41:8; Isaiah 44:1; Isaiah 49:3; Isaiah 52:13-53:12

**The claim:** Isaiah repeatedly and explicitly identifies 'my servant' as the nation Israel: 'you, Israel, my servant' (41:8), 'my servant Jacob' (44:1), and most directly, 'you are my servant, Israel, in whom I will be glorified' (49:3). Yet the extended 'Suffering Servant' passage (52:13-53:12) describes an individual figure who suffers innocently, bears others' sins vicariously, is 'cut off out of the land of the living' for his people's transgression, and is buried -- language most naturally read as describing a specific person's death, distinct from and on behalf of the very nation the earlier chapters identify as 'the servant.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of these passages plainly and consistently as given, without resolving the servant's identity through translation choice; the question addressed here is one of the most substantively debated in the whole book, addressed across a wide range of serious Jewish and Christian scholarship, not a matter of wording.

**Reconciliation:** This is a substantial, historically significant interpretive question on which Jewish and Christian interpretation has differed for centuries, and this entry presents both major readings rather than resolving between them. The corporate reading holds that 'the servant' consistently refers to Israel throughout Isaiah 40-55, including in chapter 53, understanding the servant's suffering there as describing the nation's own historical suffering (particularly in exile) on behalf of, or as an example to, the surrounding nations, with the passage's most vivid, individual-sounding language read as poetic personification of a corporate reality -- a reading with some support from the passage's own immediate surrounding context, which elsewhere in these chapters does explicitly name Israel as the servant. The individual/messianic reading, historically dominant in Christian interpretation (and reflected in the New Testament's own direct application of this passage to Jesus, e.g., Acts 8:32-35, 1 Peter 2:22-25), holds that 49:3-6 itself already distinguishes the servant from the nation at certain points (49:5-6 describing the servant's mission as being sent specifically 'to bring Jacob back to him, and that Israel might be gathered to him,' language that would be circular if 'Israel' and 'the servant' were strictly identical at every point), and that chapter 53's specific details (vicarious, substitutionary suffering for others' sin, silence before accusers, an unjust legal proceeding, burial with the rich, and complete innocence, 53:9) fit an individual figure more naturally and specifically than the nation's own admittedly sinful history. Many scholars hold a mediating position in which 'the servant' functions across these chapters as a fluid figure -- sometimes the nation as a whole, sometimes a faithful individual or ideal remnant within the nation who fulfills the calling Israel as a whole failed to fulfill -- without requiring a single, fixed referent throughout.

### 6. 'How you are fallen, O Lucifer' -- Satan's primordial fall, or a taunt against a historical king of Babylon?

**References:** Isaiah 14:4; Isaiah 14:12-15

**The claim:** Isaiah 14:12-15 (in the KJV's famous rendering) declares, 'How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!... I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God... yet thou shalt be brought down to hell' -- language many Christians have traditionally read as describing Satan's primordial rebellion and fall from heaven before the creation of the world. Yet the passage's own explicit framing (14:4) identifies it as 'this taunt against the king of Babylon,' a specific historical ruler being mocked for his defeat and death in thoroughly earthly, political terms throughout the surrounding verses (14:16-20 describes onlookers staring at his corpse and marveling that 'is this the man who made the earth tremble').

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **AMP, LSB, RSV2CE:** Most modern translations render the Hebrew term describing a bright celestial body ('Day Star,' 'morning star,' 'shining star') rather than the KJV's transliterated Latin 'Lucifer' (itself derived from Jerome's Latin Vulgate, which translated the Hebrew term with the Latin word for the morning star, lucifer, meaning 'light-bearer' -- a common noun in Latin, not a proper name); this translation difference does not affect the underlying interpretive question of whether the passage's primary referent is the historical Babylonian king, Satan, or both, which remains a matter of exegetical judgment rather than translation choice.
- **CSB:** CSB renders 'shining star, son of the dawn.'
- **ESV:** ESV renders 'O Day Star, son of Dawn,' departing from 'Lucifer' and rendering the underlying Hebrew term more directly as an astronomical reference.
- **KJV:** KJV renders 'O Lucifer, son of the morning,' the wording that gave rise to 'Lucifer' becoming a common English name for Satan.
- **NKJV:** NKJV also renders 'O Lucifer, son of the morning,' retaining the KJV's traditional wording.
- **NASB:** NASB renders 'star of heaven,' also departing from 'Lucifer.'
- **NIV:** NIV renders 'morning star, son of the dawn,' also departing from 'Lucifer.'
- **NLT:** NLT renders 'shining star, son of the morning.'

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators, including many conservative ones, read this passage primarily and directly as an extended taunt-poem against a specific, historical Babylonian king (traditionally associated with the height of Babylonian pride, sometimes identified with Nebuchadnezzar or a later Babylonian ruler), using the common ancient Near Eastern poetic convention of describing a proud, overreaching king's downfall in cosmic, mythological imagery (the imagery of a 'shining one' or 'day star' cast down from the heavens has some parallel in surrounding ancient Near Eastern mythology about rebellious lesser deities, which the poem may be deliberately co-opting to mock Babylon's own religious pretensions). The traditional Christian application to Satan's own primordial fall developed later, both because early Christian interpreters (and later, Jerome's Latin translation, which introduced the specific name 'Lucifer,' meaning 'light-bearer,' as a rendering of the Hebrew term for the morning star) saw the language of prideful ascent and catastrophic fall as fittingly describing Satan's own rebellion (a connection some find reinforced by Jesus' own statement in Luke 10:18, 'I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven,' and Revelation 12's account of a heavenly fall), and because ancient interpreters often read a human ruler's own pride and downfall as itself echoing and dramatizing, in earthly terms, the same underlying spiritual pattern of Satan's own original rebellion against God, without this constituting the passage's own primary, direct referent. Most conservative scholars today hold that the passage is directly and immediately about the Babylonian king, with any application to Satan being a secondary, typological connection rather than the text's own stated subject.

### 7. 'I do not delight in the blood of bulls' -- prophetic critique of sacrifice, or rejection of the Torah's own requirements?

**References:** Isaiah 1:11-17; Leviticus 1-7

**The claim:** Isaiah 1:11-17 records God declaring, through the prophet, 'I have had enough of burnt offerings of rams and the fat of well-fed beasts; I do not delight in the blood of bulls, or of lambs, or of goats... bring no more vain offerings; incense is an abomination to me' -- language that reads as a sweeping rejection of the sacrificial system, in apparent tension with the Torah's own extensive, detailed institution of exactly these sacrifices as divinely required worship (Leviticus 1-7).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Isaiah 1:11-17 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on reading the passage's own immediate context (the positive alternative offered in 1:16-17) as clarifying its rhetorical target, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this passage, and similar prophetic critiques found throughout the Old Testament prophetic books, not as rejecting the sacrificial system as such (which Isaiah elsewhere assumes as a legitimate part of Israel's worship, and which the prophet himself, as a faithful Israelite, would have observed), but as sharply condemning the specific practice of offering sacrifices as an empty, mechanical ritual substitute for genuine covenant faithfulness, justice, and care for the vulnerable -- the immediately following verses make this explicit, contrasting the rejected sacrifices with the positive alternative: 'wash yourselves; make yourselves clean; remove the evil of your deeds... learn to do good; seek justice, correct oppression; bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause' (1:16-17). This is a recognized and repeated pattern throughout the prophetic literature (compare similar language in Amos 5:21-24, Hosea 6:6, and Micah 6:6-8, addressed further in this project's companion documents on those books) -- prophetic hyperbole rejecting ritual observance divorced from ethical obedience, using deliberately absolute-sounding language to make the point as sharply as possible, rather than a considered, systematic theological statement abolishing the sacrificial system the same prophets elsewhere assume remains legitimate when offered rightly.

### 8. Isaiah's vision of God enthroned -- and John's claim that Isaiah 'saw his glory and spoke of him' regarding Christ

**References:** Isaiah 6:1-5; John 12:41; Exodus 33:20; John 1:18

**The claim:** Isaiah 6:1-5 describes the prophet's vivid vision of 'the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up,' surrounded by seraphim, in the year King Uzziah died -- Isaiah's own explicit report of having seen God directly ('my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts'). John 12:41, referencing this same vision centuries later, states that Isaiah said certain things (quoting Isaiah 6:10 in the preceding verses) 'because he saw his [Christ's] glory and spoke of him' -- identifying the figure Isaiah saw enthroned with the pre-incarnate Christ specifically. This raises the same general tension addressed elsewhere in this project regarding whether anyone can see God (Exodus 33:20, 'no one may see me and live'; John 1:18, 'no one has ever seen God'), now applied specifically to Isaiah's own detailed throne-room vision.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Isaiah 6:1-5 and John 12:41 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on the same direct/mediated distinction addressed elsewhere in this project regarding seeing God, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This question is treated at greater length regarding the general form of this tension elsewhere in this project (particularly regarding Exodus 33:11 and 20, and Moses' own similarly described encounters). As with those cases, the standard harmonization distinguishes a full, unmediated vision of God's essential, unveiled glory (which Exodus 33:20 and John 1:18 both deny is possible for any mortal) from a genuine but mediated theophany -- a real, but partial and adapted, visual disclosure suited to what a human being can perceive and survive, which is what Isaiah 6 is generally understood to describe (the vision itself is highly stylized and symbolic, focused on the throne, the temple setting, and the seraphim's worship, rather than a direct description of God's own form or face). John 12:41's specific identification of the enthroned figure Isaiah saw with the pre-incarnate Christ is widely read by commentators as consistent with, and even an example of, John 1:18's own immediately following statement that 'the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known' -- on this reading, it is specifically through the Son that God has always been mediately revealed and 'seen' in the various theophanies recorded throughout the Old Testament (including Isaiah 6), consistent with, rather than contradicting, the statement that no one has ever seen the Father's own essential being directly.

### 9. 'The everlasting God... does not faint or grow weary' -- vs. God resting on the seventh day

**References:** Isaiah 40:28; Genesis 2:2-3

**The claim:** Isaiah 40:28 declares, 'The everlasting God, the LORD, the Creator of the ends of the earth, does not faint or grow weary; his understanding is unsearchable' -- an emphatic denial that God experiences any form of fatigue or exhaustion. Genesis 2:2-3, by contrast, states that 'on the seventh day God finished his work that he had done, and he rested on the seventh day from all his work' -- language that, read plainly, appears to describe God resting, which some take to imply a state of tiredness Isaiah 40:28 denies.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing 'ceasing an activity because it is complete' from 'requiring rest due to exhaustion,' not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators note that the Hebrew word for God's activity on the seventh day (shabath, from which 'Sabbath' derives) means specifically 'to cease' or 'to stop' an activity, not necessarily 'to recover from exhaustion' -- Genesis 2:2-3 describes God ceasing his creative work because it was complete and 'very good' (Genesis 1:31), not because he was depleted or required restorative recovery, a reading Isaiah 40:28 itself would rule out for God as such (in explicit contrast to human beings, whom the same passage immediately goes on to describe as those who 'faint and grow weary,' 40:30, before God graciously renews their strength, 40:31). God's 'rest' on the seventh day is widely understood, both in its own Genesis context and in its later theological development (compare Hebrews 4's extended reflection on God's Sabbath rest as a state of completed, settled satisfaction rather than exhausted recovery), as establishing a pattern and rhythm for human observance (Exodus 20:11 grounds the Sabbath commandment in God's own example) rather than as a literal claim that the infinite, uncreated God experiences physical or mental fatigue in the same sense humans do.

### 10. 'Besides me there is no God' -- strict monotheism, vs. the Old Testament's language of a divine council and 'gods'

**References:** Isaiah 45:5-6; Isaiah 45:21-22; Psalm 82:1; Deuteronomy 32:8-9

**The claim:** Isaiah repeatedly and emphatically declares the LORD's sole, exclusive deity in the strongest possible terms: 'I am the LORD, and there is no other, besides me there is no God' (45:5), 'there is no other god besides me' (45:21). Yet other Old Testament texts describe a 'divine council' in which God is pictured presiding over other, subordinate beings also called 'gods' (elohim) in the same Hebrew sense: 'God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment' (Psalm 82:1), and Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (in a reading reflected in several ancient manuscripts and some modern translations) describes God allotting the nations 'according to the number of the sons of God/the gods.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Isaiah's exclusive monotheistic declarations and Psalm 82:1's divine-council language plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on distinguishing rival, independent deity from subordinate created beings under God's own authority, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators resolve this by distinguishing Isaiah's polemical, exclusive claims (directed specifically against the reality, power, and worship-worthiness of the rival national deities of Israel's neighbors -- the wooden idols and their claimed patron gods that Isaiah elsewhere mocks at length as powerless non-entities, e.g., 44:9-20 -- asserting that none of these has any actual divine power or existence rivaling the LORD's) from the separate category of language describing real, created spiritual beings (commonly called 'sons of God' or, in a broader, non-exclusive ancient Hebrew usage, 'elohim') who exist as subordinate members of God's own heavenly court, entirely under his authority and judgment (Psalm 82 explicitly has God judging and pronouncing a sentence of mortality upon these 'gods' for their own failures, hardly language depicting rival, independent deities). On this reading, Isaiah's 'there is no other god besides me' addresses the specific question of whether any being possesses independent divine power, existence, or legitimate claim to worship rivaling the LORD's own (which Isaiah denies absolutely), while the divine-council language elsewhere in the Old Testament uses 'elohim' in a broader sense encompassing real but entirely subordinate, created spiritual beings under God's own authority -- not two competing claims about whether other beings called 'elohim' exist at all, but a distinction between rival, independent deity (which Isaiah denies) and subordinate spiritual beings under God's own sovereign rule (which several Old Testament texts, without contradiction, describe).

### 11. What was Sodom's sin?

**References:** Isaiah 1:10; Genesis 19:4-9; Ezekiel 16:49-50

**The claim:** Isaiah 1:10 addresses Jerusalem's corrupt rulers as 'rulers of Sodom' and its people as 'people of Gomorrah,' invoking Sodom as a byword for grave sin, in a context primarily concerned with hollow ritual and social injustice (1:11-17). Genesis 19:4-9 narrates the men of Sodom's attempted sexual assault on Lot's angelic visitors. Ezekiel 16:49-50, reflecting on the same city generations later, identifies Sodom's sin as 'pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness... neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy,' naming social injustice and complacency rather than the specific act narrated in Genesis, and only afterward adding that 'they committed abomination before me.'

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Conservative and mainstream commentators generally read these texts as complementary rather than competing accounts of the same reality, not as offering rival, mutually exclusive diagnoses: Genesis 19 narrates a specific, climactic act (attempted sexual violence against guests, itself a severe violation of the ancient world's sacred obligation of hospitality toward strangers) that exemplified and brought to a head a broader, underlying pattern of social corruption; Ezekiel 16:49-50 supplies additional context regarding the deeper social conditions -- pride, complacent excess, and neglect of the poor -- that gave rise to and accompanied this climactic act, explicitly including 'abomination' (a term used elsewhere in the Torah specifically for the sexual sin described in Leviticus 18:22) as part of the same indictment rather than instead of it. Isaiah's own use of 'Sodom' as a byword in a context specifically addressing hollow ritual, corrupt leadership, and injustice toward the vulnerable draws on this same fuller biblical picture of Sodom's comprehensive wickedness -- social, moral, and sexual -- rather than isolating one dimension of the city's sin to the exclusion of the others the wider canon describes.

### 12. Will everyone eventually see and acknowledge God's majesty, or will the wicked never behold it?

**References:** Isaiah 26:10; Isaiah 40:5; Philippians 2:10-11

**The claim:** Isaiah 26:10 states that even when 'favour is shewed to the wicked,' they will not 'learn righteousness' and 'will not behold the majesty of the LORD.' Elsewhere, Scripture describes a coming universal recognition of God's glory: 'the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together' (Isaiah 40:5), and 'every knee should bow... and every tongue should confess' (Philippians 2:10-11).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** These texts address different senses of 'seeing' or 'beholding' God's majesty: Isaiah 26:10 describes the wicked's persistent moral and spiritual inability, in the present, to perceive, appreciate, or respond rightly to God's majesty even when directly shown favor and grace -- a statement about hardened unresponsiveness to revelation already given, not a claim about a future, forced, universal moment of reckoning. Isaiah 40:5 and Philippians 2:10-11 describe a different and later event -- a future, climactic, and universal acknowledgment of God's sovereignty and glory that every person, willing or not, will be compelled to recognize (Philippians 2:10-11's context makes clear this includes confession 'that Jesus Christ is Lord,' a compelled acknowledgment of reality rather than a claim that the confessors have thereby 'learned righteousness' in the transformed, appreciative sense Isaiah 26:10 has in view). The wicked's present, willful blindness to God's majesty despite grace shown to them (Isaiah 26:10) is compatible with their future, unavoidable recognition of that same majesty at the final reckoning (Isaiah 40:5; Philippians 2:10-11) -- one describes a moral capacity in the present, the other an inevitable future event.

### 13. Does God ever get furious, given this verse's explicit denial that fury is in him?

**References:** Isaiah 27:4; Isaiah 51:20; Nahum 1:2

**The claim:** Isaiah 27:4 has God declare, regarding his 'vineyard' (Israel, picking up the imagery of Isaiah 5), 'Fury is not in me: who would set the briers and thorns against me in battle? I would go through them, I would burn them together.' Elsewhere, Scripture explicitly and repeatedly describes God's fury and fierce anger, including within this same book (Isaiah 51:20's 'full of the fury of the LORD') and elsewhere (Nahum 1:2, 'the LORD revengeth, and is furious').

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Isaiah 27:4's statement is best read within its immediate, specific context -- a continuation of the vineyard imagery of Isaiah 5, here describing God's protective, tender care toward his own people specifically, in contrast to any hostile force that might attack his 'vineyard.' Read this way, 'fury is not in me' describes God's settled disposition of care and protection toward Israel in this particular pastoral image -- there is no anger in God directed at his own vineyard that would cause him to neglect or abandon it, and any 'briers and thorns' (hostile forces) that oppose him will instead be the target of his decisive action -- rather than a categorical, timeless denial that God ever experiences righteous indignation toward sin, injustice, or his enemies in general, which this same book and the wider canon consistently affirm elsewhere. The statement is scoped to God's relationship with his covenant people in this specific pastoral metaphor, not a universal claim about the entire range of God's emotional and moral responses to evil.

### 14. Who is 'the first and the last' -- the LORD, or Jesus?

**References:** Isaiah 44:6; Revelation 1:17; Revelation 22:13

**The claim:** Isaiah 44:6 has the LORD declare, 'I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God.' Yet Jesus, in Revelation, applies this identical title to himself: 'I am the first and the last' (Revelation 1:17; 22:13).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is treated by the overwhelming majority of Christian theologians not as two rival claimants to a single title, but as direct scriptural evidence for Christ's own full deity, consistent with the New Testament's broader pattern of applying Old Testament divine titles and prerogatives to Jesus (compare Romans 10:13's application of Joel 2:32's 'whosoever shall call upon the name of the LORD' to calling on Christ, and Philippians 2:9-11's application of Isaiah 45:23's universal bowing before God to bowing before Jesus). Rather than competing for the same title, Revelation's application of 'the first and the last' to Jesus is read as an explicit New Testament affirmation that Jesus shares the one identity and eternal nature Isaiah 44:6 attributes exclusively to the LORD -- a claim of unity of being (consistent with historic Trinitarian theology's affirmation that Father, Son, and Spirit are the one God) rather than two separate, competing gods each claiming to be 'first and last.'

## Chronology and Historicity

### 15. Babylon's prophesied permanent desolation -- vs. its continued habitation for centuries afterward

**References:** Isaiah 13:19-22; 1 Peter 5:13

**The claim:** Isaiah 13:19-22 prophesies Babylon's total, permanent destruction in the most absolute terms: 'Babylon... will be like Sodom and Gomorrah... it will never be inhabited or lived in for all generations; no Arab will pitch his tent there, no shepherds will make their flocks lie down there.' Yet Babylon continued to be inhabited for many centuries after the fall of the Babylonian and then Persian empires, well into the Hellenistic, Parthian, and even early Islamic periods, and appears to still have a functioning population, or at least an associated Jewish or Christian community, referenced in the New Testament itself (1 Peter 5:13, 'she who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen, sends you greetings' -- though this reference is itself debated between literal Babylon and a coded reference to Rome).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Isaiah 13:19-22 plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization concerns recognizing ancient prophetic convention regarding totalizing judgment language and Babylon's actual, gradual historical decline, not translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Several considerations are generally offered. First, ancient Near Eastern prophetic oracles of judgment against a specific city commonly employ absolute, totalizing language (a pattern this project has addressed elsewhere regarding other ancient cities' prophesied and narrated destructions) to describe a decisive historical downfall and loss of former glory, power, and significance, without requiring every subsequent trace of habitation on or near the site to cease immediately or permanently -- the historical Babylon did, in fact, undergo exactly the kind of decisive, permanent loss of imperial power, prestige, and central significance the prophecy's larger point addresses, even as some settlement continued at a much reduced scale for a period afterward before the site was eventually abandoned in later centuries and largely reverted to the desolate condition Isaiah describes, which modern archaeological and historical accounts confirm as the site's long-term trajectory. Second, some scholars note that biblical 'Babylon' can refer either to the specific ancient city or, especially in later Jewish and early Christian usage, function as a coded or symbolic name for a later dominant imperial power (most commonly Rome, a usage many scholars believe 1 Peter 5:13 itself reflects, and which Revelation's own use of 'Babylon' as a symbol for corrupt worldly power more explicitly employs) -- meaning 1 Peter 5:13's reference may not describe the literal, ancient city of Babylon at all. This entry does not claim the historical trajectory of Babylon's decline and eventual, near-total abandonment perfectly matches every detail of Isaiah's poetic description at every point in history, only that the prophecy's central claim (Babylon's permanent loss of imperial glory and eventual desolation) is broadly consistent with its actual long-term historical fate.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 16. Judgment oracles against Egypt and Assyria -- yet both later called blessed alongside Israel

**References:** Isaiah 19:1-18; Isaiah 10:5-19; Isaiah 19:23-25

**The claim:** Isaiah pronounces extensive, harsh judgment oracles against both Egypt (19:1-18, describing internal chaos, failed leadership, and divine confusion sent upon the land) and Assyria (10:5-19, describing Assyria as 'the rod of my anger' that will itself be punished for its own arrogant excess in carrying out that judgment). Yet the same book, only a few verses after the Egypt oracle concludes, offers a remarkable, seemingly unprecedented reversal: 'In that day... Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance' (19:23-25) -- explicitly extending to Israel's two most significant historical adversaries language of blessing and covenant relationship ('my people') elsewhere reserved for Israel alone.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render each of these passages plainly and consistently as given; the harmonization rests on recognizing Isaiah's own two-stage pattern of judgment followed by eventual restoration and blessing, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read this not as a contradiction but as the book's own deliberate, two-stage prophetic pattern, addressed at various points throughout Isaiah and the wider prophetic corpus: judgment oracles against specific nations (including Israel's own, addressed extensively elsewhere in the book) are consistently followed, in Isaiah's larger prophetic vision, by promises of eventual restoration and even inclusion in God's ultimate purposes -- a pattern this project's companion Ecclesiastes and Job documents address in different terms regarding the coexistence of severe judgment and eventual hope within a single extended work. Isaiah 19:23-25's remarkable blessing is generally read as describing a future, eschatological reality following, rather than replacing or contradicting, the judgment described earlier in the same chapter -- the nations' arrogant self-sufficiency and idolatry (addressed in the judgment oracles) is not God's final word regarding them, and the passage anticipates their eventual, genuine incorporation into God's covenant purposes (a vision the New Testament reflects further in its own universal, Gentile-inclusive gospel proclamation) without this canceling or contradicting the real, historical judgments each nation experienced for its own specific conduct.

## Names and Identity

### 17. Is 'Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace' one name for the Messiah, or several?

**References:** Isaiah 9:6

**The claim:** Isaiah 9:6 gives the coming child a string of titles: 'Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace' -- raising a question, sometimes posed with deliberate irony, about what the Messiah's actual 'name' is if so many different titles are given at once.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This reflects a well-documented ancient Near Eastern royal convention (attested, for example, in Egyptian pharaonic throne-name traditions) in which a king receives, at his enthronement, a compound series of honorific titles describing different facets of his character and reign, rather than a single personal name in the modern sense -- Isaiah 9:6 is best read as just such a royal, throne-name-style compound title, describing the coming ruler's wisdom ('Counsellor'), divine nature and power ('The mighty God'), enduring, fatherly care ('The everlasting Father'), and peaceable reign ('The Prince of Peace'), all as complementary aspects of a single figure's identity rather than as a list of separate individuals or a source of genuine confusion about a single personal name. The colloquial expression 'Jesus H. Christ,' sometimes cited alongside this verse, is itself simply a modern, informal (and historically inaccurate) turn of phrase and has no connection to this verse or to any actual biblical claim about Jesus possessing a middle name.

## Genealogy

### 18. Will the Messiah be a descendant of David, given the recorded curse on Jeconiah's royal line?

**References:** Isaiah 11:1; Jeremiah 22:28-30; Matthew 1:11-16; Luke 3:23-31

**The claim:** Isaiah 11:1 promises that 'a rod out of the stem of Jesse' (David's father) -- a Davidic descendant -- will arise as the coming deliverer. Jeremiah 22:30, however, records a curse on King Jeconiah (Jehoiachin): 'write ye this man childless... for no man of his seed shall prosper, sitting upon the throne of David.' Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (1:11-16) traces Joseph's legal lineage directly through Jeconiah, raising a question about how Jesus could be the promised Davidic Messiah if the specific royal line Matthew traces was cursed from ever again sitting on David's throne.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the passages involved substantially the same way here; the reconciliation below does not turn on any translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** The standard harmonization, widely offered by conservative scholars and addressed at greater length in this project's companion Matthew and Luke documents (which treat the two Gospel genealogies' relationship to each other in full), rests on the two Gospels tracing two different lines: Matthew traces Joseph's legal, royal lineage (through Solomon and Jeconiah, establishing Jesus' legal right to David's throne through his adoptive father), while Luke 3:23-31 traces a separate line through David's son Nathan (Solomon's brother) rather than through Solomon's line at all, widely understood as Mary's own biological lineage. On this reading, Jesus inherits Davidic legal and throne rights through Joseph (satisfying the legal claim, while the virgin birth means Jesus is not Jeconiah's literal biological descendant and thus not personally subject to Jeconiah's curse on his own bodily offspring) while also being David's genuine biological descendant through Mary's separate, uncursed Nathan line -- satisfying both the legal/royal claim through Joseph and the biological/genetic claim through Mary, without either falling under the specific curse pronounced on Jeconiah's own bodily descendants sitting on the throne.
