# Reportedly Contradicting Passages in Nahum

This document catalogs 5 commonly cited alleged contradictions and notable historical
and theological questions involving the book of Nahum, either entirely internal to the book or
in comparison with Jonah and Exodus 34. For each item, this document states the claim as it is
typically raised, notes how the ten tracked translations render the passages involved (only where
translation wording materially affects how sharp or resolvable the claim appears in English), and gives the standard scholarly reconciliation or harmonization.

**A note on scope:** Nahum is a short (3-chapter) book addressing a single subject -- Nineveh's
coming destruction. This document has 5 entries, reflecting that genuinely focused scope.

**A note on this book's central relationship:** Entries 1, 2, and 3 all address different
facets of the same underlying question: how Nahum's unrelieved judgment oracle against
Nineveh relates to Jonah's account of the same city's earlier repentance and reprieve,
roughly a century before.

**Verification status:** Entry 4 (Nahum's dating, constrained by the fall of Thebes in 663 BC
and Nineveh's own fall in 612 BC) was checked against an unusually large number of sources for
this document, confirming both the majority dating range and a genuine, documented minority
position resting on a real Septuagint/Vulgate textual variant. The remaining entries are
theological and literary questions where all ten translations render the same underlying text
and the harmonization is exegetical rather than a matter of wording.

**A note on posture:** Inclusion of an item here is not a concession that Scripture actually
contradicts itself. Every entry below is paired with the harmonization most commonly offered
by conservative and mainstream biblical scholarship. Readers are encouraged to consult the
primary texts and the cross-references directly.

## Internal Narrative Tensions

### 1. Nineveh spared through repentance in Jonah -- yet condemned to total destruction in Nahum

**References:** Nahum 1:1-3:19; Jonah 3:1-10

**The claim:** The book of Jonah records the entire city of Nineveh repenting at the prophet's brief warning, resulting in God relenting from the judgment he had threatened (Jonah 3:5-10). Nahum, addressed entirely to the same city, announces its total, final, unmitigated destruction, with no invitation to repent anywhere in the book's three chapters -- raising a question about whether these two books present a genuine contradiction (the same city spared in one book, destroyed in another) or a coherent historical sequence.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render both books plainly and consistently as given, without cross-referencing or explicitly connecting the two accounts in either book's own text; the harmonization rests on recognizing a coherent historical sequence separated by roughly a century, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction but a coherent sequence separated by roughly a century, and is widely read as one of the clearest illustrations in Scripture of a repentance-driven reprieve proving temporary rather than permanent. Jonah's mission to Nineveh is generally dated to around the mid-eighth century BC (roughly 760 BC, during the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel, consistent with the historical Jonah named in 2 Kings 14:25, addressed in the companion Jonah document); Nahum's prophecy, datable on internal grounds (addressed in entry 4 below) to sometime between 663 and 612 BC, addresses the same city roughly a century or more later, after the historical record shows Assyria had returned fully to the same pattern of brutal imperial conquest that had provoked God's original warning in Jonah's day -- including, within this same general period, the Assyrian destruction of the northern kingdom of Israel itself (722 BC) and repeated invasions of Judah. On this reading, Nineveh's repentance under Jonah's preaching was genuine and effective at averting judgment at that specific historical moment, but proved to be a temporary, one-generation reform rather than a permanent, ongoing transformation of Assyrian national character and conduct -- Nahum's total absence of any renewed invitation to repent (addressed further in entry 3 below) is consistent with a nation that has already received, and exhausted, its opportunity for repentance a century earlier.

## Theological Tensions

### 2. The same classic formula of God's character -- quoted by Jonah as grounds for mercy, and by Nahum as grounds for total judgment

**References:** Nahum 1:2-3; Jonah 4:2; Exodus 34:6-7

**The claim:** Nahum opens with a description of God's character closely echoing the classic self-revelation formula of Exodus 34:6-7: 'The LORD is a jealous and avenging God... slow to anger and great in power, and the LORD will by no means clear the guilty' (1:2-3). This is the very same formula Jonah cites, almost word for word, as his own personal reason for having fled his commission to Nineveh in the first place: 'I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love... therefore I made haste to flee' (Jonah 4:2) -- yet the two prophets draw opposite practical conclusions from citing the identical formula: Jonah cites it expecting (and resenting) God's mercy sparing the guilty city, while Nahum cites the same formula's less-quoted final clause ('will by no means clear the guilty') to ground the announcement of the same city's unmitigated, final destruction.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Nahum 1:2-3, Jonah 4:2, and Exodus 34:6-7 plainly and consistently as given, preserving the close verbal echo across all three passages; the harmonization rests on recognizing both mercy and justice as integrated, non-competing aspects of the single formula both prophets draw upon, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** This is not treated as a contradiction between the two prophets' theology, but as each prophet drawing on a different, but equally genuine, clause of the same complete, many-sided divine self-revelation first given in Exodus 34:6-7 -- a formula that has always held mercy and justice together as two integrated, non-competing aspects of a single divine character, rather than as two mutually exclusive options between which God simply alternates. Jonah's citation emphasizes the formula's mercy-oriented clauses ('gracious,' 'merciful,' 'abounding in steadfast love') because his own historical moment (Nineveh's genuine, first-time repentance) called for and received that specific aspect of God's revealed character; Nahum's citation emphasizes the formula's justice-oriented clause ('will by no means clear the guilty') because his own historical moment -- addressing a nation that had already received mercy once, in Jonah's own day, and had since returned fully to the same violent conduct that first provoked judgment -- called for the same complete divine character's other, equally genuine aspect. Both prophets accurately invoke the same one, undivided divine character; the different practical outcomes reflect different historical moments and different responses from Nineveh itself, not competing or contradictory theologies held by the two prophets.

## Literary Composition

### 3. A judgment oracle with no invitation to repent -- unlike most other prophetic books

**References:** Nahum 1:1-3:19

**The claim:** Almost every other prophetic book in the Old Testament, even amid its harshest judgment oracles, includes some explicit call to repentance or conditional language holding out the possibility of averting the announced judgment (compare Jeremiah 18:7-10, addressed at length in the companion Jeremiah document, as the Bible's clearest statement of this general conditional principle). Nahum is a notable exception: its entire three chapters address Nineveh's coming destruction without a single explicit invitation to repent or any stated condition under which the judgment might be averted.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render the entire book of Nahum plainly and consistently as given, without adding a call to repentance not present in the Hebrew text; this entry addresses a distinctive feature of the book's own content and its relationship to the wider conditional-prophecy principle addressed in the companion Jeremiah document, not a matter of translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators do not read this absence as inconsistent with the conditional-prophecy principle addressed elsewhere in this project, but as reflecting the specific historical circumstance addressed in entries 1 and 2 above: Nineveh had already been given, and had already received, its opportunity to repent a century earlier under Jonah's preaching (addressed in entry 1), and had since fully returned to, and in fact intensified, the same violent imperial conduct that had originally provoked judgment. On this reading, Nahum's total absence of a renewed call to repentance is not a departure from the Bible's normal pattern of conditional prophetic warning, but a deliberate reflection of a specific case in which that opportunity had already been extended and had already run its course -- consistent with, rather than contradicting, the general principle (stated most explicitly in Jeremiah 18:7-10) that prophetic judgment announcements are conditioned on the guilty party's response, since Nineveh's own specific historical response (repentance followed by relapse) had already determined the outcome by the time Nahum wrote.

## Chronology and Historicity

### 4. Nahum's dating: unusually well-constrained by internal evidence, in contrast to several other Minor Prophets

**References:** Nahum 3:8-11; Nahum 1:1

**The claim:** Unlike Joel and Obadiah (addressed in their own companion documents as among the most genuinely uncertain dating questions in the Old Testament), Nahum's own text supplies two firm internal chronological anchors: 3:8-10 describes the Assyrian destruction of the Egyptian city of Thebes (called 'No-amon' in the Hebrew) as an already-completed past event -- an event independently, precisely datable from Assyrian and Egyptian records to 663 BC -- while the entire book's central subject is Nineveh's own still-future destruction, which occurred in 612 BC. This places the book's composition somewhere within this roughly fifty-year window, a considerably narrower range than Joel's or Obadiah's genuine uncertainty.

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations follow the Masoretic Hebrew's past-tense description of Thebes's fall at Nahum 3:8-10, consistent with the majority scholarly dating of 663-612 BC (more narrowly, 663-654 BC); none of the ten translations reflects the minority, present-tense reading found in the ancient Septuagint and Vulgate traditions, which underlies the minority Jotham-era dating proposal.

**Reconciliation:** This is not a contradiction, but an example, addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding other specific historical details (Belshazzar's status in the companion Daniel document, the earthquake of Amos 1:1 in the companion Amos document), of internal biblical evidence intersecting productively with external historical and archaeological data to produce a genuinely well-constrained answer, in contrast to cases (like Joel's dating) where internal evidence remains too sparse to narrow the range meaningfully. Many scholars narrow the window further, to approximately 663-654 BC, based on the book's silence regarding Thebes's own subsequent reconstruction (which occurred around 654 BC) -- an argument from silence some scholars find persuasive and others regard as less conclusive, since Nahum's rhetorical purpose (using Thebes's fall as a warning example to Nineveh) would not necessarily require mentioning any later rebuilding regardless of when Nahum wrote. A minority ancient tradition, reflected in the historian Josephus and supported by the Greek Septuagint's and Latin Vulgate's own rendering of the Thebes reference in the present tense rather than the past tense (a genuine, documented textual variant at this specific point), instead places Nahum considerably earlier, during the reign of Jotham -- a minority position most modern scholars do not follow, given the strong majority reading of the Masoretic Hebrew's past-tense construction, but one this entry notes for completeness given its basis in a real textual difference between manuscript traditions rather than mere speculation.

## Ethical Tensions

### 5. An oracle of unrelieved satisfaction at a brutal empire's downfall -- and its ethical reception by later readers

**References:** Nahum 3:1-19

**The claim:** Nahum's closing chapter describes Nineveh's downfall in vivid, celebratory detail, ending with a rhetorical question addressed to the fallen city: 'there is no easing your hurt; your wound is grievous. All who hear the news of you clap their hands over you. For upon whom has not come your unceasing evil?' (3:19) -- a tone of unrelieved satisfaction at a national enemy's total destruction that some modern readers find in tension with a broader biblical ethic of loving one's enemies (addressed at several points elsewhere in this project regarding the imprecatory psalms, particularly Psalm 137).

**How the ten translations render it:** All ten translations render Nahum 3:1-19 plainly and consistently as given, without softening its tone; the harmonization rests on distinguishing satisfaction at documented, large-scale historical injustice being righted from a model for personal vindictiveness, not on translation choice.

**Reconciliation:** Most commentators read Nahum's tone in light of the same considerations addressed regarding the imprecatory psalms in the companion Psalms document: the book addresses, in the plainest terms, God's own righteous judgment against a specific historical empire whose own contemporary records (Assyrian royal inscriptions, which Nahum's original audience would likely have been well aware of through direct, often brutal experience) boast openly and repeatedly of extreme, deliberately terrorizing cruelty toward conquered peoples -- Nahum 3:1's own indictment ('bloody city, all full of lies and plunder') is not read as disproportionate rhetoric but as an accurate description independently corroborated by Assyria's own self-congratulatory monumental inscriptions boasting of exactly this conduct. The book's satisfaction is generally read as directed at the vindication of justice for genuine, documented, large-scale atrocity and the relief this brought to Assyria's many victims across the ancient Near East, rather than as personal vindictiveness modeling how an individual should feel toward a personal enemy -- a distinction this project has addressed in comparable terms regarding the imprecatory psalms' own entrusting of vengeance to God's own justice rather than modeling private retaliation.
