# Isaiah: Significant Textual Variants Across Ten Translations

**Translations covered:** AMP, CSB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, RSV2CE

---

## Isaiah 53:11 — does the Servant "see [and be satisfied]," or "see light [and be satisfied]"?

**The variant:** MT reads, awkwardly, "out of the anguish of his soul he shall see... and be satisfied" — the verb "see" has no stated object. The Septuagint's underlying Hebrew, and three separate Dead Sea Scrolls Isaiah manuscripts (1QIsaᵃ, 1QIsaᵇ, 4QIsaᵈ), all supply the missing object: "he shall see **light**."

**How the ten translations render it:**
- **Include "light":** NIV ("he will see the light of life," with a footnote citing the DSS and Septuagint), CSB (similarly, in the main text).
- **Follow MT without "light":** ESV, KJV, NASB (main text omits it; ESV and NASB footnote the DSS/Septuagint alternative), NKJV (keeps MT's shorter reading in the main text but footnotes the DSS/Septuagint alternative explicitly).
- I don't have solid, separately verified data for AMP, LSB, NLT, and RSV2CE on this specific verse.

**The difference in meaning:** With "light," the verse reads as a statement of vindication or renewed life after the Servant's suffering and death described in the surrounding verses — some read this as an implicit resurrection reference. Without it, the verse simply states the Servant "will see" (with the object left for the reader to infer from context) and be satisfied.

## Side note: a case where all ten translations agree, but the underlying Hebrew word is genuinely ambiguous rather than textually variant

- **Isaiah 7:14** — MT's word *almah* means a young woman of marriageable age, with virginity implied by context rather than stated outright (a distinct Hebrew word, *betulah*, means "virgin" specifically and isn't used here). The Septuagint's *parthenos* ("virgin") reflects one legitimate reading of the ambiguous term, not a competing manuscript reading. All ten of your translations render "virgin" — the real division here is between Christian translations (uniformly "virgin") and Jewish translations like the JPS Tanakh ("young woman"), not something splitting the ten tracked translations.
